# Abstract 13 

Neural activity in frontal-striatal circuits underlies reinforcement learning. Traditional theories suggest that 14 

reinforcement signals, which drive learning, strengthen connections within the basal ganglia. This 15 

strengthening is believed to shift information processing from cortical regions to subcortical regions as 16 

learning becomes established over time. To examine this hypothesis, we trained macaques to associate 17 

multiple sets of images with their values. Selecting different images led to either an increase (+2, +1) or a 18 

decrease ( −1, −2 ) in the number of tokens, which subsequently determined the amount of juice reward the 19 

macaques received. We simultaneously recorded neuronal activity from orbitofrontal cortex, ventral 20 

striatum, amygdala, and dorsomedial thalamic nucleus, analyzing the dynamic changes in these brain 21 

regions during both the initial learning and overlearned stages. The results indicated that as learning 22 

progressed from the initial stage to the overlearned stage, information processing shifted from the ventral 23 

striatum to the orbitofrontal cortex, corresponding to the abstraction from stimulus value to state value. This 24 

finding challenges traditional theories and provides a new perspective on the neural circuit mechanisms of 25 

learning. 26

# Introduction 27 

Reinforcement learning (RL), or the ability to adapt behavior in response to environmental feedback, is 28 

fundamental to cognitive flexibility. Extensive work has shown that RL is governed by frontostriatal 29 

circuits 1-4 . For the learning of action-outcome associations, prior research has focused on the transition 30 

from goal-directed actions that are sensitive to outcome degradation to automated stimulus-response habits 31 

that are insensitive to outcome degradation 5,6 . However, the neural dynamics of these processes during the 32 

transition from initial acquisition to overlearned associations remain less thoroughly explored. 33 

During early learning, values associated with choices are updated, possibly through plasticity in frontal-34 

striatal and amygdala-striatal circuits 7-11 . Although the ventral striatum (VS) has traditionally been regarded 35 

as the primary site for reinforcement signals and rapid learning 12,13 , recent evidence suggests that prefrontal 36 

signals, particularly from the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), may precede or regulate striatal activity to bias 37 

motivational learning 4,14 . During these early stages of learning, the OFC and amygdala (AMY) jointly 38 

evaluate contingent outcomes and guide initial choice selection 15 .39 

Studies have suggested that as learning progresses into the overlearned stage, the goal-directed system goes 40 

through functional reorganization. Traditional "cortical-to-subcortical" shift hypotheses have proposed that 41 

subcortical structures, such as the basal ganglia, take over control as tasks become well-learned 7,16-21 .42 

However, recent research shows that the prefrontal cortex does not relinquish control but instead develops 43 

more stable, high-dimensional, and resilient representations 22-28 . Consequently, long-term learning appears 44 

to transform prefrontal cortex representations, suggesting that expertise within a goal-directed framework 45 

is associated with more sophisticated cortical coding and consolidation 29-31 . Despite these advances, the 46 

precise evolution of information processing across frontostriatal nodes during this transition remains 47 

insufficiently understood. 48 

In this study, we investigated the functional reorganization of the frontostriatal circuit across different 49 

learning stages. Macaque monkeys were trained on a two-armed bandit learning task using a token-based 50 

symbolic reward system, with both novel and familiar blocks to assess learning and overlearning within a 51 

single session. Multi-region neuronal recordings were conducted simultaneously in the OFC, VS, AMY, 52 

and mediodorsal thalamus (MDt) to track the evolution of information processing. Contrary to the 53 

prevailing view that cortical involvement decreases as behavior becomes overlearned, our results reveal a 54 

shift in information processing from the VS to the OFC during the transition from early to late learning. 55 

Specifically, while the VS leads initial value updating, the OFC ultimately dominates stable state value 56 

coding and transmits stronger value signals to the VS during overlearning. Our findings provide a new 57

Two rhesus monkeys were trained on a two-armed bandit task. In this task, the monkeys collected tokens, 61 

which were periodically exchanged for juice rewards (Fig. 1a-b). In each block, we introduced 4 images 62 

corresponding to different token outcomes: +2, +1, −1, and −2. In each trial, 2 of the 4 images were 63 

presented on the screen. We defined a trial ’s condition as a combination of image values, resulting in a total 64 

of 𝐶𝐶 42 = 6 distinct conditions. The choice of an image led to the corresponding change of token with a 65 

probability of 75%, and no token change with a probability of 25% (Fig. 1b). Tokens were accumulated 66 

across trials and were cashed out every 4 to 6 trials, with one drop of apple juice for each token. 67 

There were 2 kinds of blocks, novel and familiar (Fig. 1c), based on the monkeys ’ experience with the 68 

images. In novel blocks, novel images were presented to the monkey, and it had to learn the values of the 69 

images by choosing them and observing the outcome. Each novel block comprised 108 trials, in which each 70 

condition appeared 18 times (18 * 𝐶𝐶 42 = 108). During task training, before neurophysiology recordings, 71 

some image sets were presented repeatedly every day for about 1 year. This allowed monkeys to overlearn 72 

the stimulus-outcome associations. During recording, these familiar image sets were presented in familiar 73 

blocks, and the monkey only needed to recall the values of each image to choose the better one. Each 74 

familiar block comprised 48 trials, in which each condition appeared 8 times (8 * 𝐶𝐶 42 = 48). Discrimination 75 

of novel and familiar blocks provides a basis for comparing the learning of new stimulus-outcome 76 

associations with that of overlearned stimulus-outcome associations. Because blocks of each condition are 77 

presented interleaved (Fig. 1c), we can compare activity within the same session in the two conditions. 78

We quantified performance as the fraction of times the monkeys chose the image associated with the higher 80 

value in each condition (e.g., choosing +1 when presented with -1 and +1 images), hereafter called correct 81 

rate (CR). To describe the learning process, we regard CR as a function of the number of observations per 82 

condition (Fig. 1d). 83

familiar blocks, CR was not affected by the number of observations ( F7, 7033 = 0.65, p = 0.714), but condition 87 

(F5, 7033 = 267.19, p < 0.001) and the number of tokens before choice ( F10, 7033 = 2.42, p = 0.007) had 88

# Neurophysiological recordings 101 

Neural activity was recorded simultaneously from 4 regions within the ventral cortico-striatal-thalamo-102 

cortical circuit using multi-site linear probes. We isolated 606 neurons from the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), 103 

829 from the ventral striatum (VS), 1,607 from the basolateral amygdala (AMY), and 1,035 from the medial 104 

portion of the mediodorsal thalamus (MDt). The semi-chronic recording procedure was described in detail 105 

in our previous work 33 . The novel block data were previously analyzed to examine the neural coding of the 106 

token reinforcers 33 . Here, with additional familiar block data, we aim to examine the plasticity of the neural 107 

circuit during learning. 108

assess the strength and latency of representations for the six choice conditions. With advancing learning, 139

# Representation of chosen value 153 

We next examined chosen value, the assigned value of the chosen image. To extract it from neural 154 

population activity, we performed targeted dimensionality reduction 33 . This analysis first defined axes of 155 

task-related variables in the space of neural populations using regression coefficients from a linear model, 156 

and then projected neural activity onto the axis corresponding to the variable of interest (Fig. 4a-c). Using 157 

this approach, we isolated chosen value, which was embedded in the mixed neural representation. 158 

We wanted to ask how strongly each region encoded chosen value. The stronger a region encodes chosen 159 

value, the more distinct its responses should be for different values. We classified the trials into 4 groups 160 

of chosen value +2, +1, −1, and −2, calculated their respective mean activity in each group (Fig. 4b), and 161 

defined the standard deviation across groups (which captures the spread of the average for each value) as 162 

the encoding strength (Fig. 4c). We found that the strongest encoding appeared in the Cue period (Fig. 4e) 163 

and the Outcome period (Fig. 4f). 164 

We found an apparent transfer of encoding time in VS, from the Outcome period to the Cue period (Fig. 165 

4c). Early in learning, VS represented changes in token outcomes. As learning progressed, the value signal 166 

shifted gradually from tokens to images as the monkeys became more familiar with images’ value later in 167 

the block. This represents the classic transfer of TD reward prediction errors from outcome to cue 34,35 ,168 

further supported by the overlearned data. In familiar blocks, the monkeys remembered the image values, 169 

so learning was no longer involved. As a result, value information was carried by images rather than tokens 170 

(Fig. 4d top row). The pattern was absent or not as strong in the other 3 regions (Fig. 4d, bottom row). In 171 

OFC, during the Outcome period, encoding strength rapidly declined from a moderate level during early 172 

learning. During the Cue period, encoding strength increased during novel blocks and was maintained and 173 

extended in time in familiar blocks. For AMY, encoding in the Cue period was stable but low in novel 174 

blocks and significantly rose in familiar blocks. MDt showed increasing encoding strength in novel blocks 175 

during both the Cue and Outcome periods. But in familiar blocks, the two periods diverged: the Outcome 176 

period showed greater strength, while encoding in the Cue period weakened. 177

observed in novel blocks, but OFC encoding was earlier than VS in familiar blocks (Wilcoxon signed-rank 179

# Unique encoding of state value in OFC 182 

Considering its significance above, we further investigated how the OFC encodes state value. State value 183 

is the expected future reward a monkey can obtain starting from a given state 36,37 . It reflects the subjective 184 

value of that state to the animal. For example, the Fixation period in the late learning stage has a higher 185 

value than in the early learning stage, because monkeys are more likely to receive rewards due to experience 186 

with the cues. To estimate state value, we constructed a POMDP model 38 based on the R-W model ’s value 187 

in each trial (Fig. 5a-b), but also including additional factors related to the number of accumulated tokens 188 

and time since the last cashout. It divided a trial into several epochs (ITI, Fixation, Cue, Outcome, Cashout) 189 

and computed their values (Fig. 5b). Specifically, our model accounted for learning effects on state value. 190 

We fit the firing rates of neurons with a multivariate linear model that included state value as a predictor, 191 

and used the percentage of variance explained by state value as a measure of encoding strength. We first 192 

examined the distribution of encoding strength within the neural population (Fig. 5c). Only a small 193 

proportion of neurons encoded state value strongly. We further investigated the encoding strength of each 194 

region (Fig. 5d-g). Fig. 5d shows the mean encoding of state value across a trial in the novel (Learning) 195 

and familiar (Overlearned) blocks. We expect that as learning progresses, the state value propagates 196 

backward from the Outcome to the Cue, Fixation, and the inter-trial interval (ITI), as monkeys become 197 

more certain about the outcomes they will receive and the choices they will make. 198 

As expected, state value encoding increased in all regions after learning, in the Outcome and also Cue 199 

periods (Extended Data Fig. 2f). For AMY and MDt, the encoding difference between the Outcome and 200 

Cue periods decreased after overlearning (Fig. 5e), suggesting an extra gain of state value in earlier periods 201 

compared to token outcome. However, OFC uniquely encoded state value with greater strength and earlier 202 

timing (Fig. 5d-g). In novel blocks, encoding in OFC was significantly stronger than in other regions from 203 

the Fixation period to the Outcome period (Fig. 5f). In familiar blocks, this advantage expanded to even 204 

earlier stages in the ITI. We also examined the encoding of the difference in state value across trial periods, 205 

and OFC was the strongest encoder as well (Extended Data Fig. 2, a-e). Specifically, OFC exceeded the 206 

other 3 regions in encoding the difference between Fixation and ITI. These results suggest that OFC 207 

uniquely encodes early state value, a key distinction from chosen value and token outcomes. 208

# Discussion 217 

We examined the single-unit, population, and network representation of value information across the 218 

ventral cortico-striatal-thalamo-cortical network. Our findings reveal a dynamic functional reorganization 219 

of this circuit as learning progresses from early to late to overlearned. Specifically, we observed that while 220 

the VS was crucial for rapid updating of stimulus value during early learning, the OFC showed stronger 221 

and earlier value representation, and increased state value representation during overlearning. This results 222 

in a significant shift of the information processing center from subcortical to cortical structures, providing 223 

a direct challenge to traditional "cortical-to-subcortical" shift hypotheses 17,39 .224 

# Distinguishing learning from overlearning 225 

Previous studies have examined learning across different time scales by collecting data over a long-term 226 

training history 28,40 . This addresses the learning process at a single-day level. However, it compares the 227 

coding of learning across different groups of neurons. To address the neural basis of different learning 228 

stages within a single session, we employed both novel and familiar blocks in the task, allowing us to probe 229 

the same neurons across distinct learning stages. 230 

Previous studies suggest a shift from goal-directed to automated behavior after sufficient learning 5,6,16 . Our 231 

behavioral results were consistent with this perspective, where monkeys’ choice preferences evolved 232 

rapidly in novel blocks (goal-directed learning) but remained stable in familiar blocks (automated behavior 233 

without learning). Different learning stages vary in their reliance on neural plasticity and the speed of signal 234 

updates. While prior work has separately implicated the OFC, VS, and AMY in value processing 15,33 , their 235 

specific roles within a broader network remain unclear, especially during the transition from early 236 

acquisition to overlearning 41 .237

# The ventral striatum encodes early value updates 238 

Although value updating has been attributed to the VS, previous evidence was controversial. fMRI studies 239 

in humans reported RPE-related BOLD signal in VS 12,42-44 , but single-unit recording suggests VS acts like 240 

a “critic” but not simply a RPE-encoder 10,13 . This may be due to the fact that VS is not simply encoding 241 

RPE, but an integration of multiple values 45 and temporal information 46 . To isolate the RPE-related signal 242 

from this mixed information, we applied targeted dimensionality reduction to the neural populations. We 243 

found that the VS is primarily involved in updating stimulus values during early learning. In early learning, 244 

the VS showed earlier and stronger encoding of stimuli and outcomes than the other regions. The major 245 

change in VS was that the encoding of values shifted from the reinforcement feedback to its corresponding 246 

stimulus. This is consistent with the classic view of the VS as a primary recipient of dopaminergic temporal-247 

difference reward prediction errors (RPE) 35 and its role in rapid associative learning 47-50 .248 

We also found that the expected value representation in VS is limited to the stimulus period. This implies 249 

that information abstraction in VS is related to sensory events, rather than explicitly holding information 250 

online. In early learning, as learned information is simple, rapid, and model-free, VS is dominant in the 251 

network. But once the value is assigned and the association is consolidated, the computation shifts to OFC. 252 

# OFC contributes to the encoding of state value 253 

As the monkeys transitioned from early to late learning and then to the overlearned stage, we observed that 254 

both stimulus and value processing shifted from VS to OFC, characterized by higher decoding accuracy 255 

and shorter information latency. This finding contrasts with the suggestion that the cortex should become 256 

less involved late in learning 31 . Previous findings suggest the role of OFC is to encode state information 257  

> 30,36

, and we found OFC was the main encoder of state value among the recorded regions. In late learning 258 

in novel blocks, we observed increased information in OFC during stimulus encoding, not during value 259 

encoding. In this period, OFC has learned the state determined by the presented images, but these states are 260 

not firmly associated with value. Only after extensive training does the OFC show the strongest and earliest 261 

representation of value, as indicated by condition decoding and the encoding latency of the chosen value. 262 

Our results on information flow also support this, showing that flow from OFC to VS is only enhanced in 263 

familiar blocks, not in late novel blocks. We suppose the meaning of this flow is to use state value in OFC 264 

to guide later action selection in the basal ganglia, and this requires state value in OFC to be reliable 14 ,265 

which is present during overlearning. 266 

In conclusion, the shift of information processing from VS to OFC corresponds to the advancement from 267 

stimulus value to state value 51 , which aligns closely with computational frameworks of frontostriatal 268

# The role of the ventral cortico-striatal-thalamo-cortical network in 273 

# learning 274 

Our findings reveal that the ventral cortico-striatal-thalamo-cortical network operates as a dynamic system 275 

that redistributes processing as expertise is gained. During early learning, the network operates in a "bottom-276 

up" manner, driven by subcortical dominance of the VS and AMY to rapidly encode reinforcement signals 277 

and allocate reinforcement 4,15 . Within this stage, value updating in the VS is limited to the immediate 278 

outcome. Here, the VS handles model-free value predictions 13,52 , shifting the encoding of value from 279 

feedback to the corresponding stimulus 35 . These rapid subcortical signals serve as guiding inputs, relayed 280 

via the MDt to slower-learning cortical systems 54 . Once stimulus value is consolidated, the computational 281 

burden shifts. This transition begins in late learning, where a cortical advantage emerges in the OFC for 282 

stimulus encoding. The OFC has mapped the task states, but they are not yet firmly associated with value 283 

> 30

.284 

Ultimately, after overlearning, the network hierarchy reverses, shifting the information processing center 285 

from subcortical to cortical structures 55 . The center of both stimulus and value processing shifts from the 286 

VS to the OFC. During overlearning, the OFC acquires state value encoding and emerges as the main node 287 

> 56

. Consequently, communication becomes “top-down”: enhanced information flow runs exclusively from 288 

the OFC to the VS in familiar blocks to guide subsequent action selection in the basal ganglia 14 . This shift 289 

from VS to OFC corresponds to an advancement from stimulus value to state value, lifting the abstraction 290 

level from simple model-free associative learning to structured, model-based reinforcement learning 3,36,57 .291

# Methods 453 

# Subjects 454 

The experiments were performed on two adult rhesus macaques ( Macaca mulatta ), a 10 kg male and a 7.5 455 

kg female, between 8 and 10 years old. The monkeys were housed in pairs whenever possible and fed on a 456 

24-hour schedule. On testing days, water intake was controlled, requiring the subjects to earn juice rewards 457 

through task performance. Conversely, on non-testing days, water was provided ad libitum .458 

# Ethics 459 

Experimental procedures for all monkeys followed the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals 460 

and were approved by the National Institute of Mental Health Animal Care and Use Committee. 461 

# Experimental Setup 462 

Monkeys were trained to perform a saccade-based two-armed bandit task. Stimuli were presented on a 19-463 

inch LCD monitor situated 40 cm from the monkeys ’ eyes. During training and testing, the monkeys sat in 464 

a primate chair with their heads restrained. Stimulus presentation and behavioral monitoring were 465 

controlled by MonkeyLogic 58 . The eye movements were monitored at 400 fps using a Viewpoint eye 466 

tracker (Arrington Research, Scottsdale, AZ) and sampled at 1 kHz. A fixed amount of apple juice was 467 

delivered via a pressurized plastic tube, gated by a solenoid valve, on rewarded trials. 468 

# Task Design 469 

The task was based on our previous study 32 . Each block used a set of 4 images corresponding to the values 470 

+2, +1, −1, and −2 . The monkeys obtained more tokens by choosing images associated with larger values. 471 

Choosing one of the images led to gaining or losing a corresponding number of tokens with a probability 472 

of 75%, and no token change for the other 25% (Fig. 1b). Also, token numbers could not be negative, so 473 

choosing a loss image when there were no accumulated tokens had no effect. 474 

To complete a trial successfully, the monkey first acquired and held central fixation for 400–600 ms. Then, 475 

the computer randomly selected 2 of the 4 images and displayed them on the screen. The animal made its 476 

selection by saccading to one of them. The unchosen image disappeared when the monkey reached the 477 

target. Saccade fixation was maintained on the chosen image for 500 ms. After that, the chosen image 478 

disappeared, and the token number was updated accordingly. Tokens accumulated across trials and were 479

# Surgical procedures 494 

To prepare for the study, a titanium headpost and a 25 × 35 mm recording chamber were surgically 495 

implanted into each monkey, enabling vertical grid access to the OFC, VS, AMY, and MDt 33 . 3.0 T T1-496

adhesive. Every 3–5 recording days, the guide tubes were replaced and shifted to adjacent locations, with 500 

their new positions verified via subsequent MRI scans. All surgical procedures were conducted under sterile 501 

conditions while the subjects were under anesthesia. 502

# Choice behavior 515 

Each block had six conditions, pseudo-randomly arranged in the blocks. The number of observations in 516 

each condition is regarded as progress of learning, which increases from 1 to 18 in novel blocks, and from 517 

1 to 8 in familiar blocks. We first computed the fraction of choosing the image associated with the higher 518 

value (correct rate, CR) in each condition to evaluate choice behavior. We fitted monkeys ’ behavior with 519 

the RW model (Fig. 1d). 520 

To compare performance across stages, particularly late novel and familiar blocks, we conducted a two-521 

sample proportion test. Trials were divided into 3 groups: Early was the first 48 trials in novel blocks, Late 522 

was the last 48 trials in novel blocks, and Overlearned was all trials from familiar blocks. The number 48 523 

was set to balance the number of trials per group, which was also used in the neural analyses. We conducted 524 

a pairwise comparison among the 3 trial groups using the chi-squared test for proportions. 525 

# Neural activity and proportion of responsive neurons 526 

To analyze neural activity changes resulting from learning, we extracted two time periods from each trial: 527 

Cue and Outcome, during which firing rates were highest. The Cue period is a 500-ms window at 700-200 528 

ms before the token outcome, beginning approximately at cue onset. The Outcome period was set to 0-500 529 

ms after the token outcome. The mean firing rate for each neuron was computed in each time window and 530 

each learning stage, and ANOVAs were performed to check the main effect of learning stage on firing rate 531 

in each recorded region. 532 

To identify neurons that respond to different task components at different times during trials, we fit a 533 

sliding-window multi-way ANOVA model to spike counts computed in 200 ms bins (Extended Data Fig. 534 

1). The bins were aligned to the time of the token outcome and advanced by 50 ms. Factors in ANOVA 535 

included the number of tokens on the screen (#token, may change after the choices), the change of token 536 

numbers ( ∆token), the drops of juice delivered at juice Outcome period ( #juice), image pairs with different 537 

value combinations presented (condition, e.g., +2 vs. −1), the order of blocks (blockID), the assigned value 538 

(cValue), and direction of the chosen image (cDir, left or right), and the identity of the chosen image 539

# Decoding analysis 545 

We decoded the trial condition (values of the image pair) from the firing rates of neurons in different regions. 546 

We balanced the number of trials used in different learning stages. For each session, 3 familiar blocks and 547 

3 novel blocks (randomly chosen from 9) were used. For each decoding round, we used continuous 48 trials 548 

from 3 blocks of the same type (e.g., the 1st through 48th trials in 3 novel blocks for Early learning). We 549 

generated 1,000 bootstrap samples, each consisting of 500 neurons randomly drawn from the dataset. Then, 550 

decoding was obtained with leave-one-out cross-validation. 551 

Decoding latency was defined as the earliest time point at which decoding accuracy was significantly higher 552 

than during the Fixation period. The Fixation period was 500 -0 ms before cue onset, and the accuracy 553 

baseline in a bootstrap sample was the mean decoding accuracy for this period. The criterion of significant 554 

was that decoding accuracy was higher than the baseline in over 99% bootstrap samples. We also conducted 555 

the same analysis when decoding the chosen direction. 556 

Because of the 48-trial window, we obtained 61 windows from novel blocks and 1 from familiar blocks. 557 

Decoding latency of 61 windows in novel blocks was fit by a sigmoid curve. We also checked changes in 558 

decoding accuracy across these time windows, using the mean accuracy during 200-500 ms after cue onset 559 

to represent the cue information before the token outcome. 560
