## Abstract 21 

Transitive inference (TI) is the ability to reason about transitive relationships in an 22 

ordered set of items (e.g., if A>B and B>C, then A>C). TI is widely held to depend on a linear 23 

representation of the serial (rank) order of those items. By what computational mechanism is 24 

such an ordering constructed during learning, and how is it used to make choices that obey 25 

transitivity? Here we take a minimalist approach, applying least-squares estimation (LSE) to a 26 

serial learning task commonly used to test TI in humans and animals. In this formulation, LSE 27 

computes a linear classifier that maps task conditions onto behavioral outcomes. This algorithm 28 

makes no explicit assumptions about transitivity or serial order, yet it reproduces key empirical 29 

features of TI; namely, the ability to generalize beyond the training set, and a symbolic distance 30 

effect (SDE) in performance accuracy. Applying the classifier to individual items produces an 31 

internally ordered representation of rank from which both generalization and the SDE naturally 32 

emerge. The approach also yields a decision mechanism, in the form of a differencing operation, 33 

for selecting the correct item from any pair. These findings reframe TI as a linear classification 34 

problem, challenging conventional assumptions about the cognitive mechanisms required for 35 

transitive reasoning. 36

## Introduction 38 

Computational models of cognitive tasks are often built around complex, neurally 39 

inspired algorithms that seek to capture the richness and dynamics of underlying cognitive 40 

processes. Such models are validated by their ability to reproduce behavioral and neural 41 

responses observed in living subjects. To understand what these models reveal about cognition, 42 

however, it is equally important to understand the computational complexity of the task itself. 43 

Can simple, ostensibly non-neural algorithms illuminate how cognitive tasks are solved? Here we 44 

show that serial learning with transitive inference can be solved by a standard least-squares 45 

linear estimation (LSE) algorithm that reduces TI to linear classification. In contrast to neural 46 

network and reinforcement learning approaches, which involve many free parameters, the LSE 47 

algorithm has no residual degrees of freedom. Yet it exhibits emergent hallmarks of TI 48 

performance, including generalization to out-of-training-set comparisons and the oft-reproduced 49 

symbolic distance effect in accuracy. 50 

Transitive inference (TI) is a classic paradigm widely used to study cognition in humans 51 

and animals. An early example is the problem: “Edith has fairer hair than Suzanne, and Edith 52 

has darker hair than Lili. Does Suzanne have darker hair than Lili?” (Piaget, 1928). Such 53 

problems can be presented to animals in non-verbal form, and every vertebrate species that has 54 

been tested has shown some ability to make transitive inferences (Jensen 2017). The ability to 55 

perform TI has been put forth as evidence of logical reasoning in animals (McGonigle and 56 

Chalmers 1977). 57 

TI is a component of serial, relational learning and extends to ordered sets much larger 58

Over the past few decades, many computational models of TI have been proposed 65 

(Wynne 1995; Wu & Levy 2001; De Lillo et al., 2001; Vasconcelos 2008; Kumaran & 66 

McClelland 2013; Rombouts et al., 2015; Jensen et al., 2015; Kumaran et al., 2016; Lazareva et 67

DiAntonio et al., 2024). Jensen et al. (2019) conducted an extensive analysis of reinforcement 69 

learning algorithms applied to TI tasks, finding that the most successful algorithms exploit 70 

transitivity to explicitly construct an ordered representation, thereby enabling generalization 71 

and symbolic distance effects. 72 

A recent study (Lippl et al., 2024) used first principles to derive minimal conditions for 73 

performing TI and showed that linear stimulus-response mappings necessarily generalize 74 

transitively. This analysis raised the possibility of a radically simplified framework for analyzing 75 

TI using linear classification. The present study explores that possibility with the aim of 76 

clarifying the mathematical basis of transitive inference. 77

is characterized by three numbers ( p = { p1, p 2, p 3}), which can be plotted in a 3D space ( Fig. 137 

2A ). These numbers can be thought of as stimulus features. For example, they could 138 

represent the color, shape, and size of the stimuli. These properties are assigned at random, and 139

from those of other items. While Fig. 2 presents one particular mapping (with the red diamond 150

weights is a linear classifier. 154

Standard TI Task with Transfer 156 

A typical 7-item transitive inference task can be described as a system of linear 157 

equations. To formalize this, a set of n = 7 unique stimuli is generated (the analysis generalizes 158 

to larger sets). Although laboratory tasks often use pictorial images, a simpler stimulus 159 

representation suffices here. Each stimulus is tokenized as a row vector of p = 7 elements, where 160 

each element is assigned a random value between 0 and 1; p is referred to as the description 161 

length . The stimulus vector can be thought of as a unique “bar code” for each stimulus. 162 

Each of the seven stimuli is randomly assigned a unique rank, indicated by the letters A 163 

through G, creating an ordered list ( Fig. 1A ). The task is presented to participants as a series 164 

of discrete trials. During each trial ( Fig. 1B ), two stimuli are presented. The correct response is 165 

to choose the stimulus with the higher rank (e.g., choose A from the pair A/B). 166 

The training set comprises the six adjacent stimulus pairs (A/B, B/C, C/D, D/E, E/F, 167 

F/G, Fig. 1C ). Each pair is presented in both spatial configurations—once with the higher-168 

ranked stimulus on the left and once on the right. The testing set ( Fig. 1D ) comprises all pairs, 169 

adjacent and non-adjacent, presented in both spatial configurations. Spatial counterbalancing 170 

ensures the decision is non-trivial: because there are two possible response locations for each 171 

stimulus pair, the subject cannot respond correctly without identifying the relative ranks of the 172 

presented stimuli. 173

embedding ,W, that represents the training set as a ( 2(𝑛 − 1) × 2𝑝 ) matrix (Fig. 3A ). Each 175

The LSE solution incorporates a mechanism for choosing the correct stimulus from each 210 

pair. First, note that the classifier, x, is anti-symmetric ( Fig. 4A) such that the lower half of x211 

is the negative of the upper half. This points to a differencing mechanism in which the margin 212 

(inner product), M, for any generic stimulus pair, U/V , is equal to: 213 

Eqn. 4. M = [ U1…Up] * [ x1… x p] – [ V1…Vp] * [x1… x p]214 

The decision rule is that when the sign of M is positive, the stimulus on the left is chosen; when 215

the subtraction happens automatically by taking the inner product of the trial vector 217

the inner product determines the choice. This decision mechanism is embedded in the LSE 219

Independence of Trial Order 246 

The LSE solution does not depend on how the rows of the design matrix are ordered. In 247 

the example shown above, the rows of the design matrix W have a particular order (the first 248 

row is always A/B, the second B/A, the third B/C, and so forth). The results do not depend 249 

on this ordering of rows. The solution depends only on the elements of the stimulus vectors. 250 

The rows can be randomly shuffled and LSE will still find a solution that generalizes and shows 251 

a symbolic distance effect. In fact, the lines in Fig 4A represent a solution when the design 252 

matrix ( W) was ordered as in Fig. 2A , while the filled dots represent the solution when the 253 

rows of the same task design matrix were randomly shuffled. 254 

The main results, i.e. generalization from adjacent-only pairs to all pairs, and the 255 

symbolic distance effect, do not depend on the outcomes being symmetric (+1 for correct and -1 256 

for incorrect choices). The set of possible outcomes can be any pair of values, e.g. [1 0], as long 257 

as the values are distinct. 258

Transverse patterning 321 

Conventional TI has an open-ended topology. The two terminal items have different 322 

outcome contingencies compared to the internal list items. For example, internal item C is the 323

## Discussion 374 

In this study, a transitive inference problem was formulated as one that could be solved 375 

by least-squares linear estimation (LSE), which finds a linear classifier mapping the task 376 

representation onto the outcome space. The approach makes no explicit assumptions about 377 

transitivity or list order, and requires no iterative training to update weights. Nevertheless, the 378 

LSE solution reproduces key features of TI behavior observed in living subjects. (1) A classifier 379 

trained on adjacent pairs correctly generalizes to non-adjacent pairs. (2) The magnitude of the 380 

decision variable (margin) scales with the symbolic distance between stimuli, yielding a 381 

symbolic distance effect. (3) The solution implies a differencing mechanism, also identified in 382 

neural network models of TI (Kay et al. 2024). Lippl et al. (2024) formally derive how this 383 

mechanism arises from linear stimulus-response mappings. (4) The solution produces a linear 384 

representation of rank, i.e. a “mental line,” that is consistent with several computational models 385 

of TI. 386 

The way LSE solves TI becomes clear when we examine the inner product of each 387 

stimulus with the linear classifier ( Fig 4B ). The magnitude of these inner products tracks the 388

ranking of the stimuli. This ranking allows the solution to generalize and exhibit a symbolic 389 

distance effect. This implied ranking is reminiscent of models that solve TI by implementing 390 

transitivity explicitly (e.g., Jensen et al., 2015; Jensen et al., 2019). The explicit application of 391 

the transitive rule yields an ordered representation. In the LSE solution, the transitive rule is 392 

not explicit; rather, transitivity implicit in the task design constrains the linear estimator so 393 

that an ordered representation emerges from solving Eqn 1 and then applying the linear 394 

classifier to each stimulus. 395 

The approach described here is not intended as a literal model of how the brain performs 396 

TI. However, biologically plausible mechanisms for matrix operations such as the pseudoinverse 397 

have been proposed (Tapson & van Schaik 2013). More broadly, the LSE approach reveals 398 

information that is latent in the task structure and accessible via linear classification and thus 399 

amenable to a broad class of models including perceptrons and support vector machines. 400 

Transitivity is not assumed a priori but emerges from constraints implicit in the task. These 401 

results suggest that generalization from adjacent to non-adjacent pairs, and the symbolic 402 

distance effect, may be properties of the task structure itself rather than of any particular 403 

computational architecture. This view is consistent with recent findings that generic 404 

architectures with minimal constraints can perform TI (Kay et al. 2024; Lippl et al. 2024). 405 

Subjects who have learned a TI task likely acquire an implicit representation of that structure, 406 

and the present results suggest this representation may suffice to account for many serial 407 

learning phenomena. 408

Cognitive tasks are generally designed to probe specific cognitive functions, but the 415 

correspondence between task and function is seldom exact, and validation is necessary. Some 416 

cognitive functions are defined entirely by performance on specific tasks; others involve 417 

cognitive structures inferred from performance patterns. Computational modeling is an 418 

important validation step, but models often presuppose a particular solution strategy. Subjects, 419 

by contrast, may be given minimal instruction and are free to adopt any strategy—effective or 420 

otherwise. They may arrive at an understanding of the task, or discover strategies, that 421 

experimenters never anticipated. For this reason, it is valuable to consider the full range of 422 

computational approaches to solving a task, including those that may not initially appear 423 

biologically plausible. 424
