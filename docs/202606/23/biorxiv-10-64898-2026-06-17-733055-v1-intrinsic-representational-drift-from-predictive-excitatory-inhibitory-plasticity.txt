# Abstract 10 

Neural representations drift gradually over time even under stable environmental conditions, 11 

but the synaptic mechanisms driving this drift remain unclear. Here we show that 12 

representational drift can arise intrinsically from predictive synaptic plasticity in spiking 13 

excitatory-inhibitory networks. During repeated exposure to unchanged input patterns, 14 

individual neurons gradually changed their preferred pattern while ensemble-level coding 15 

remained stable. These changes in preference were preceded by a weakening of the net 16 

excitatory-inhibitory drive supporting the current preference relative to competing patterns, 17 

and this relative drive predicted changes on the following trial. Extending the model to 18 

hippocampal place coding reproduced experience-dependent tuning-curve drift in CA1, 19 

including the dissociation between elapsed time and intervening exposure. At the population 20 

level, drift was expressed as coordinated rotation and translation of neural state space. Thus, 21 

representational drift can emerge as an intrinsic consequence of predictive E/I plasticity that 22 

maintains balanced, selective population codes. 23

# Introduction 25 

The stability of memories and behaviors is often thought to rely on the stability of the 26 

underlying neural representations. Hippocampal place cells, for example, provide an 27 

apparently robust map of familiar environments by firing at specific locations (O’Keefe & 28 

Dostrovsky, 1971). Yet chronic recordings over days to weeks have revealed that these 29 

representations are not static. In hippocampal CA1, many neurons change their preferred 30

encode spatial information reliably (Ziv et al., 2013; Rubin et al., 2015; Hainmueller & Bartos, 32 

2018). Similar representational drift has been observed in parietal cortex, visual areas, and 33 

olfactory cortex (Driscoll et al., 2017; Deitch et al., 2021; Marks & Goard, 2021; Schoonover et 34 

al., 2021), suggesting that ongoing representational change is a general feature of cortical 35 

circuits rather than a hippocampal specialization. At the population level, this drift has 36

with the relational geometry of the code partly preserved even as individual tuning profiles 38 

change (Sylte et al., 2025). These observations have motivated broader theoretical accounts 39 

of how neural representations can remain informative despite long-term variability and 40 

partial instability (Clopath et al., 2017; Gonzalez et al., 2019; Driscoll et al., 2022; Rule & 41 

O’Leary, 2022; Delamare et al., 2024). 42

Recent experiments have further shown that representational drift is not simply random 44 

degradation. In CA1, the identities and tuning properties of individual neurons can change 45 

over days to weeks, while population-level information is partially preserved (Rubin et al., 46 

2015; Sadeh & Clopath, 2022). Moreover, time and experience affect different aspects of 47 

representational stability. Ensemble firing rates and spatial tuning curves show distinct 48 

dependencies on elapsed time and the number of intervening experiences, and active 49 

experience can drive representational change even within a day (Geva et al., 2023; Khatib et 50 

al., 2023). Related studies of engram dynamics further show that neuronal ensembles are not 51 

fixed entities. Instead, ensemble membership is shaped by experience history and cellular 52 

state, which bias memory allocation and subsequent ensemble remodeling over time (Cai et 53 

al., 2016; Cho et al., 2021; Silva et al., 2009; Zhou et al., 2009; Rashid et al., 2016; Rogerson et 54 

al., 2014; Pignatelli et al., 2019; Josselyn & Tonegawa, 2020; Mau et al., 2020; Delamare et al., 55 

2024). These findings suggest that representational drift reflects an interaction between 56 

ongoing circuit changes and experience-dependent plasticity. However, the synaptic 57 

mechanisms linking experience, plasticity, and individual neuronal changes remain unclear. 58 

In particular, it remains unknown what determines when and why individual neurons 59 

change their representational preference. 60

75 

Here we address this problem using a recurrent spiking excitatory-inhibitory network 76 

trained with a local predictive learning rule, in which excitatory synapses predict 77 

postsynaptic activity and inhibitory synapses track excitatory drive to maintain predictive 78 

E/I balance. Repeated exposure to the same input patterns was sufficient to produce 79 

representational drift. Trial-resolved drive analysis showed that changes in preference were 80 

preceded by a weakening of the net drive supporting the current preference, partly through 81 

progressive strengthening of inhibition onto the active assembly. This relative drive predicted 82 

changes on the following trial, linking ongoing E/I rebalancing to individual preference 83 

changes. Extending the same framework to hippocampal place coding reproduced recently 84 

reported experimental findings, including separable time- and experience-dependent 85 

stability (Geva et al., 2023) and coordinated rotation and translation of population state space 86 

(Sylte et al., 2025). These results provide a synaptic mechanism linking local predictive 87 

plasticity to single-neuron instability and population-level stability. 88

E/I rebalancing drives single-neuron preference change 143 

We next sought to identify the synaptic changes that precede preference changes in 144 

individual neurons. In the drifting assemblies described above, some neurons lost their 145 

association with one pattern-selective assembly and became reassigned to another assembly 146 

(Fig. 3A). Because the underlying drift was continuous, we operationally defined stable 147 

preference changes as changes in preferred pattern that persisted across consecutive trials. 148 

We then aligned these transitions to the time of reassignment and examined how pattern-149 

conditioned synaptic drive changed around them. 150 

Neurons undergoing a preference change showed a stereotyped transition in net E/I drive. 151 

The net drive associated with the previously preferred pattern was dominant before the 152 

change but declined and dropped sharply around reassignment. Concurrently, the net drive 153 

associated with the newly preferred pattern began rising before the change and became 154 

dominant afterward (Fig. 3B). The preference change was therefore not an isolated abrupt 155 

event, but reflected a competition between the two assembly representations that resolved in 156 

favor of the new pattern. 157 

To identify the synaptic mechanism underlying this net drive transition, we examined the 158 

excitatory weight update associated with the previously preferred pattern. Before the change, 159 

the excitatory update was positive, consistent with continued strengthening of synapses 160 

supporting the current assembly. Shortly before reassignment, however, this update 161 

decreased sharply and turned negative after the change (Fig. 3C). This reversal coincided 162 

with a transient increase in inhibitory drive. Because the excitatory update depends on the 163

The model reproduces experience-dependent hippocampal representational 196 

drift 197 

We next asked whether the same predictive plasticity framework could reproduce the time-198 

and experience-dependent components of hippocampal representational drift reported by 199 

Geva et al. (2023). In that study, CA1 activity was recorded longitudinally while mice 200 

experienced two familiar environments at different frequencies, allowing elapsed time and 201 

intervening experience to be dissociated. We therefore simulated two linear-track 202 

environments with different exposure schedules: Env A was visited every 2 days, whereas 203 

Env B was visited every 4 days (Fig. 4A). During each environmental exposure, spatially 204 

tuned input neurons drove the recurrent network along the linear track. After learning, the 205 

model produced place-cell-like tuning curves that gradually changed across days (Fig. 4B). 206 

Despite this single-neuron drift, the population retained an ordered spatial representation. 207 

In Env A, sorting population activity by Day 1 preferred positions revealed disruption of 208 

sequential place-field organization, whereas sorting each day by its own preferred positions 209 

restored sequential population maps (Fig. 4C). Thus, as in the pattern-assembly task, spatial 210 

organization was preserved at the population level while the identity and preferred positions 211 

of individual neurons changed over time. Similar activity maps for both environments 212 

confirmed that spatial tuning drift occurred in Env A and Env B, while sequential population 213 

organization was preserved within each environment (Supplementary Fig. 2). 214

hippocampal representational drift across repeated environmental exposures. Single-neuron 255 

tuning changed gradually, but population-level spatial organization remained structured, 256 

and different stability measures showed separable dependencies on the exposure schedule. 257

We then decomposed model drift into three similarity transformations: rotation, translation, 277 

and uniform scaling of the population state space. For each simulated recording day, we 278 

constructed a population activity matrix whose rows corresponded to spatial positions and 279 

whose columns corresponded to model neurons, and compared each day with Day 1. 280 

Rotation was quantified as the mean principal angle between the Day 1 PCA subspace and 281 

the corresponding subspace on each later day. Translation was quantified as the change of 282 

the population centroid relative to Day 1, and uniform scaling was quantified as the relative 283 

norm of mean-centered population activity. Rotation increased gradually across recording 284 

days, and translation also increased progressively over time (Fig. 5C). In contrast, scaling 285 

remained approximately stable, indicating that drift was not primarily caused by a global 286 

expansion or contraction of the population activity cloud. 287 

Finally, we asked which of these transformations best explained the decline in population-288 

vector correlation with Day 1. Using a linear mixed model that controlled for the other 289 

transformations, we found that rotation was the strongest predictor of population-vector 290 

drift, with a weaker contribution from translation and little contribution from uniform 291 

scaling (Fig. 5D). 292 

Together, these results indicate that local predictive plasticity does not merely destabilize 293 

individual place fields. Instead, it produces a coordinated population-level drift in which the 294 

spatial code remains geometrically organized while the population state space gradually 295 

rotates and translates over time. 296

This work suggests that representational drift need not reflect random degradation of stored 313 

representations. Instead, it can emerge from the same plasticity mechanisms that maintain 314 

predictive E/I balance in the circuit. In the present learning rule, inhibitory synapses track 315 

total excitatory drive, implementing a form of homeostatic E/I co-tuning that has been 316 

established experimentally and theoretically (Vogels et al., 2011). As excitatory weights 317 

continue to change during ongoing learning, inhibitory plasticity gradually alters the relative 318 

net drive between competing representations, making individual neurons susceptible to 319 

reassignment. Changes in preference therefore occur not because excitation to the preferred 320 

pattern simply collapses, but because synaptic drive for that pattern is weakened as 321 

inhibition progressively strengthens onto the currently active assembly. At the population 322 

level, the same learning dynamics produced coordinated rotation and translation of state 323 

space, allowing population geometry to remain organized despite ongoing single-neuron 324 

drift. 325 

This view differs from models in which drift is driven by externally imposed synaptic noise, 326 

stochastic weight perturbations, or Hebbian competition among fluctuating inputs (Kossio 327 

et al., 2021; Qin et al., 2023). In those frameworks, individual changes are effectively 328 

stochastic. Related work has shown that stable task information can be maintained despite 329 

unstable neural populations (Rule et al., 2020; Rule & O’Leary, 2022), and other models have 330 

linked assembly drift to STDP-based plasticity, dynamic attractor mechanisms, intrinsic 331 

excitability fluctuations, or synaptic volatility (Manz & Memmesheimer, 2023; Spalla et al., 332 

2021; Mongillo et al., 2017; Delamare et al., 2024). Our results indicate that the CA1-like drift 333 

phenomenology does not require explicit synaptic rewiring or externally imposed weight 334 

perturbations. In the proposed model, predictive plasticity during active environmental 335 

exposure was sufficient to generate progressive changes in spatial tuning. This produced a 336 

day-dependent decline in ensemble rate correlation, while tuning-curve correlation was 337 

more closely aligned with the number of intervening environmental exposures. 338 

More broadly, the framework suggests that different components of representational change 339 

can be dissociated because they are differentially shaped by experience-dependent synaptic 340 

learning and by the temporal structure of environmental exposure. In biological circuits, 341 

structural turnover may provide an additional source of long-timescale variability, and 342 

understanding how such processes interact with activity-dependent plasticity could help 343

The model makes several experimentally testable predictions. First, neurons with weaker 349 

current response selectivity should be more likely to change their preferred representation 350 

on subsequent exposures, because low selectivity reflects reduced net drive for the current 351 

preferred pattern. This prediction can be tested in longitudinal recordings by asking whether 352 

single-cell selectivity in one session predicts remapping probability in the next. Second, 353 

partial suppression of inhibitory plasticity should slow drift rates while preserving pattern 354 

selectivity, because inhibitory weight changes are the proximal driver of the excitatory 355 

learning-signal reversal that underlies preference change. Finally, although structural 356 

synaptic turnover was not required in the present model, it may contribute to long-timescale 357 

drift in biological circuits. 358 

Together, these findings suggest that representational drift reflects an intrinsic consequence 359 

of predictive E/I plasticity rather than a breakdown of memory stability. By continually 360 

rebalancing excitation and inhibition, the same local learning rule can preserve structured 361 

population codes and organized state-space geometry while gradually reassigning the 362 

representational roles of individual neurons. 363

Trial-resolved drive decomposition and preference tracking 433 

In Figure 3, we recorded trial-averaged firing responses, excitatory drive, inhibitory drive, 434 

net E/I drive, and excitatory prediction errors for each neuron during the drift phase. 435 

Pattern-conditioned values were estimated with a sliding-window average over the five most 436 

recent presentations of each pattern, and the preferred pattern was defined as the pattern 437 

evoking the largest mean firing response. Stable changes were defined as changes in 438 

preferred pattern that persisted for at least two consecutive trials before and after the 439 

transition. We then computed transition-triggered averages around detected changes and 440 

asked whether current selectivity predicted change on the following trial. Selectivity was 441 

defined as the difference between the largest and second-largest pattern-conditioned values 442 

for response, excitatory drive, or inhibitory drive, and change probability was computed 443 

after binning trials by each selectivity measure. 444

Details of simulating place field sequences 446 

In Figure 4, we used a spatial variant of the same model to reproduce the experimental 447 

paradigm of Geva et al. (2023). Afferent input was provided by 800 spatially tuned input 448 

neurons with Gaussian tuning curves tiling a virtual linear track of 400 positions. Two 449 

environments were encoded by non-overlapping subsets of input neurons: neurons 1–400 for 450 

Env A and neurons 401–800 for Env B. The protocol followed the 21-day schedule of Geva et 451 

al. (2023): Env A was visited every 2 days, whereas Env B was visited every 4 days. Each 452 

session consisted of a training epoch with learning enabled, followed by a frozen-weight test 453 

epoch during which spatial tuning curves were measured. 454 

455 

Representational stability was quantified using the same two metrics as Geva et al. (2023). 456 

Ensemble rate correlation (ERC) was computed as the Pearson correlation between vectors 457 

of mean firing rates across neurons, whereas tuning curve correlation (TCC) was computed 458 

as the mean across neurons of the Pearson correlation between spatial tuning curves. Both 459 

metrics were evaluated as a function of elapsed calendar days and intervening sessions, 460

Population geometry and similarity transformation analyses 464 

In Figure 5, to quantify coordinated drift of the spatial population code, for each session, we 465 

constructed a position-by-neuron population activity matrix after normalizing each neuron’s 466 

tuning curve within session. Single-cell tuning correlation was computed as the mean 467 

correlation between each neuron’s tuning curve in a given session and on Day 1. Population 468 

geometry correlation was computed by comparing representational dissimilarity matrices 469 

across spatial positions with the Day 1 reference. 470 

471 

For visualization, Day 1 activity was used to define a three-dimensional PCA space, and 472 

activity from later sessions was projected into this space before and after alignment to the 473 

Day 1 reference trajectory. To quantify coordinated drift, we decomposed changes relative to 474 

Day 1 into rotation, translation, and uniform scaling. Rotation was measured as the mean 475 

principal angle between PCA subspaces, translation as the displacement of the population 476 

centroid, and scaling as the relative norm of mean-centered population activity. Finally, we 477 

used a linear mixed model to estimate how each transformation contributed to population-478 

vector drift while controlling for the others, with simulation identity included as a grouping 479 

factor. 480
