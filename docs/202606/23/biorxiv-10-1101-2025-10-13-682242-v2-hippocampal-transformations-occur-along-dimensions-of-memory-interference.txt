ABSTRACT 1

The role of the hippocampus in resolving memory interference has been greatly elucidated by 2

considering the relationship between the similarity of visual stimuli (input) and corresponding 3

similarity of hippocampal activity patterns (output). However, these input-output functions can take 4

surprisingly different forms. Here, we reconcile seemingly conflicting findings by considering the 5

possibility that the hippocampus prioritizes different dimensions of visual similarity across different 6

stages of learning. First, we generated a set of natural scene images from two visual categories and 7

rigorously characterized visual similarity using a wide array of methods: neural networks, artificial 8

intelligence models, and human perceptual and memory decisions. We then identified two 9

orthogonal dimensions of visual similarity that each predicted memory interference, but that did so 10 

at distinct stages of learning. Using high-resolution fMRI, we then tested for dimension-specific 11 

input-output functions within the hippocampus. Within CA3 and dentate gyrus (CA3/DG), we show 12 

that dimensions of visual similarity were inverted (repulsion) at stages of learning when they 13 

contributed to memory interference. In contrast, CA3/DG preserved similarity when dimensions did 14 

not contribute to interference. These findings reveal that hippocampal representations of visually-15 

similar stimuli are highly dynamic and critically depend on the dimensions of similarity that currently 16 

contribute to memory interference. 17

INTRODUCTION 18 

A critical facet of the hippocampus’ role in episodic memory is its capacity to minimize interference 1– 

19 6. Because memory interference is driven by similarity between stimuli 7–11 , theories of interference 20 

resolution in the hippocampus have focused on how stimulus similarity (input similarity) translates 21 

to the similarity of corresponding hippocampal representations (output similarity). While a general 22 

principle of interference resolution is that output similarity should be lower than input similarity 1,5 ,23 

this principle can be satisfied in qualitatively distinct ways. Indeed, in some contexts, increases in 24 

stimulus similarity yield positive, but sub-linear increases in hippocampal similarity 1,5,12 . In other 25 

contexts, increases in stimulus similarity yield no change in hippocampal similarity 13,14 . In yet other 26 

contexts, higher stimulus similarity has been associated with lower hippocampal similarity 15–17 . A 27 

major challenge for theories of the hippocampus is to explain this variability in the shape of input 28 

output functions. 29 

One reason why relationships between stimulus similarity and hippocampal similarity may be so 30 

variable is because similarity, itself, can be defined using multiple metrics 18–23 that potentially 31 

capture multiple underlying dimensions of similarity 18–21,23–25 . This raises the obvious question: which 32 

dimensions ‘matter’ to the hippocampus? If a primary function of the hippocampus is to minimize 33 

interference, then a simple, but potentially clarifying perspective is that the hippocampus will target 34 

dimensions of similarity that drive interference. That said, an underappreciated point is that 35 

interference may be a moving target : the specific dimensions of similarity that drive interference may 36 

shift with learning 26 . While speculative, this may explain why relationships between stimulus 37 

similarity and hippocampal similarity sometimes change with experience 16,17,27,28 . However, formally 38 

testing this idea requires (a) identifying distinct dimensions of stimulus similarity, (b) characterizing 39 

how these dimensions influence memory interference across stages of learning, and (c) testing 40 

whether dimension-specific influences on behavior explain the shape of input-output functions in 41 

the hippocampus. 42 

Here, we first developed a set of natural scene images from two visual categories and obtained 10 43 

different metrics of visual similarity. These metrics of visual similarity were generated from new 44 

human behavioral data and various computational methods. By applying Principal Component 45 

Analysis (PCA) to the similarity metrics, we identified two orthogonal dimensions of similarity that 46 

strongly predicted memory interference errors. Strikingly, we found that the relative influence of 47 

these dimensions on behavior sharply changed with experience (learning). Whereas one dimension 48 

drove interference during early stages of learning, the other drove interference during later stages of 49 

learning. Finally, using high-resolution fMRI, we show that dimension-specific input-output 50 

functions in CA3 and dentate gyrus (CA3/DG) assumed qualitatively different shapes across stages 51 

of learning. Specifically, CA3/DG inverted each dimension of visual similarity (negative input-output 52 

functions) when it contributed to memory interference but preserved each dimension (positive 53 

functions) when it was not actively contributing to interference. Thus, entirely opposite coding 54 

schemes of similarity dimensions in the hippocampus can be explained by the extent to which these 55 

dimensions were behaviorally relevant. These findings provide important insight into how the 56 

hippocampus resolves memory interference, demonstrating highly dynamic and adaptive processes 57 

that dramatically increase the representational distance between memories that are most at risk for 58 

interference. 59

We first generated a set of 48 visual stimuli (natural scenes) drawn from two categories: beaches 63 

and gazebos ( Fig. 1a ). We then selected 10 different similarity metrics that yielded (for each metric) 64 

a matrix ( Fig. 1b ) describing the similarity of each pair of scenes within each category (276 beach 65 

pairs + 276 gazebo pairs = 552 pairs in total). Two of these metrics were based on human behavioral 66 

ratings that we collected in separate experiments. In one experiment ( behavioral memory 67 

experiment ), we measured associative memory confusability ( Fig. 1c , left; also see Supplementary 68 

Fig. 1 ). In the other experiment ( perceptual similarity experiment ), we measured subjective 69 

judgments of perceptual similarity ( Fig. 1c , right). The other 8 metrics were based on neural network 70 

models [three different layers of VGG-16, AlexNet, and Contrastive Language-Image Pretraining 71 

(CLIP)], Natural Language Processing (NLP) applied to AI-generated text descriptions of images 72 

(Supplementary Fig. 2 ), a measure of structural image features [Structural Similarity Index (SSIM)], 73 

and a model of global image features (GIST). (See Methods for details of each measure). Rather than 74 

identifying a single measure or model that ‘best’ describes stimulus similarity, our goal was to 75 

extract dimensions of similarity from the set of measures/models. 76 

Given that the behavioral memory experiment and the perceptual similarity experiment were based 77 

on human responses, it was important to establish that resulting similarity metrics were reliable. To 78 

test this, we computed split-half reliability. Specifically, we divided the pool of participants in half, 79 

calculated similarity matrices within each half of the participants, and then correlated these 80 

similarity matrices. For both experiments, the mean split-half reliability (r) exceeded 0.69, with 81 

reliability asymptotically increasing as a function of the total number of participants included ( Fig. 82 

1d ). 83 

While the 10 similarity matrices were generally positively correlated with each other, Pearson 84 

correlations ranged from -0.154 to 0.665 ( Fig. 1e ). To identify different dimensions of similarity, each 85 

of the 10 similarity matrices was vectorized and combined in a new matrix that contained the 86 

pairwise similarity for all 552 within-category pairs across all 10 similarity metrics ( Fig. 1f ). We then 87 

applied a principal component analysis (PCA) to this new matrix to identify orthogonal components 88 

(dimensions) of similarity across the 10 metrics ( Fig. 1g ). The first 2 principal components (PC1 and 89 

PC2) explained over 60% of the variance in the similarity matrix (41.3% and 19.1%, respectively; Fig. 90 

1h ). For PC1, the metrics with the highest positive loadings were VGG max pooling layers 1 and 3, 91 

SSIM, and AlexNet. For PC2, the metrics with the highest positive loadings were memory 92 

confusability (behavioral memory experiment), perceptual judgments (perceptual similarity 93 

experiment), NLP of text descriptions, and GIST ( Fig. 1i ). These loadings suggest that PC1 captured 94 

low- to mid-level perceptual similarity, whereas PC2 captured semantic similarity (see Methods for 95 

additional consideration of PC interpretations and see Supplementary Fig. 3 for pairs of images with 96 

high vs. low PC scores). The loadings for all 10 PCs are in Supplementary Table 1 .97

Establishing behaviorally relevant dimensions of similarity 116 

We next sought to establish the dimensions of visual similarity that were related to behavioral 117 

expressions of memory interference. We addressed this by analyzing behavioral data from the fMRI 118 

experiment , which was entirely independent from the data reported above. The fMRI experiment was 119 

modeled after the behavioral memory experiment ( Fig. 1c ), using the same stimuli (24 beaches, 24 120 

gazebos) and associative learning framework. The experiment included three phases ( Fig. 2a ): 121 

scene-object associative learning conducted prior to fMRI scanning (training phase), scene 122 

exposure during scanning (exposure phase), and an associative memory test conducted after 123 

scanning (post-test). 124 

The training phase consisted of alternating study-test rounds (3 total). During study rounds, each 125 

scene was presented with a unique object. During test rounds, scenes were presented along with 126 

three objects and participants selected the associated object. Of the three objects, one was correct 127 

(target), one was associated with a scene from the same category as the current scene (competitor) 128 

and one was associated with a scene from the other category (non-competitor). Importantly, for 129 

each participant, one visual category was designated the ‘high training’ category, and the other was 130 

‘low training’. High training associations were presented more often, for longer durations, and with 131 

feedback during test rounds. During the exposure phase, participants were repeatedly shown all 48 132 

scenes from the training phase, along with a handful of novel scenes. Participants were instructed 133 

to identify the novel scenes. In the post-test, each scene was presented along with all 24 objects 134 

that had been associated with that category (1 target, 23 competitors). Participants attempted to 135 

select the target. 136 

The post-test data were used to identify dimensions of visual similarity that produced interference. 137 

In particular, the data were used to generate a pairwise memory confusability matrix for each visual 138 

category ( Fig. 2b ). To identify similarity dimensions that predicted memory interference errors, we 139 

ran mixed-effects models (a separate model for each PC) for which the dependent measure was the 140 

pairwise value from the post-test confusability matrix and the predictor included PC scores for one 141 

of the 10 dimensions of similarity ( Fig. 1g ). Significant effects of PC score (at Bonferroni corrected α 142 

= 0.005) were observed for PC1 (β = 0.288, t (28699) = 10.933, P < 0.001), PC2 (β = 0.361, t (28699) = 9.470, 143 

P < 0.001), PC4 (β = -0.193, t (28699) = -3.478, P < 0.001), and PC6 (β = 0.212, t (28699) = 2.823, P = 0.005) 144 

(Fig. 2c ). As a robustness test, we also ran an alternate version of the analysis in which we computed 145 

subject-level correlations (Pearson correlation) between confusability matrices and PC-derived 146 

similarity matrices. Resulting correlation coefficients were z-transformed and compared against 147 

zero via one-sample, two-tailed t-tests. Significant effects of PC score (at Bonferroni corrected α = 148 

0.005) were observed for PC1 (t (51) = 10.251, P < 0.001, d = 1.450), PC2 (t (51) = 10.672, P < 0.001, d = 149 

1.509), and PC4 (t (51) = -3.422, P = 0.001, d = -0.484). 150

Experience-dependent changes in behavior 174 

The training manipulation in the fMRI experiment (high vs. low training) was used to test for 175 

experience-dependent changes in behavior and the hippocampus. To confirm that the training 176 

manipulation was successful, we used mixed-effects models to assess behavioral performance 177 

during the training phase (prior to scanning), the exposure phase (during scanning), and the post-178 

test (after scanning). During the training phase, there was a main effect of training level (high vs. low; 179 

β = 0.347, t (307) = 12.313, P < 0.001; Fig. 3a , left) and a main effect of test round on memory accuracy 180 

(β = 0.088, t (307) = 11.460, P < 0.001). These main effects indicate, respectively, that associative 181 

memory was (i) better with high vs. low training and (ii) improved across test rounds. When 182 

participants did make errors during the training phase, these tended to be ‘interference errors’: 183 

choosing an object that was associated with the same scene category as the target, as opposed to 184 

the opposite category (β = 0.231, t (615) = 15.091, P < 0.001; Fig. 3a , right). For the exposure phase— 185 

which only measured scene recognition—discriminability (d’) was significantly greater for high- vs. 186 

low-training scenes (β = 0.473, t (101) = 3.887, P < 0.001; Fig. 3b ). For the post-test, associative memory 187 

accuracy was again better for high- vs. low-training scenes (β = 0.291, t (101) = 9.320, P < 0.001; Fig. 3c ), 188 

confirming that the benefits of high training (in the training phase) persisted throughout the 189 

experiment. 190 

We next tested whether the relative influence of each dimension of similarity on memory errors 191 

changed as a function of training. To test this, we focused on performance during the test rounds of 192 

the training phase (of the fMRI experiment). Specifically, we used mixed-effects models in which the 193 

dependent variable represented trial-by-trial interference errors (selecting the competitor; Fig. 2a ). 194 

The key independent fixed effects were the degree centrality of the presented scene, separately for 195 

PC1 and PC2. We predicted that scenes with higher degree centrality (stronger ‘connections’ to 196 

other scenes; Fig. 2d ) would be associated with more interference errors. 197 

For PC2, there was a significant interaction between degree centrality and training (β = 0.036, t (7464) =198 

2.408, P = 0.016; Fig. 3d ); for PC1, the interaction was qualitatively opposite, but not significant (β = 199 

-0.015, t (7464) = -1.110, P = 0.267; Fig. 3d ). A linear contrast (F-test) confirmed a significant difference 200 

between these two effects ( Dβ = 0.051, F (1, 7464) = 4.144, P = 0.042; Fig. 3d ). Qualitatively, the influence 201 

of PC1 on memory errors was stronger with low training, whereas the influence of PC2 was stronger 202

but not PC2 (β = 0.0005, t (3732) = 0.111, P = 0.912) whereas for the high-training condition there was a 205 

significant effect of PC2 (β = 0.0367, t (3732) = 2.596, P = 0.009), but not PC1 (β = -0.0064, t (3732) = -0.510, 206

Discussion 327 

Relationships between visual similarity and hippocampal similarity are at the core of theories of 328 

hippocampal function 1–5 . Yet, these relationships can take qualitatively different forms 5,12,14– 

329 17,27,28,30,31,34–41 . Here, we show that the shape of input-output functions critically depends on the 330 

current relevance of individual dimensions of visual similarity to behavioral expressions of memory 331 

interference. We first identified two dimensions of visual similarity that robustly contributed to 332 

memory interference. We then show that these dimensions of similarity influenced behavior 333 

sequentially , across stages of learning. Finally, in CA3 and dentate gyrus (CA3/DG), we reveal a 334 

striking parallel to these behavioral findings: across learning, CA3/DG inverted each dimension of 335 

similarity precisely when it contributed to memory interference. These findings provide important 336 

insight into when and why the hippocampus transforms dimensions of visual similarity. Specifically, 337 

hippocampal input-output functions dramatically change with experience, and these changes 338 

reflect a dynamic prioritization of dimensions of similarity that are most relevant to behavior. 339 

The major innovation in our approach is that we rigorously characterized different dimensions of 340 

visual similarity. We did this by aggregating 10 similarity metrics and using PCA to extract orthogonal 341 

components (dimensions). Thus, rather than operationalizing dimensions based on an assumed 1-342 

to-1 mapping (metric X = dimension Y), our approach allowed dimensions to be distributed across 343 

metrics ( Fig. 1i ). Importantly, the dimensions we identified were behaviorally relevant—participants 344 

were much more likely to confuse pairs of images that had high PC1 or PC2 scores ( Fig. 2c ). While 345 

many prior behavioral studies also show that similarity drives memory interference 7–11 and that 346 

interference occurs along specific visual dimensions 42 , our findings reveal an important caveat: 347 

different dimensions of similarity drive interference at different stages of learning. Indeed, we 348 

show—and replicate—that during early stages of learning, there was an influence from PC1, but not 349 

PC2 ; in late stages of learning, there was an influence from PC2, but not PC1 (Fig. 3d-e ). 350 

The fact that dimensions of similarity drove interference in serial (not parallel), is reminiscent of— 351 

and consistent with—findings from the perceptual discrimination literature which show that, when 352 

categorizing multidimensional stimuli, humans favor unidimensional strategies 43–47 . Here, PC1 353 

putatively drove interference errors first (followed by PC2) because, by definition, PC1 explained 354 

more variance in the similarity matrices (41.3%) than PC2 (19.1%) 46 . The fact that PC1 had no 355 

influence during later stages of learning indicates that interference on this dimension was 356 

successfully resolved. While caution is warranted when explicitly labeling or describing PCA 357 

components, the observed loadings for PC1 and PC2 ( Fig. 1i ) suggest that PC1 reflected relatively 358 

lower-level/perceptual information whereas PC2 reflected higher-level/semantic information. This 359 

interpretation is potentially compatible with other evidence showing that, with experience, 360 

memories transition from perceptual to semantic information 48–51 . That said, we do not predict that 361 

the relative prioritization of PC1 vs. PC2 observed here will necessarily generalize across other 362 

stimulus sets and procedures. Rather, we believe the current findings reflect a generalizable 363 

principle : that interference is resolved ‘one dimension at a time’. For example, by changing task 364 

demands or the composition of the stimulus set, it may be possible to manipulate which dimensions 365 

of similarity are relevant at each stage of learning. However, the key prediction would be that even if 366 

dimensions are re-prioritized, interference would still be resolved one dimension at a time. 367 

By considering different dimensions of visual similarity and different levels of experience, we show 368 

that relationships between visual similarity and hippocampal similarity (input-output functions) can 369 

take remarkably different forms, ranging from strongly positive to strongly negative ( Fig. 4c ;370 

Supplementary Table 3 ). These findings add to recent evidence that relationships between visual 371 

similarity and hippocampal similarity are experience-dependent 17,27,28 . However, our behavioral 372 

findings provide a compelling framework for understanding these experience-dependent changes. 373

Namely, for PC1 and PC2, CA3/DG either preserved or inverted each dimension according to 374 

whether it actively contributed to interference. Thus, CA3/DG had access to diverse visual input 52 ,375 

but selectively transformed this input when it was adaptive for behavior 53–55 .376 

The fact that we specifically implicate CA3/DG in resolving memory interference (as opposed to CA1) 377 

is consistent with prior human and rodent studies 15,31,36,37,56–58 . In particular, CA3/DG has been 378 

implicated in orthogonalizing similar memories (pattern separation) 1,5,14 . That said, standard 379 

accounts of pattern separation do not predict or explain negative input-output functions 1–5 . In fact, 380 

with perfect orthogonalization, input-output functions would be flat, not negative (for additional 381 

consideration of this point, see 27,59 ). Thus, while reinforcing the relevance of CA3/DG to memory 382 

interference, our findings also challenge existing models of how CA3/DG resolves interference. 383 

An important aspect of our design and analyses is that we densely sampled a narrow range at the 384 

high end of similarity space. Namely, our models included 276 pairwise comparisons within each 385 

visual category. In contrast, the vast majority of prior empirical evidence for negative input-output 386 

functions in the hippocampus comes from studies that have only sparsely sampled similarity 387 

space 15–17,27,28,30,34,35,37,39,60 (high vs. low similarity; cf .34 ). More broadly, the input-output functions we 388 

observed in CA3/DG (positive and negative) indicate a striking degree of sensitivity to gradations 389 

within a relatively narrow similarity space. In fact, CA3/DG was more sensitive to within-category 390 

differences (e.g., differences between beaches) than across-category differences (differences 391 

between beaches and gazebos) (i.e., within-category similarity did not systematically differ from 392 

across-category similarity; Fig 4b ). 393 

Although we primarily focus on input-output functions within each visual category, we also observed 394 

a surprising main effect of training in CA3/DG: lower similarity scores (greater differentiation) in the 395 

low training condition compared to the high training condition ( Fig. 4b ). This effect strongly 396 

contrasted with EVC, which showed a robust decrease in similarity scores with training. While 397 

speculative, these opposing effects are potentially interrelated: CA3/DG differentiation may be most 398 

likely to occur when input from visual cortical areas is un-differentiated 16,17,27,28 . From this 399 

perspective, if visual cortical areas differentiate stimuli (through experience-dependent attention to 400 

diagnostic features 61–63 ), then there is less need for additional differentiation by CA3/DG 27,38 .401 

In summary, we show that, in behavior and the hippocampus, memory interference is resolved one 402 

dimension at a time. When a particular dimension of visual similarity is actively contributing to 403 

memory interference, CA3 and dentate gyrus dramatically—but transiently—invert that dimension. 404 

These findings can be interpreted through a simple framework in which the hippocampus selectively 405 

exaggerates the representational distance between memories that are most at risk for interference. 406

Participants 408 

Human subject data were collected for three experiments: the behavioral memory experiment, the 409 

perceptual similarity experiment, and the fMRI experiment. Across the three experiments, a total of 410 

655 participants were enrolled. All participants’ enrollment followed procedures approved by the 411 

University of Oregon Institutional Review Board. For the behavioral memory experiment and the fMRI 412 

experiment, all participants were right-handed native-English speakers. For the fMRI experiment, all 413 

participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, with no self-reported psychiatric or 414 

neurological disease. 415 

For the behavioral memory experiment, data were collected from 463 participants (200 female, 416 

mean age = 28.02, range = 18 – 35 years) using the online platform Prolific. Informed consent was 417 

obtained, online, for each participant prior to the experiment. To ensure that the memory 418 

confusability matrix obtained from this experiment was highly reliable, participants with low 419 

performance were excluded (see ‘Experimental procedure – Behavioral memory experiment’ and 420 

Supplementary Fig. 12 ). The final analysis included data from 122 participants. Participants in the 421 

behavioral memory experiment received monetary compensation. 422 

For the perceptual similarity experiment, data were collected from 141 undergraduate students from 423 

the University of Oregon Human Subjects pool (95 female, mean age = 19.04, range = 18 – 27 years) 424 

that completed the experiment online through the Sona system. Informed consent was obtained, 425 

online, for each participant prior to the experiment. There were two versions of the perceptual 426 

similarity experiment: one that only included images of beaches, and another with images of 427 

gazebos. Of the 141 participants, 71 participated in the beach experiment and 70 participated in the 428 

gazebo experiment. To ensure that the perceptual similarity measure from this experiment was 429 

reliable, participants with an insufficient amount of data and/or low performance were excluded (see 430 

‘Experimental procedure – Perceptual similarity experiment’). The final analysis included data from 431 

113 participants—57 for the beach experiment and 56 for the gazebo experiment. Participants in the 432 

perceptual similarity experiment were compensated with study credit. 433 

For the fMRI experiment, 53 participants (42 female, mean age = 21.23, range = 18 – 31 years) 434 

completed the experiment with a target sample of at least 50. Because relevant effect sizes were not 435 

available (for the effects of interest here), we did not perform a formal sample size calculation. 436 

Rather, the goal was simply for the experiment to be well powered. Written informed consent was 437 

collected for each participant prior to the experiment. One participant’s data was excluded due to a 438 

technical error in the MRI acquisition. All analyses of fMRI data are based on 52 participants. 439 

Participants in the fMRI experiment received monetary compensation. 440

Experimental procedure 457 

Behavioral memory experiment 458 

The behavioral memory experiment, conducted online (using PsychoPy’s online platform, Pavlovia), 459 

involved learning scene-object associations. It consisted of a training phase followed by a post-test. 460 

Across all phases, stimuli were presented on a gray background on a computer screen. The 461 

experiment was implemented in PsychoPy2024.1.5 and lasted for about 35 minutes. The training 462 

phase consisted of 3 study-test rounds and was most similar to the low training condition of the fMRI 463 

experiment (described below). In each study/test round, all 48 scene-object associations were 464 

studied/tested once, in random order. The assignment of objects to scenes was random for each 465 

participant but was stable throughout the experiment. For each trial of the study rounds, a scene and 466 

its associated object were displayed next to each other on the screen for 1500 ms (scene on left, 467 

object on right), with a white fixation cross in between. Study trials were separated by a 1000 ms 468 

inter-trial interval during which a fixation cross was displayed. For each trial of the test rounds, a 469 

scene (probe) was displayed at the top of the screen, with 3 objects presented underneath. One of 470 

the objects was the target (the object that was paired with the probe during the study rounds). One 471 

object was the competitor (an object that was paired with a scene from the same category as the 472 

probe). One object was the non-competitor (an object that was paired with a scene from the 473 

opposite category as the probe). In each test round, each object appeared once as the target, once 474 

as the competitor, and once as the non-competitor. For each scene, the competitor and non-475 

competitor objects were randomly and independently selected in each round. Participants had 10 s 476 

to select one of the objects using the keyboard. After making a response, or after 10 s elapsed, the 477 

trial ended. Test trials were separated by a 1000 ms inter-trial interval during which a fixation cross 478 

was displayed. 479 

The post-test consisted of three rounds, during which each of the 48 studied scenes was tested once 480 

per round, in random order. On each post-test trial, a scene (probe) was displayed at the top of the 481 

screen, with 24 objects displayed below (3 rows x 8 columns). One of the 24 objects (target) had been 482 

paired with the probe. The other 23 objects (competitors) had been paired with a scene from the 483 

same category as the probe. Participants were instructed to use their trackpad or mouse to select 484 

the object that had been paired with the scene. Participants had 30 seconds to respond on each trial. 485 

After responding, or after 30 s elapsed, the trial ended. Post-test trials were separated by a 500 ms 486 

inter-trial interval during which a fixation cross was displayed. Post-test data were used to generate 487 

a confusability matrix (see ‘Metrics of Similarity,’ below). The rationale for testing each scene three 488 

times during the post-test was to obtain a more reliable confusability matrix (for validation, see 489 

Supplementary Fig. 1c ). 490 

Because the behavioral memory experiment was used to establish the normative confusability of 491 

scene pairs, it was important that the data were internally reliable. Thus, separately for each scene 492 

category, we excluded participants whose mean accuracy across the three post-test rounds was 493 

below 28%. While this threshold was well above chance (4.17%), we selected this threshold to 494 

optimize split-half reliability of the memory confusability measure. 495

Perceptual similarity experiment 497 

The perceptual similarity experiment, conducted online (using PsychoPy’s online platform, Pavlovia), 498 

involved making subjective judgments about the relative similarity of scene images. It was modeled 499 

after the triplet odd-one-out task described in 20 . Stimuli were presented on a gray background on a 500 

computer screen. The experiment was implemented in PsychoPy2024.1.4 and each session lasted 501 

approximately 15 minutes. In short, participants completed a single task in which three scenes were 502 

shown, and participants select the ‘odd one out’ (the scene that was least similar to the other two 503 

scenes). Each participant in the experiment made perceptual similarity judgments for one of the two 504 

visual categories (beach experiment or gazebo experiment). For each experiment (beach or gazebo), 505 

participants were allowed to enroll in a second session (for the same category) to be completed 506 

within three days of the first session for additional credit. The second session presented triplets that 507 

were not shown in the first session. A minimum two-hour break between sessions was enforced. For 508 

each category, because there were 24 scenes, there were 2,024 possible triplets. For each 509 

participant, the sample of triplets was randomly and independently chosen. On each trial, one of the 510 

2,024 triplets was randomly selected (without replacement within participants). The corresponding 511 

three images were presented equidistantly on a virtual circle. On each trial, the image array was 512 

randomly rotated by 1–360 degrees to prevent predictable locations and discourage habitual 513 

responses. This prevented participants from repeatedly selecting the same screen location without 514 

evaluating the presented options. Participants selected the odd image by clicking on it with the 515 

trackpad or mouse. There was no time limit for responses. Trials were separated by a 500 ms inter-516 

trial interval during which a fixation cross was displayed. Each session lasted 15 minutes with a self-517 

terminated break after every 50 trials. On average, each participant completed a total of 339 trials 518 

per session (SD = 106.0) in the beach experiment and 314 trials per session (SD = 73.4) in the gazebo 519 

experiment. 5% of trials were ‘catch trials’ in which two of the scenes were identical. The catch trials 520 

were used to identify participants responding randomly. Participants were not informed about the 521 

presence of catch trials. Participants were excluded if their accuracy on catch trials (selecting the 522 

objectively different image) was lower than 80%. Data from the perceptual similarity experiment 523 

were used to generate a perceptual similarity matrix (see ‘Metrics of Similarity,’ below). The 80% 524 

threshold for exclusion was chosen to optimize split-half reliability of the perceptual similarity matrix. 525

with 6 new ‘lure’ scenes (3 beaches, 3 gazebos). The 54 scenes were presented one at a time, in 562 

random order. The lures never repeated across scan runs. Thus, the exposure phase contained a 563 

total of 6 (lures) * 8 (runs) = 48 lures. Participants’ only task during the exposure phase was to make 564 

a button press using their index finger when they detected a new scene—i.e., one not presented 565 

during the study phase. Performance was evaluated in a signal detection framework, with button 566 

presses for objectively new scenes treated as ‘hits’ and button presses for objectively old scenes 567 

treated as ‘false alarms.’ On each trial, a scene was displayed for 2000 ms, followed by 4000 ms 568 

inter-trial interval during which a white fixation cross was displayed. Participants were instructed to 569 

respond (if relevant) by the end of the fixation period. Participants were not instructed or encouraged 570 

to practice recalling the object associations during the exposure phase and they were not made 571 

aware that scene-object memory would be tested again in a post-test. By design, the exposure phase 572 

had minimal behavioral demands to resolve interference. The rationale for this was to minimize 573 

potentially confounding effects of behavioral responses/decisions. Instead, the exposure phase was 574 

intended to capture consequences of prior learning (training) and stimulus similarity. 575 

The post-test consisted of a single round in which each of the 48 studied scenes was tested once, in 576 

random order. On each post-test trial, a scene (probe) was displayed at the top of the screen, with 577 

24 objects displayed below (3 rows x 8 columns). As in the behavioral memory experiment, one of 578 

the 24 objects was the target, and the other 23 objects were competitors. Participants were 579 

instructed to use the trackpad to select the object that had been paired with the scene. Participants 580 

had 99 seconds to respond on each trial. After responding, or after 99 s elapsed, the trial ended. 581 

Post-test trials were separated by a 500 ms inter-trial interval during which a fixation cross was 582 

displayed. 583

Memory confusability 593 

Responses from the post-test of the behavioral memory experiment were used to generate a 594 

normative memory confusability matrix. After excluding participants based on performance 595 

thresholds (see ‘Experimental procedure – Behavioral memory experiment’), a matrix was generated 596 

for each participant that reflected the pairwise confusability for each pair of scenes within a category. 597 

For example, in a post-test round, when presented with beach 3 during the post-test, if participants 598 

selected the object that had been paired with beach 7 (see Fig. 1c , left), this would be considered a 599 

‘confusion’ between beach 3 and beach 7 (coded as ‘1’ in the confusability matrix for that round, see 600 

Fig. 2b ). For each participant, the confusion matrices over the 3 post-test rounds were averaged, 601 

constituting the confusion matrix for that participant. For example, if beach 3 was confused with 602 

beach 7 in 2 out of the 3 post-test rounds, that element of the confusability matrix would be 0.667. 603 

Participant-level matrices were then averaged. Because the resulting distribution of confusability 604 

values was skewed (many pairs were rarely confused, resulting in a left-skew), a log transformation 605 

was applied, yielding a distribution of confusability values that was approximately normal. 606

Perceptual similarity 608 

Responses from the perceptual similarity experiment were used to generate a normative perceptual 609 

similarity matrix. Similarity for a given pair of scenes (e.g., beach 2, beach 7 similarity) was defined 610 

as the proportion of trials, when beach 2 and beach 7 were presented together, on which the other 611 

scene (the third scene) was selected as the ‘odd one out’. In other words, the proportion of trials 612 

when the ‘other’ scene was selected reflects the similarity of a given pair of scenes relative to other 613 

possible pairs of scenes. For each participant, a similarity matrix was generated where each cell 614 

represented the proportion of ‘other’ responses. After performance-based exclusion of participants 615 

(see the details in ‘Experimental procedure – Perceptual similarity experiment’), similarity matrices 616 

were averaged across participants. Because the resulting distribution of similarity values was 617 

skewed (many pairs had low/zero similarity, resulting in a left-skew), a log transformation was 618 

applied, yielding a distribution of similarity values that was approximately normal. 619

Dimensions of Similarity 684 

For each of the 10 similarity metrics described above, we computed pairwise similarity for all 276 685 

pairs of beaches and 276 pairs of gazebos, yielding 552 similarity values in total. We then 686 

constructed a 552 × 10 matrix, where each row corresponded to a scene pair and each column to a 687 

similarity metric. Principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to this matrix, yielding 10 688 

orthogonal dimensions of similarity, with each scene pair assigned a score on each dimension (PC 689 

score). 690 

Our rationale for applying PCA to the set of 10 similarity matrices (described above) was that 691 

different metrics are likely to capture shared variance. As such, similarity may be better explained 692 

by a few underlying dimensions than by individual metrics. We primarily focus on the first two 693 

dimensions (PC1 and PC2) because (1) by definition, they explained the most variance and (2) they 694 

strongly predicted memory interference (Fig. 2c ). Importantly, PC1 and PC2 were each 695 

characterized by positive loadings from several metrics, with the loadings for other metrics closer to 696 

0 ( Fig. 1i ). Based on the specific metrics that loaded positively on each PC, we interpreted them as 697 

reflecting perceptual similarity (PC1) and semantic similarity (PC2). To validate this interpretation, 698 

we submitted the PC loadings to ChatGPT using the following prompt: 699 

‘Here are the loadings from the10 metrics of similarity. Please tell me what these two PCs of 700 

similarity capture in just one word/term.’ 701 

ChatGPT’s response was: 702 

‘I would characterize them roughly as: PC1: Perceptual-structural similarity PC2: Semantic-703 

conceptual similarity or even shorter: PC1: Perceptual PC2: Semantic.’ 704 

Thus, although our approach to identifying dimensions of similarity was entirely data driven (i.e., we 705 

did not impose a priori predictions), the resulting dimensions (PC1, PC2) are generally consistent 706 

with perceptual vs. semantic similarity, respectively. It is important to note that other dimensions 707 

(e.g., PC4), were characterized by a mix of positive and negative loadings (see Supplementary 708 

Table 1 ). 709

fMRI pattern similarity scores 798 

fMRI pattern similarity was defined as the Pearson correlation between t-stats maps from a given 799 

ROI. All correlations were restricted to t-maps from different scan runs; that is, correlations were 800 

never computed between t-maps (trials) from the same scan run. Correlations were performed for 801 

all pairwise comparisons of runs and then averaged. For example, to compute the pattern similarity 802 

between beach 4 and beach 2 within a given ROI, 56 Pearson correlations were computed, reflecting 803 

all possible combinations across different scan runs (e.g., beach 4–run 1 vs. beach 2–run 2, beach 804 

4–run 3 vs. beach 2–run 7, etc.). The 56 correlation coefficients were then Fisher z-transformed and 805 

averaged to yield a single similarity value for that scene pair. 806

similarity, e.g., beach 4 vs. beach 2) was always expressed relative to the mean pattern similarity 808 

between all scenes from opposite categories (across-category similarity). The difference between 809 

these measures (within-category minus across-category) is referred to as the similarity score. Thus, 810 

if a given pair of images from the same category (or a set of images from the same category) has a 811

Statistical analyses and reproducibility 818 

Split-half reliability for human-based similarity metrics 819 

For human-based measures of memory confusability and perceptual similarity, we conducted 820 

reliability analyses. Specifically, split-half reliability was computed separately for each category 821 

(beaches, gazebos) as a function of the number of participants included (participant count). For 822 

each participant count, 50 random permutations were generated by selecting that number of 823 

participants and randomly dividing them into two equal groups. For each participant group, 824 

confusability/similarity matrices were averaged across participants. Reliability was defined as the 825 

Pearson’s correlation (r) between the matrices for each group. To compute the overall reliability 826 

across all 50 permutations, correlation values were first converted to Fisher’s z, then averaged, and 827 

then converted back to r (for interpretability). 828

β(PC2 × round) = 0 for the behavioral memory experiment). 904

Relationships between experience, similarity dimensions, and the hippocampus 906 

To test the overall effects of training on fMRI similarity scores for each ROI ( Fig. 4b ), we used linear 907 

mixed-effects models where the dependent measure was the mean fMRI similarity score, and fixed 908 

effects included training level (high vs. low) and the participant’s designated high-training category 909 

(beaches vs. gazebos). Note: these models were based on mean responses per 910 

participant/condition (i.e., they did not include trial-level data). In addition, to test whether an ROI’s 911 

average similarity score was above zero, two-tailed one-sample t-tests (contrast value = zero) were 912 

conducted. 913 

To test whether fMRI similarity scores within each ROI were related to dimensions of visual similarity 914 

(PC1, PC2 for Fig. 4c-d and Supplementary Fig. 11 ; all 10 PCs for Supplementary Table 3 ) and 915 

training level (low vs. high), we ran a separate set of linear mixed-effects models. In these models, 916 

the dependent measure was the fMRI similarity score for every pairwise combination of scenes from 917 

the same category (baselined against the mean across-category similarity). Specifically, for each 918 

participant, the model included 552 fMRI similarity values, reflecting every pairwise combination of 919 

beaches (276 total) and gazebos (276 total). Fixed effects included PC scores (continuous 920 

predictors), training level (low vs. high), and the high-training scene category for each participant 921 

(beaches vs. gazebos). 922 

To further support the argument that repulsion within CA3/DG was a direct reaction to memory 923 

interference, we implemented another mixed-effects model that predicted similarity scores in 924 

CA3/DG, but instead of using PC1 and PC2 scores as fixed effects, we used similarity values taken 925 

directly from the two behavioral similarity experiments ( Fig. 1c ): the behavioral memory experiment 926 

and the perceptual similarity experiment ( Supplementary Fig. 9 ). In these models, the dependent 927 

measure was CA3/DG similarity scores (552 values per participant) and fixed effects included 928 

perceptual similarity and memory confusability values (continuous predictors), training level (low vs. 929 

high), and the high-training scene category for each participant (beaches vs. gazebos). 930 

Finally, for each ROI and each scene category, we tested the extent to which the representational 931 

structure after high training preserved vs. transformed the representational structure after low 932 

training (independent of any similarity metric or PC scores) ( Supplementary Fig. 10 ). Because the 933 

assignment of each scene category to each training condition was counterbalanced across 934 

participants , this analysis required across-participant alignment. Specifically, for each participant, 935 

pairwise similarity scores in the high training condition were treated as the dependent measure 936 

whereas similarity scores in the low training condition from other participants were the critical 937 

predictor (fixed effect). For example, for a given participant, if the high training category was beaches, 938 

then the dependent measure for that participant would be the 276 pairwise similarity scores for 939 

beaches. To obtain the corresponding similarity scores for the low training condition, the mean 940 

similarity score (for each of the 276 pairs of beaches) was computed from all participants (N = 26) 941 

for which the low training category was beaches. Thus, participant-specific and category-specific 942 

representational structure in the high training condition was considered as a function of normative 943 

representational structure in the low training condition. We refer to this normative measure as the 944 

‘normative low-training fMRI similarity scores’. For the mixed-effects models, the high-training 945 

category (beaches vs gazebos) was also included as a fixed effect. 946

Supplementary Fig. 9 | Effects of memory confusability and perceptual similarity on CA3/DG similarity scores. For the two human-based measures of similarity (memory confusability and perceptual similarity judgments), we tested for relationships with similarity scores in CA3/DG. We ran a linear mixed-effects model with pairwise CA3/DG similarity scores (552 pairs per participant) as the dependent variable.
