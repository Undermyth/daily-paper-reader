## Abstract 

We modeled shared representational spaces in macaque brains using fMRI data from 12 rhesus monkeys obtained during movie-viewing and response hyperalignment (RHA). RHA greatly improved inter-subject correlation of movie response time-series in occipital, temporal, and prefrontal cortices and afforded highly accurate between-subject decoding of movie time-points and estimation of idiosyncratic functional topographies. These results reveal shared high-dimensional, high capacity representational spaces encoded in idiosyncratic fine- and coarse-grained functional topographies.

## Main 

We used fMRI data collected during naturalistic stimulation in macaques and response hyperalignment (RHA) to model shared representational spaces for information encoded in fine-scale patterns of response and to predict idiosyncratic coarse-scale category-selective topographies. 

Information is encoded in neural population responses, and these responses can be decoded in 

monkeys from recordings of multiple single units with pattern classifiers 1,2  or representational 

similarity analysis 3,4 . Population responses also can be decoded from fMRI data in both 

humans 5,6  and monkeys 7,8 , indicating that the cell response tuning profiles that underlie 

decodable population responses have a fine-grained topographic structure. that affords decoding of response patterns within the confines of areas defined by category selectivity, such 

as the monkey face patches or human face and place areas 5,9–11 . At the same time, cortical 

functional topography also has a coarse-scale structure that separates different sensory, motor, 

and cognitive functions into large areas, pathways, and systems 12,13 . The fine-scale 

topographies cannot be aligned across brains based on anatomical features. Consequently, decoding is usually done within individual brains, and results are aggregated across brains as mean accuracies, leaving unanswered the question of whether the population codes that underlie accurate decoding have a common structure. 

RHA models the shared information embedded in idiosyncratic fine-scale functional 

topographies 10,14–17 . RHA first derives a common model space that has an anatomical structure 

in which each common model dimension is a location in a high resolution model of cortex. The model derivation is based on neural response patterns to naturalistic stimuli (usually a movie) to capture neural representations of a broad range of stimuli and cognitive processes. In a second

RHA is a flexible and multifaceted computational environment that was developed to model fine-scale topographies in human fMRI data but has broader application. Resampling vertex time-series responses into the common model space greatly improves inter-subject correlation of response and connectivity profiles and enhances between-subject decoding accuracy. RHA has also been applied to single-unit recordings in the rat hippocampus to derive a common 

space for representations of navigation 18 . Interestingly, RHA also affords high-fidelity modeling 

of coarse-scale category-selective and retinotopic topographies by projecting fMRI localizer data 

from other brains into an index brain 14,15,19,20 .

We applied response RHA to macaque fMRI data collected in 12 macaques during naturalistic movie viewing — the commercial movie, Monkey Kingdom . Using movie responses to the first part of the movie as training data, we built a common representational space and estimated subject-specific transformations to align individual brains into that space. We used these transformation matrices to hyperalign responses to the second part of the movie (test data) and tested whether HA improved inter-subject correlation of movie-evoked responses, enhanced between-subject decoding of movie time-points, and enabled prediction of individual functional topographies from other macaques’ functional localizer data. 

Compared to anatomical alignment (AA), RHA increased inter-subject correlation (ISC) across most of the cortex (Fig. 1a). Mean ISC across all cortical vertices increased from 0.253 to 0.496. The strongest gains were observed in occipital, temporal, and lateral prefrontal cortices (Fig. 1a), showing that RHA improved alignment not only in visual areas but also in higher order

We tested whether we could decode which part of the movie a macaque was watching by comparing the patterns of response in individuals to the mean patterns in other macaques. We performed between-subject classification of time-points (2 s intervals) using both whole-brain and searchlight patterns. We used nearest neighbor classification based on correlation distance. For a correct classification, the correlation between an individual macaque’s pattern for a single time-point with the mean pattern of response to the same time-point in other macaques is higher than correlations between that time-point in the index macaque and all other time-points in the other macaques. Chance performance is 1/900 (<0.0012). For whole-brain decoding we calculated the principal components of response patterns and used the first ten to 300 principal components. We tested whether HA could be used to predict coarse-scale individual functional topographies from other macaques’ data. Visual category functional localizer data were available for four of the macaques from independent studies using still images of faces, bodies, and objects. We used pairwise hyperalignment transforms estimated from movie responses to project category-selective localizer maps (face-versus-object and bodies-versus-object) from other macaques into the cortical anatomy of each of these four macaques. 

Different macaques showed similar but, nonetheless distinctively idiosyncratic topographies based on functional localizers for visual category-selectivity (Fig. 3a, left). The predicted maps recovered individual-specific category-selective topographies (Fig. 3a, right), including face-patches such as ML/MF and AL (Supp. Fig. S1). To quantify recovery of individual-specific topographies, we calculated correlations between each individual’s measured and predicted category-selective maps in inferior temporal (IT) cortex (congruent maps) and compared these to correlations between each individual’s maps and the predicted maps for other individuals (incongruent maps). For each macaque and both localizer maps, congruent predictions were consistently higher than incongruent predictions (Fig. 3b). These results show that the RHA transformation matrices, based on responses to a naturalistic movie, also successfully model idiosyncrasies of coarse-scale category-selective response topographies with high fidelity.

In addition to modeling shared information encoded in fine-scale topographies, RHA of macaque movie data also modeled idiosyncrasies of coarser-scale, visual category-selective topographies. 

In sum, our results show that shared information in high-dimensional and high-capacity cortical representational spaces in the macaque brain can be measured with fMRI using naturalistic stimulation and modeled with RHA. These representational spaces modeled with RHA captured both shared information encoded in idiosyncratic fine-scale topographies and individual differences in coarser-scale functional topographies. Similar results have been reported in 

human fMRI studies using hyperalignment 10,14,15,17,19,20,24 , and the parallel human analyses in this 

study showed the same overall pattern using data obtained while humans watch the same movie that the macaques watched (see Supp. Figs. S2 and S3), indicating that nonhuman and human primates both have high-dimensional and high-capacity cortical representational spaces that can be revealed with naturalistic fMRI and modeled with RHA.

## Methods 

Datasets & Preprocessing 

12 macaques were scanned at the KU Leuven using a 3T Siemens PRISMA scanner. 8 macaques were scanned at a spatial resolution of 1.25*1.25*1.25mm³ with a TR = 1s, while 4 macaques were scanned at a spatial resolution of 0.8*0.8*0.8mm³ with a TR = 2s. To enhance the contrast-to-noise ratio, macaques were administered 8-11 mg/kg monocrystalline iron oxide nanoparticles (MION, BioPAL) immediately prior to scanning. All macaques watched the movie 

Monkey Kingdom in five continuous clips in Spanish (900 seconds per clip), with each macaque viewing the movie an average of eight times in the scanner. To match the temporal resolution across datasets, the fMRI time-series for each clip were resampled to 450 time-points. Face-and body-selective localizer data were also collected for 4 of the 12 macaques. Macaque cortical surface reconstruction was performed using a revised version of the NHPpipeline (Glasser et al., 2013), based on high-resolution T1-weighted (0.4mm) and, when available, T2-weighted (0.4mm) anatomical images. Initial brain and white-matter masks were generated using nBEST and then manually refined. Macaque fMRI data were preprocessed using in-house scripts, including skull stripping, slice-timing correction, and motion correction. The preprocessed macaque fMRI data were then mapped to the mkavg-ico32 cortical surface template, which was generated from the cortical surfaces of all 12 macaques. 

24 humans were scanned at Dartmouth using a 3T Siemens scanner equipped with a 32-channel phased-array head coil. Functional BOLD images were acquired at a spatial resolution of 2.5*2.5*2.5mm³ with a volume size of 96*96*52 and TR = 1s. All subjects watched the movie Monkey Kingdom in 5 continuous clips (900 seconds per clip) once outside the scanner in English to ensure that they understood the storyline and once inside the scanner in Spanish to reduce the influence of language comprehension on cross-species comparison.

Human fMRI data were preprocessed using fMRIPrep 20.2.7 (Esteban et al., 2019) and resampled to the onavg-ico32 cortical surface template (Feilong et al., 2024). 

The same denoising procedure was applied to both human and macaque fMRI data. Nuisance regressors included six head-motion parameters and their temporal derivatives, framewise displacement, 6 principal components extracted from a combined cerebrospinal fluid and white matter mask (aCompCor), and polynomial trends up to the second order. 

Response Hyperalignment 

We used response hyperalignment (RHA) to model shared information by projecting individual brain responses into a common model space using movie data. Whole-brain templates for both macaques and humans were built from the first 3 movie clips using a searchlight-based algorithm that finds shared information via a singular value decomposition of group data followed by rotating that information with the Procrustes transformation into the central tendency of topographic distribution (Feilong et al., 2023). There were 19,863 overlapping macaque searchlights (mean size: 167 vertices) and 19,341 human searchlights (mean size: 121 vertices). For each subject, a transformation matrix was estimated with ridge regression using the same three clips and applied to the 2 held-out clips to project data into the common space. To evaluate HA performance, we performed two analyses on anatomically aligned and hyperaligned test movie-viewing data: inter-subject correlation (ISC) of response time-series and movie time-point prediction. We further tested functional map prediction by calculating pairwise RHA transformation matrices from all movie data in the four macaques with localizer data and using these transformations to project other macaques’ localizer data into each left-out index macaque’s anatomical space.

Inter-subject Correlation 

To evaluate the cross-subject similarity in movie responses, we computed vertex wise inter-subject correlation (ISC). For each vertex, we calculated the Pearson correlation between the response time-series of each subject and the average time-series of all other subjects, where higher ISC indicates better performance. ISC was computed for both anatomically aligned data and hyperaligned data and then compared across cortex. 

Movie time-point prediction 

Using leave-one-subject-out cross-validation, we performed whole-brain time-point prediction in two steps. First, we applied Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to the training subjects to reduce dimensionality. Second, we predicted each time-point in the left-out subject as the nearest-neighbor mean response pattern in the training subjects. Across all training subjects, we identified the number of principal components that yielded the highest classification accuracy, and then used these components in the left-out test subject for prediction. 

To determine which brain regions contributed most to prediction performance, we also ran searchlight-based classification using the same searchlight definitions as in the HA analysis. Time-point classification was computed separately within each searchlight and then averaged across subjects. Accuracy for each searchlight was assigned to that searchlight’s center vertex to produce whole-brain maps of mean prediction accuracy. 

Functional map prediction 

For each of the 4 macaques with visual category localizer data, we computed pairwise HA transforms from each of the other 3 macaques to the target macaque using all 5 movie clips. We then projected each of the other 3 macaque’s localizer data into the target macaque’s space to generate predicted category selective maps and averaged the three predictions. Prediction
