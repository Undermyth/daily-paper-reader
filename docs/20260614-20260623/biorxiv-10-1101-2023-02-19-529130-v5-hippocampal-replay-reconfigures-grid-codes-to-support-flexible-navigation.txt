Abstract 14 

The brain's cognitive map relies on entorhinal grid cells, whose hexagonal firing provides a metric 15 for spatial and abstract knowledge, and on hippocampal replay, the compressed reactivation of 16 experience that supports learning and planning. Whether hippocampal replay contributes to the 17 organization of the entorhinal grid metric remains unresolved. Here we recorded 18 magnetoencephalography during a direction-resolved navigation task that yielded 1,800 trial-19 unique events per participant, allowing replay and entorhinal grid-like activity to be estimated 20 event-wise across the full 360°. Reverse and forward replay emerged early in each navigation 21 event, followed by a six-fold entorhinal grid code. Replay was anchored to the grid axes, with 22 reverse replay time-locked to the grid orientation expressed early in the grid-coding window and 23 forward replay to that expressed later. Notably, forward and reverse replay encoded the two 24 opposite directions (180° apart) of each grid axis, together spanning the full six-fold structure. A 25 continuous-attractor network reproduced this organization, with grid-axis replay yielding 26 significantly higher gridness than simulations without replay. Finally, navigation behaviour 27 corroborated these findings. Movement speed showed two-fold directional periodicity before grid-28 like activity emerged, peaking at two opposite directions (180° apart) that mirrored the opposite 29 tuning of forward and reverse replay, whereas trajectory deviation showed two-fold periodicity 30 after grid-like activity emerged and aligned with the grid orientation, suggesting that the grid 31 orientation rotates to guide heading toward the goal. 32 33

Introduction 34 

The internal map of space in the mammalian brain rests on two phenomena of the hippocampal-35 entorhinal system. In the hippocampus (HPC), place cells fire at specific locations and are 36 reactivated in temporally compressed sequences, termed replay, in rodents and humans (Foster & 37 Wilson, 2006; Diba & Buzsáki, 2007; Vaz et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2019). Replay carries distinct 38 functional roles. Forward replay supports prospective, model-based decision-making (Wimmer et 39 al., 2023; McFadyen et al., 2023) and reshapes abstract cognitive maps offline (Son et al., 2024), 40 whereas reverse replay re-enacts recently experienced trajectories and propagates value to support 41 reward-based learning (Foster & Wilson, 2006; Gillespie et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2021). In the 42 entorhinal cortex (EC), grid cells fire at the vertices of a hexagonal lattice and provide a metric 43 that integrates discrete locations into a continuous map (Hafting et al., 2005). This metric 44 generalises beyond physical space to conceptual and even olfactory dimensions (Constantinescu 45 et al., 2016; Bao et al., 2019). Although both phenomena are central to spatial cognition, how the 46 hexagonal grid metric is constructed and stabilised, and whether HPC replay participates in 47 organising it, remains unresolved. 48 Converging evidence indicates that the HPC shapes the entorhinal grid pattern. The grid 49 pattern is not self-sustaining and requires continuous excitatory drive from the hippocampus. 50 Reducing hippocampal theta input degrades grid periodicity (Koenig et al., 2011), and inactivating 51 the HPC abolishes grid firing (Bonnevie et al., 2013). Replay is a principal form of hippocampal 52 output, observed not only during rest and sleep but also during active, awake behaviour (Foster & 53 Wilson, 2006; Vaz et al., 2020), and the entorhinal grid network also participates in these 54 reactivation events. During rest, entorhinal and hippocampal populations replay coherently, with 55 the HPC leading by approximately 11 ms (Ólafsdóttir et al., 2016), and superficial entorhinal layers 56

A developmental dissociation points in the 57 same direction, as preconfigured hippocampal sequences are available early (Dragoi & Tonegawa, 58 2011) whereas grid cells mature only after place and head-direction cells (Wills et al., 2010). In 59 humans, hippocampal ripples during post-learning rest predict the later emergence of grid-like 60 activity in EC and prefrontal cortex (Xiao et al., 2025). These relationships derive largely from 61 offline states; whether replay is organised with respect to the geometry of the grid axes, and 62 whether such coordination already arises during active navigation, has not been examined. 63 The grid axes are not arbitrary. Within an individual they share a common orientation that 64 is anchored to environmental geometry and preserved across environments (Hafting et al., 2005; 65 Fyhn et al., 2007; Krupic et al., 2015; Stensola et al., 2015), and both entorhinal activity and 66 navigation are preferentially organised along them, with conjunctive grid-by-direction cells tuned 67 to the grid axes and movement along the axes evoking the strongest population activity (Sargolini 68 et al., 2006; Doeller et al., 2010). Whether replay is likewise organised along these axes, rather 69 than only along the path just travelled, has not been examined. We reasoned that, if replay 70 contributes to the grid metric, it would be anchored to these same axes, and we further asked 71 whether forward and reverse replay play complementary geometric roles. Each grid axis comprises 72 two opposite directions. Forward and reverse replay of a directed movement run in opposite 73 directions (Foster & Wilson, 2006; Diba & Buzsáki, 2007), so when each navigation event isolates 74 a single direction, grid-axis-anchored replay would partition into two complementary half-lattices, 75 with forward and reverse replay each selecting one direction per axis, 180° apart, and together 76 tiling the full six-fold structure. A way 87 forward is offered by the observation that entorhinal grid-like activity is engaged not only during 88 locomotion but also during visual and gaze-based exploration of space (Julian et al., 2018; Nau et 89 al., 2018; Wilming et al., 2018; Staudigl et al., 2018). Hippocampal replay activity aligns with the later entorhinal grid orientation 156 

The temporal succession of replay activity (reverse: 100–240 ms; forward: 360–620 ms; Fig. 1F) 157 followed by EC six-fold activity (500–1000 ms; Fig. 1J) raised the question of whether 158 hippocampal replay events are spatially anchored to the entorhinal grid metric that emerges later. 159 To address this question, we first asked whether the two types of activity are spatially aligned at 160 the level of direction. In doing so, replay strength was estimated for each of 120 directions (Fig. 161 2A, II), giving each connection a direction-by-direction replay profile. The direction label of replay 162 activity was then realigned to the participant’s grid orientation φ. Every direction was shifted by 163 −φ and grid-aligned directions (multiples of 60° from φ) and grid-misaligned directions (offsets of 164 30°) became comparable across participants (Fig. 2A, I). Next, for each replay type, replay strength 165 was contrasted between grid-aligned and grid-misaligned directions (Fig. 2A, III). Given that the 166 EC grid periodicity emerges after replay (Fig. 1F), the grid-orientation estimation window cannot 167 be assumed to coincide with the replay analysis window; we therefore tested all combinations: 168 each replay time-point was realigned using the grid orientation extracted at every grid-orientation 169 time-point (Fig. 2A, IV). 170

We next asked whether a six-fold periodicity could be detected in replay activity. Grid-219 orientation–realigned forward and reverse replay were analysed independently with an FFT, and 220 spectral power at folds 0–6 was tested at each time point. We predicted that (i) for forward and 221 reverse replay considered separately, this analysis would reveal significant one- and three-fold 222 power but no six-fold power (since each replay type was associated with only three of the six grid-223 aligned directions), and (ii) averaging forward and reverse replay would instead yield six-fold 224 power, with the one- and three-fold components cancelling out. 225 Both forward and reverse replay reached significance at one-fold and three-fold (Fig. 3D, 226 top and middle; initial threshold p < 0.05, cluster-based permutation p < 0.05, one-tailed). Forward replay 229 showed an additional later three-fold cluster at 630–670 ms. Empirically constrained grid-axis replay stabilizes entorhinal grid formation 251 

The empirical findings established that hippocampal replay events are anchored to entorhinal grid 252 axes and that forward and reverse replay share the same hexagonal axes in opposite directions. To 253 test whether this empirically observed replay pattern supports stable grid emergence, we ran a 254 continuous-attractor network (CAN) simulation. 255 We adopted a CAN implementation that generates grid-cell firing through path-integration 256 input (Fuhs and Touretzky, 2006). The network comprised 101 × 101 grid-cell units, with spatial 257 frequency ω determined by the target grid scale σ. To simulate the time compression of empirical 258 replay, the replay-input amplitude was scaled relative to the motion-direction input amplitude 259 (Supplementary Table S1). 260

Discussion 355 

The present study characterized hippocampal replay and entorhinal six-fold grid coding within a 356 single human MEG task that spanned the full directional space, and found that replay is 357 geometrically organized along the grid axes, with forward and reverse replay unfolding along the 358 two opposite directions of each grid axis. In a continuous-attractor network model, replay directed 359 along the grid axes produced more robust grid patterns, with significantly higher gridness, than 360 replay along the movement axis or without replay, indicating that grid-axis replay supports stable 361 grid formation. These findings were further supported by navigation behaviour. Both movement 362 speed and trajectory deviation exhibited two-fold directional periodicity, with their two preferred 363 directions separated by 180°, and the phase of deviation aligned with the grid orientation, 364 indicating that the grid orientation became aligned with the goal location toward the end of each 365 connection. Together, these results support the view that replay and grid coding are coordinated 366 not only in time but also in geometry. 367 Our central directional finding is that forward and reverse replay unfold along the two 368 opposite directions of the same grid axes. This extends to the directional domain previous reports 369 of temporal coordination between HPC and EC replay (Ólafsdóttir et al., 2016; O'Neill et al., 2017) 370 and of offline hippocampal ripples predicting later grid-like coding in humans (Xiao et al., 2025). 371 This organization points to an operation required for self-centred navigation. The HPC encodes 372 self-location through place cells (O'Keefe & Dostrovsky, 1971; O'Keefe & Nadel, 1978), the EC 373 provides a global metric through grid cells (Hafting et al., 2005; Moser et al., 2008), and flexible 374

Reverse replay (looking back toward the origin) and forward replay (looking ahead 376 toward the destination), engaged one after the other along the same metric axis, may be the neural 377 expression of this binding of self-location to the global metric. 378 Importantly, this 180° opposition does not imply that reverse and forward replay 379 selectively encode the 0–180° and 180–360° ranges, respectively, as the directional binning in Fig. 380 3A might suggest, for three reasons. First, the assignment of forward and reverse replay to opposite 381 halves was obtained after aligning to each participant's grid orientation, a reference that is blind to 382 the actual spatial direction during the connection. Second, the two replay types were characterized 383 at temporally separate windows, with reverse replay earlier (around 150 ms) and forward replay 384 later (around 500 ms); the 180° opposition therefore reflects a relationship between the two replay 385 types across these windows rather than a continuous relationship maintained throughout the 386 connection. Third, the absolute phases of forward and reverse replay were distributed near-387 uniformly across 0–360° (Fig. 3E), and only their difference was reliably clustered near 180° 388 across participants. Our analyses therefore do not support an absolute directional representation 389 for either forward or reverse replay, and only the 180° opposition between the two is interpretable. 390 Our results indicate a pathway from the HPC to the EC, in which hippocampal replay 391 activity participates in organizing entorhinal grid coding. This is consistent with the dependence 392 of grid firing on excitatory hippocampal drive (Bonnevie et al., 2013) and with the HPC leading 393 the EC by approximately 11 ms during replay (Ólafsdóttir et al., 2016). Classical and oscillatory-394 interference models, by contrast, emphasize the opposite direction, in which entorhinal grids 395 provide metric input to hippocampal place cells (Burgess et al., 2007). Because grid-like coding is also found in non-434 spatial two-dimensional spaces such as conceptual and olfactory spaces (Constantinescu et al., 435 2016; Bao et al., 2019), this organizing role of replay over grid geometry may generalize as a 436 mechanism for constructing abstract cognitive maps. In sum, hippocampal replay reconfigures the 437 entorhinal grid code to support flexible navigation. 438
