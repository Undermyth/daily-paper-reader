SUMMARY 16 

Turning left or right is a core element of animal navigation. Such turns occur in precise 17 

coordination with the ongoing stepping rhythm and require internal planning for the animal's future 18 

path. The superior colliculus (SC) is a midbrain structure involved in turning. Whether turn -related 19 

activity in the SC is coordinated with stepping and internal representations of future paths is 20 

unknown. Here , while recording from left - or right -preferring “turn cells ” in the motor layers of the 21 

SC as mice navigated a Y -maze, we monitored locomotor dynamics and decoded internal 22 

representations of future paths from the hippocampus. We discovered that turn cell activity was 23 

tightly phase -locked to the stepping rhythm and modulated in coordination with hippocampal 24 

representations of future paths. The coordination of turn -related activity in the SC with stepping 25 

and internal representations of future paths may allow animals to seamlessly execute turns during 26 

locomotion while navigating toward planned destinations. 27

INTRODUCTION 53 

An animal’s path through its environment is shaped by a series of left and right turns. These 54 

changes in locomotion direction are fundamental components of spatial navigation and goal -55 

directed behavior. While such turns unfold seamlessly along the animal’s trajectory, they are in 56 

fact precisely coordinated with the ongoing locomotor stepping rhythm and require internal 57 

planning for the animal’s future path. On one hand, coordinating turns with specific phases of the 58 

stepping cycle optimizes efficiency and stability during turning 1–3. On the other hand, internal 59 

planning for the animal’s future path ensures smooth and accurate trajectories during turns 4,5 .60 

Whether the activity of brain structures directly involved in turning is coordinated with both the 61 

stepping rhythm and internal representations of future paths is unknown. 62 

63 

Here, we tested this possibility in the superior colliculus (SC), a conserved sensorimotor midbrain 64 

structure that plays a central role in orienting movements 6,7 , like left and right turn s. Studies in 65 

rodents have shown that the intermediate and deep motor layers of the SC (dSC) contain neurons 66 

whose activity reports turn direction 8–12 and whose stimulation triggers turning movements 13,14 .67 

However, neuronal activity in the dSC has been primarily studied in movement -restricted animals 68 

performing turns in response to external stimuli 8,9 . Accordingly , little is known about how turn -69 

related activity in dSC unfolds during freely -moving , internally -generated behaviors , like spatial 70 

navigation 15 , when turns need to be coordinated with both stepping and internal representations 71 

of future paths .72 

73 

Is turn -related activity in the dSC modulated in coordination with the animal’s ongoing stepping 74 

rhythm and with internal representations of future paths? To address this question, we recorded 75 

from neurons in the dSC in navigating mice while tracking locomotor dynamics with a camera and 76 

decoding internal representations of future paths from hippocampal place cell activity 16 –18 .77 

78 

We discover that the activity of left - or right -preferring ‘turn cells’ in the dSC is tightly phase -locked 79 

to the ongoing locomotor stepping rhythm such that left and right turn cells fire at opposite phases 80 

of the stepping cycle . Notably, this alternation is already present during straight locomotion , before 81 

the animal initiates the turn . Furthermore, we reveal that turn cell activity is modulated in 82 

coordination with hippocampal representations of future paths. Specifically, hippocampal 83 

representations of possible left or right future paths predict increased firing rates of dSC neurons 84 

with matching turn preference. These results reveal how turn -related activity in the SC , perhaps 85 

one of the best -understood motor outputs of this structure, is highly integrated with the ongoing 86 

behavior and internal plans of the organism as it navigates through its world. 87

RESULTS 89 

We used silicon probes to record from dSC neurons (Fig ure 1A , top , and F igure S 1) as mice 90 

performed a Y -maze spatial navigation task (Fig ure 1A, middle). Each task session consisted of 91 

multiple trial blocks during which two of the three reward ports (located at the ends of the maze 92 

arms) were active, with their locations changing uncued across blocks. This trial block design was 93 

chosen to maintain cognitive engagement with the task. During each trial, the mouse navigated 94 

from one reward port to another and, across trials, consistently alternated between the active 95 

ports, resulting in repeated left and right turns at the maze bifurcation. We first identified neurons 96 

whose activity report ed turn direction around the bifurcation (‘turn cells’ ). We then then determined 97 

whether their activity was modulated in coordination with the animal’s stepping rhythm and with 98 

hippocampal representations of future paths. 99

Turn cells in the dSC 104 

Turn cells were defined as neurons that showed significant firing rate modulation around the 105 

bifurcation of the maze (significant Turn Direction Selectivity Index , or TDSI, see Methods), 106 

consistently in the same direction, regardless of which maze arm the animal came from (Figure 107 

1B and 1C) . Turn cells accounted for ~30% of all dSC cells ( 28%, 411 /1471 , n = 12 animals). 108 

Turn cell firing became directionally selective ~ 300 ms before the bifurcation ( -283 ± 221 ms, 109 

average ± s.d., Figure 1D, top ) and peaked around the bifurcation (+ 18 ± 145 ms, average ± s.d., 110 

Figure 1D, bottom ). Notably, half of the turn cells ( 51 %, 211 /411 ) became directionally selective 111 

prior to the time at which the animals’ behavior diverged between left and right turns ( Figure 1A, 112 

bottom ) in line with premotor activity previously reported in the dSC 10,11,13,19 –21 . Furthermore, 113 

consistent with the lateralization of the dSC hemispheres for left and right turns 10,11,19,20 , about two 114 

thirds of turn cells showed selective firing for contraversive turns relative to the recorded SC 115 

hemisphere (Figure 1E , contralateral (e.g., left turn cells in the right dSC) : 69 %, 283 /411 ,116 

ipsilateral (e.g., right turn cells in the right dSC) : 31 %, 128 /411 ). Together, the properties of the 117 

turn cells identified here are consistent with the reported characteristics of motor command 118 

neurons responsible for turns in the dSC 11 –13 .119

Turn cell activity is coupled to the stepping rhythm 121 

The coordination between turn direction and step ping phase is well established in humans 1,2 , but 122 

whether this also occurs in mice has not been verified. We therefore assessed the stepping phase 123 

of an animal as it performed left or right turns. We first characterized the stepping rhythm of mice 124 

on a transparent linear track using a bottom -view camera. As in other walking tetrapods 22 –24 , w ith 125 

each forepaw swing, the head shifted laterally such that a right forepaw swing was linked to a 126 

leftward head movement and vice versa, producing a left -right oscillation of the head during 127 

locomotion ( Figure 2A and Figure S2 ). This allowed us to infer the stepping rhythm of mice on the 128 

Y-maze by monitoring the left -right oscillation of the head with a top -view camera, quantifying this 129 

oscillation as the cyclic change in head -body angle ( Figure 2B , left, see Methods). We subdivided 130 

this oscillation into the following phases of the stepping cycle ( Figure 2B , right): phase 1/2 π, when 131 

the head, aligned straight with the body, is moving left and the right forepaw is swinging forward, 132 

phase 3/2 π when the head, aligned straight with the body, is moving right and the left forepaw is 133 

swinging forward and phases 0 and π, when the animal’s head is pointing to the right or left, 134 

respectively, relative to the body. During locomotion, the amplitude of the head -body angle 135 

oscillated between positive and negative ~2.5 degrees (2.64 ± 0.06 deg., average ± s .e.m ., here 136 

and throughout unless stated otherwise ) at ~5.5 Hz (5.45 ± 0.02 Hz , ~11 steps per second). 137 

Crucially, a nimals reached the center point of the maze bifurcation at opposite phases of the 138 

stepping cycle depending on the direction of the turn ( Figure 2C , left turns near phase 0 (2 π), 6.2 139 

± 0.1 rad., right turns near phase π, 2.9 ± 0.1 rad., n = 55 sessions, p < 0.001 for each turn 140 

direction, Rayleigh tests, p < 0.05, paired Rayleigh test, mean phase difference: 3.0 rad.). Thus, 141 

at the maze bifurcation, left and right turns occur at opposite phases of the stepping cycle, 142 

consistent with other organisms 1,2 .143 

144

found that left turn cells fired preferentially near phase 1/2 π, that is, when the right forepaw is 154

Turn cell activity is modulated in coordination with hippocampal sweeps 174 

Is turn cell activity also modulated in coordination with internal representations of future paths? 175 

During navigation, the hippocampus generates internal representations of the animal’s position 176 

through the spatially tuned firing of place cells 25,26 . While place cells typically signal the animal's 177 

current location, they can also express ‘non -local’ activity patterns that sweep ahead of the animal 178 

toward possible left or right future paths 16 –18 . To determine whether dSC turn cells fire differently 179 

when hippocampal sweeps are directed toward or away from their preferred turn direction, we 180 

recorded simultaneously from the dSC and the hippocampus ( Figure S4, 167 dSC turn cells, 2642 181 

hippocampal CA1 cells , n = 5 animals ) as animals performed the Y -maze task . We used an 182 

established algorithm to decode position from hippocampal CA1 activity 27 . We detected moments 183 

when, as the animal approached the bifurcation, the decoded position swept ahead, representing 184 

future paths along the left or right arm ( Figure 4A and 4B , see Methods). 185 

186 

As expected 28,29 , most hippocampal sweeps were directed toward the path the animal would 187 

ultimately take (‘ipsi sweeps’, 73.7 ± 1.8%) and the rest toward the alternative path (‘contra 188 

sweeps’). Contra sweeps occurred earlier relative to the turn and swept farther ahead of the 189 

animal compared to ipsi sweeps ( Figure S5 A and S5 B). Moreover, while the onsets of both ipsi 190 

and contra sweeps occurred preferentially during late phases of the ~8.5 Hz (8.49 ± 0.07 Hz) 191 

hippocampal theta rhythm, contra sweeps exhibited weaker phase locking compared to ipsi 192 

sweeps ( Figure S5 C). Lastly, consistent with recent results in rats 29 , the expression of contra 193 

sweeps was transiently increased following trial block switches compared to ipsi sweeps ( Figure 194 

S5 D). 195 

196

sweeps, excluding trials with sweeps in both directions and trials without sweeps. We computed 199 

the Sweep Direction Selectivity Index (SDSI, see Methods) that quantified, for turns in a given 200 

direction, the average difference in firing rate seen on trials with sweeps in the direction taken by 201 

the animal versus the opposite direction , i.e. on trials with ipsi versus trials with contra sweeps .202

To determine whether the activity of dSC neurons can be bidirectionally modulated, that is 213 

whether dSC neurons increase or decrease their firing in coordination with sweep direction, we 214 

analyzed the firing rates of each sweep -selective turn cell around the time of the bifurcation 215 

across three distinct trial types: trials with sweeps 1) only in the cell’s preferred direction, 2) only 216 

in the cell’s non -preferred direction, and 3) in both directions, all relative to trials without sweeps. 217 

This analysis revealed a clear bidirectional modulation of dSC firing rates relative to the no -218 

sweep condition: sweep -selective turn cells fired more during trials with sweeps only in their 219 

preferred direction, less during trials with sweeps only in their non -preferred direction, and 220 

showed no significant change during trials with sweeps in both directions ( Figure 4F,221 

preferred/non -preferred only: p < 0.001, both: p > 0.05, 1 -sample t -tests). Approximately half of 222 

the turn cells whose activity was modulated in coordination with hippocampal sweeps exhibited 223 

activity that was significantly phase -locked to the stepping rhythm (48%, 27/56), mirroring the 224 

percentage of these cells in the overall population (47%, 195/411). Together, these results 225 

reveal that the activity of dSC turn cells is modulated in coordination with hippocampal 226 

representations of future paths. 227

Hippocampal sweeps predict subsequent turn cell activity 229 

Hippocampal sweeps can occur at various time points prior to the bifurcation. How early before 230 

the bifurcation do sweeps predict the activity of dSC cells at the bifurcation? To address this 231 

question, we used generalized linear models (GLMs, see Methods). As sweeps typically occurred 232 

in -phase with the hippocampal theta rhythm ( Figure S5 C), we divided each trial into theta cycles 233 

prior to and at the time of the bifurcation (cycles −4 through −1 and cycle 0, respectively) and, for 234 

each of these cycles, assessed whether the presence and direction of sweeps predicted dSC 235 

firing at the bifurcation ( Figure 5A). 236 

237 

Strikingly, sweeps occurring up to 4 cycles (~500 ms) before the bifurcation could significantly 238 

predict dSC activity at the bifurcation ( Figure 5B, p < 0.01, Wald z -tests). Predictive cycles (i.e. 239 

theta cycles during which sweeps significantly predicted changes in dSC activity at the bifurcation) 240 

were observed for 50% of sweep -selective turn cells and for 7.6% of all possible cycles ( Figure 241 

5B, cells: 21/42, cycles: 32/420), both of which significantly exceeded shuffled controls ( Figure 242 

S7 A, shuffled data: cells, 29 ± 0.2%, p = 0.001; cycles, 4.1 ± 0.03%, p < 0.001, permutation tests). 243 

Most predictive cycles were driven by sweeps in a cell’s preferred turn direction (72%, 23/32). Of 244 

these, most cycles predicted increases in activity ( Figure 5B, top , 96%, 22/23), whereas, for 245 

predictive cycles driven by sweeps in a cell’s non -preferred direction (28%, 9/32) most cycles 246 

predicted decreases in activity ( Figure 5B, bottom , 67%, 6/9 cycles). 247 

248 

Distinct dSC turn cells become directionally selective at different times prior to the bifurcation ( -249 

283 ± 2 21 ms, average ± s.d., Figure 1D, top ). This raises the possibility that those dSC turn cells 250 

that become directional ly selectiv e earlier may also be those whose firing, at the bifurcation, is 251 

modulated in coordination with early sweeps. This was indeed the case: we found a positive 252 

correlation between the time of predictive cycles and time at which turn cells became directionally 253 

selective (Figure 5C, top, Pearson r = 0.7 1, p < 0.01). In other words, sweeps occurring during 254 

earlier theta cycles predicted the activity of turn cells that became directionally selective earlier, 255 

and vice versa. Thus, hippocampal sweeps can predict turn -related activity in the dSC, and the 256

Given that hippocampal sweeps can predict dSC activity at the bifurcation, we next asked whether 260 

an interaction in the opposite direction also occurred. That is, can dSC activity prior to the 261 

bifurcation predict hippocampal sweeps at the bifurcation? This possibility would be consistent 262 

with a direct influence of head turning behavior on hippocampal representations 30 . To address this 263 

question, we again turned to GLMs (see Methods). Mirroring the GLM analysis above ( Figure 5), 264 

we divided each trial into theta cycles prior to and at the time of the bifurcation and, for each of 265 

these cycles, assessed whether dSC activity predicted the presence and direction of hippocampal 266 

sweeps at the bifurcation. 267 

268 

We identified only a single instance of a significant cycle in which dSC turn cell activity predicted 269 

hippocampal sweeps at the bifurcation ( Figure S8 A), p < 0.01, Wald z -test). Additional analyses 270 

using a less conservative significance threshold yielded only weak effects that did not robustly 271 

exceed shuffled controls ( Figure S8B and S8C ). Thus, while dSC activity does not reliably predict 272 

hippocampal sweeps at the bifurcation, hippocampal sweeps reliably predict dSC activity .273 

274 

Hippocampal sweep direction matches deviations in behavioral trajectory 275 

dSC activity impacts turning behavior. Given that the direction of hippocampal representations of 276 

future paths predicts differences in dSC activity, we asked whether it also predicts differences in 277 

the animal’s behavior. This was indeed the case. 278 

First, for a given turn direction, turns were tighter during ipsi sweep trials compared to contra 279 

sweep trials ( Figure 6A ). Trajectories during contra sweep trials deviated more from a linear path 280 

and exhibited greater cumulative angular change (IdPhi 28 ) compared to ipsi sweep trials ( Figure 281 

6B and 6C , see Methods). Second, we observed an effect of sweep presence and direction on 282 

trial duration. Trials without sweeps had the shortest duration (median: 1.73 s), followed by ipsi 283 

sweep trials (1.83 s), contra sweep trials (1.95 s), and trials with sweeps in both directions (2.22 284 

s; Figure S9 A). Consistent with this effect, running speed was highest on trials without sweeps 285 

(median: 34.1 cm/s), followed by ipsi sweep trials (32.1 cm/s), contra sweep trials (31.2 cm/s), 286 

and trials with sweeps in both directions (27.5 cm/s; Figure S9 B). Thus, the movement trajectory 287 

of the animal around the turn and its running speed reflect the presence and direction of 288 

hippocampal sweeps. 289

DISCUSSION 291 

Our study shows that turn -related activity in the SC , a highly conserved vertebrate midbrain 292 

structure, is tightly coupled to ongoing locomotor behavior and modulated in coordination with 293 

hippocampal representations of possible future s.294 

295 

The turn cells identified in this study share several characteristics with previously described motor 296 

command neurons for orienting movements in the SC: 1) turn cells are located in SC’s 297 

intermediate and deep layers, layers whose neurons project to brainstem turning centers 31,32 , and 298 

whose stimulation elicits turning movements 13,14 , 2) turn cell activity primarily encodes 299 

contraversive turning movements, consistent with dSC motor signals 10,11,19,20 , 3) turn cells become 300 

directionally selective before the time at which the animal ’s behavior diverges between left and 301 

right turns, consistent with premotor activity in the dSC 10,11,13,19 –21 , and 4) turn cells fire similarly at 302 

the maze bifurcation regardless of the animal's starting position on the maze , indicating that turn 303 

cell activity reflects an egocentric, displacement -based motor response. These observations 304 

suggest that the turn cell activity reported here likely reflects motor command s for turning. 305

Our results reveal that turn -related activity in the dSC is tightly coupled to ongoing locomotor 307 

behavior, such that left and right turn cells preferentially fire at opposite phases of the stepping 308 

cycle. To our knowledge , this is the first report of oscillatory neuronal activity in the dSC that is 309 

coupled to the stepping rhythm. Because of the opposite phases of the stepping cycle at which 310 

left and right turn cells fired, their direction preference could also be inferred during straight 311 

locomotion. The alignment of turn cell activity to opposite phases of the stepping cycle is likely to 312 

create windows of opportunity that alternatively favor left or right turns . This alignment may 313 

underl ie the precise coordination observed in vertebrates between turn direction and stepping 314 

phase 1,2 and validated here in mice (Figure 2C) .315 

316 

The basis for the observed rhythmic alternation in the activity of turn cells may lie in the SC’s 317 

external and internal connectivity. Externally, the dSC receives direct input from motor -associated 318 

regions whose activity has been shown to be rhythmically modulated during locomotion 33 –35 .319 

Internally, the dSC contains inhibitory connections both within and between hemispheres 36 –38 ,320 

which may support the segregation of turn cell assemblies on opposite phases of the stepping 321 

cycle. Locomotor rhythmic activity in the dSC could, however, also originate from sensory inputs 39 –

322 41 that may oscillate in phase with locomotion. Because not all turn cells exhibited activity that was 323 

phase -locked to the stepping cycle, the neurons described in this study may constitute a distinct 324 

cell type with specific connectivity patterns. 325 

326 

We show that turn -related activity in the dSC is also modulated in coordination with internal 327 

representations of possible future paths . Specifically, w e identified a consistent, predictive 328 

relationship between representations of possible future paths in the hippocampus and the activity 329 

of dSC neurons associated with turning behavior. Furthermore, consistent with the role of dSC 330 

activity in biasing turns, when the hippocampus represented possible paths that were not 331 

ultimately taken, movement trajectories were nonetheless biased toward those paths, as if 332 

animals partially enacted possible futures that were considered but not selected. 333 

334 

The possibility that the mere idea of a movement, unless inhibited, automatically triggers the 335 

movement itself has been put forward more than a century ago 42 . This suggestion has led to the 336 

proposal that all cognitive processes go on in terms of ‘faint reinstatements’ of overt motor acts 43 .337 

Indeed, numerous studies have also shown that cognitive processes can ‘leak’ into overt 338 

behavior 44 –48 . We propose that such leakage reflects the engagement of motor circuits by 339 

cognitive processes 49 –52 , which in turn may enable cognitive representations to guide behavior. 340 

341 

Previous work examining the relationship between hippocampal sweeps and behavior has 342 

primarily focused on vicarious trial and error (VTE) wherein animals’ heads deviate to the left 343 

and/or right as they approach a decision point 16,30 . While VTE still exists following hippocampal 344 

lesions 53,54 , whether VTE could be influenced by hippocampal activity was unclear. Our results 345 

demonstrate a clear relationship between hippocampal representations of future paths and 346 

deviations in behavioral trajectory that have been associated with VTE, pointing to a potentially 347 

direct role of mental simulations of possible futures in altering the structure of behavior. 348 

349 

How might such mental simulations be transformed into motor output to guide behavior? Our 350 

results provide support for an integration -like mechanism in the dSC related to representations of 351 

possible futures. Previous work has demonstrated that integration is a key function of the dSC, 352 

wherein inputs related to orienting decisions drive progressive increases in activity that, upon 353 

reaching a threshold, drives action 55 –58 . Our GLM analysis shows that hippocampal sweeps 354 

occurring up to 4 theta cycles (~500 ms) before the maze bifurcation can predict dSC activity at 355 

the time of the bifurcation ( Figure 5 A and 5B ). This timing suggests that hippocampal sweeps may 356 

be integrated to modulate dSC turning commands, perhaps in a sequential manner aligned with 357

By contrast, evidence for an interaction in the opposite direction, in which dSC activity influences 362 

hippocampal sweeps, was limited ( Figure S8 ). This asymmetry suggests that, rather than forming 363 

a bidirectional loop, hippocampal non -local activity may preferentially influence dSC activity during 364 

online behavior. Interestingly, a recent study in mice showed that SC activity is causally related to 365 

changes in virtual heading during REM sleep 10 , suggesting that, conversely, SC activity may 366 

preferentially influence internal representations during offline behavior. These results raise the 367 

possibility that the direction of information flow between cognitive and motor systems may shift 368 

across online and offline states 62,63 ; during online states, when behavioral control is most critical, 369 

cognitive representations may selectively bias motor activity to guide action, whereas during 370 

offline states, motor activity may play a greater role in the formation and refinement of internal 371 

models 10,64 .372 

373 

In summary, turn -related activity in the SC , perhaps one of the best -understood motor outputs of 374 

this structure, is highly coordinated with the ongoing locomotor behavior and cognitive activity of 375 

the organism as it navigates its world. This coordination may ensure that turns occur at optimal 376 

phases of the stepping cycle and provides a potential pathway for cognitive circuits to bias 377 

behavior along internally planned trajectories. 378

METHODS 601 

Experimental model and animals 602 

Neural activity was recorded from twelve male C57BL/6J mice (Jackson Laboratory #000664, 3.5 -603 

8 months old). All mice were housed on a reversed cycle (light/dark cycle 12/12h). Mice were 604 

housed with littermates before experimentation and singly housed in enriched cages during food -605 

restriction and experimental protocols. All experimental procedures were conducted in 606 

accordance with the regulations of the University of California San Francisco Institutional Animal 607 

Care and Use Committee (IACUC). 608 

609 

Behavioral training and electrode implantation 610 

The implantation procedure was performed in two phases on two distinct days. First, the 611 

implantation of the headplate, and second, the implantation of the silicon probe(s). Mice were 612 

implanted under 2% isoflurane anesthesia. The headplate was placed on the skull with dental 613 

cement (Unifast LC, GC America; Optibond Universal, Kerr Dental). After post -operative recovery 614 

of five days in their home cage, mice were deprived of food to 85% of their baseline weight and 615 

pretrained to run on an elevated 1 m linear track for liquid reward (sweetened evaporated 616 

soymilk). This training was done to familiarize the mice with running on a track and accessing 617 

reward ports. After the mice alternated between the reward ports reliably on the linear track (1 -3618 

days), they were pretrained on the Y-maze task (3 -5 days, see Y-maze task and trial 619 

segmentation ). After successful pretraining on the Y -maze, mice underwent the silicon probe 620 

implantation surgery. Silicon probes were mounted on a 3D -printed moveable microdrive to record 621 

unilaterally or bilaterally from the superior colliculus (SC). In five out of the twelve mice, a silicon 622 

probe was also implanted in the dorsal CA1 region of the hippocampus in the opposite 623 

hemisphere. The types of probes used included: Single -shank probes (Diagnostic Biochips (DBC) 624 

P64 -4, 64 channels per shank, 20 μm interchannel pitch), four -shank probes (DBC P64 -1, 250 625 

μm inter -shank distance, 16 channels per shank, 20 μm interchannel pitch, DBC P128 -6, 150 μm626 

inter -shank distance, 32 channels per shank, 25 μm interchannel pitch), and eight -shank probes 627 

(DBC P128 -8, 150 μm inter -shank distance, 16 channels per shank, 30 μm interchannel pitch). 628 

Probe shanks were inserted through a small craniotomy at the coordinates described in the 629 

Electrode insertion, electrode coordinates, histology and recording site assessment section 630 

below. The shanks were initially lowered to 0.7 -1.2 mm (SC) and 0.6 mm (CA1) below brain 631 

surface and the microdrive was then fixed, via dental cement, to the headplate. A ground electrode 632 

(0.005” diameter stainless wire, A -M systems) was inserted in the cerebellum. After post -operative 633 

recovery, mice were placed back on the Y -maze training protocol for 1 -5 days while the shanks 634 

were lowered to the intermediate and deep SC (dSC) and CA1. This protocol ensured that peak 635 

Y-maze performance coincided with the time at which the recording sites reached their targets. 636 

Mice remained on food restriction for the remainder of the experiment. 637

654 

Y-maze task and trial segmentation 655 

Mice navigated an elevated Y -maze with equally spaced arms (arm length: 45 cm, arm width: 5 656 

cm) in search of liquid food reward ( sweetened evaporated soymilk) , which was dispensed from 657 

two out of three ports at the ends of each arm. The task consisted of six to eight 50 -80 trial blocks, 658 

with the locations of the active ports switching in an uncued manner between blocks. The active 659 

ports were chosen randomly within each block, with no block containing the same two active ports 660 

as the previous block. Animals could not visit the same port on two consecutive trials in order to 661 

receive a reward, so mice learned to alternate between the active ports. For trial segmentation, 662 

we first used the track linearization package 67 to map the animal’s position on the Y -maze to a 663 

simplified one -dimensional representation with a corresponding maze arm identifier. We then 664 

defined a track “position threshold” at 70% of the length of each maze arm (31.5 cm measured 665 

outward from the center of the maze or 13.5 cm measured inward from the end of the arm). Trials 666 

were defined as periods from when the animal, after leaving a reward port, crossed the position 667 

threshold of one arm, made a turn, and crossed the position threshold of another arm without 668 

entering any other arms. The time at which an animal reached the bifurcation was defined as the 669 

time at which the animal’s linearized head position transitioned from one arm to another at the 670 

center point of the maze. Animals took ~1.5 seconds to complete each trial (median: 1.57 s, IQR: 671 

1.27 -1.98 s, n = 24207 trials across 12 mice). 672

Head angle relative to the track and time of behavioral divergence 674

vector (given by the vector perpendicular to the line connecting the ears) and the midline of the 676 

track segment from which the animal approached the bifurcation. The time of behavioral 677 

divergence was defined as the time at which the animal’s head angle relative to the track 678 

significantly diverged between left and right turns. To determine the average time of behavioral 679 

divergence across sessions and animals (n = 55 sessions, 12 animals, Figure 1A, bottom), we 680 

calculated a Behavioral Divergence Index (BDI) as the difference between session -averaged 681 

head angles relative to the track for left and right turn trials. BDI was calculated within 200 ms 682 

windows advanced in 20 ms steps from 0.85 s before to 0.85 s after the time at which the animal 683 

reached the bifurcation. BDI significance was assessed using a permutation test: for each window, 684 

a null distribution was generated by randomly permuting left and right turn labels across sessions 685 

(10,000 permutations) and recalculating BDI from the resulting averages. Windows were 686 

considered significant only when the observed BDI was more extreme than all shuffled values (p 687

Head -body oscillation and phase estimation 691 

Head -body angle was defined as the angle between the animal’s head direction vector (given by 692 

the vector perpendicular to the line connecting the ears) and its body vector (given by the line 693 

connecting the midpoint between the ears and the base of the tail). To determine the average 694 

frequency of the locomotor head -body oscillation, head -body angle traces for each trial were 695 

extracted, smoothed with a Savitzky –Golay filter (50 ms , first -order) and bandpass filtered 696 

between 3 and 12 Hz (Butterworth, third -order). For all other analyses, head -body angle traces 697 

were smoothed and bandpass filtered between 4 and 8 Hz (Butterworth, third -order). 698 

Furthermore, trials were included only if they exhibited sufficient power in the head -body angle 699 

oscillation signal. To quantify this, for each trial in a given session, power spectral density was 700 

estimated using Welch’s method and the peak spectral power within the 4 -8 Hz range was 701 

identified. To ensure that trials had sufficient power for spike -phase estimation, trials whose peak 702 

power fell more than one standard deviation below the mean peak power across trials in that 703 

session were excluded from analysis. 704 

705 

To estimate the phase of the head -body oscillation at the time of discrete behavioral or neural 706 

events (i.e., turns or spikes), we used a Hilbert transform -based interpolation approach. Head -707 

body angle traces were first smoothed and filtered as described above. Peaks and troughs of the 708 

filtered signal were then identified ( scipy.signal.find_peaks , prominence = 1 o amplitude) and the 709 

instantaneous phase was obtained using the Hilbert transform. The oscillatory signal was then 710 

segmented into half -cycles (peak -to -trough and trough -to -peak). Event times were assigned a 711 

phase by identifying the half -cycle in which the event occurred and linearly interpolating the Hilbert 712 

phase within that interval. Events occurring exactly at peaks or troughs were assigned phases of 713 

0 and π, respectively. Full oscillatory cycles, defined by successive peaks, with durations outside 714 

the expected range (i.e., cycles faster than 8 Hz or slower than 4 Hz) were excluded from analysis. 715

Due to video distortions at the edges of the track associated with the wide -angle lens, only periods 722 

when the mouse was directly above the camera (within a 12.7 cm (x) by 32 cm (y) area) and 723 

locomoting uninterrupted were analyzed. 724

Turn Direction Selectivity Index (TDSI) and turn cells 781 

Turn selectivity was quantified using a Turn Direction Selectivity Index (TDSI), calculated as the 782 

difference between the average firing rates during right and left turns divided by their sum ((Right 783 

FR - Left FR) / (Right FR + Left FR)) over a 200 ms window centered on the time of the cell’s peak 784 

firing rate. Thus, right turn cells were defined by positive TDSI values and left turn cells were 785 

defined by negative TDSI values. To obtain firing rates, spike trains were converted to binary 786 

vectors and smoothed using convolution with a Gaussian kernel (50 ms window width). The 787 

resulting spike density functions were normalized by the kernel width to express firing rate in 788 

spikes per second and averaged across trials. To determine the time window for the TDSI 789 

calculation, we first defined a wide window of ± 0.5 s around the time at which the animal reached 790 

the bifurcation and, for each neuron, identified within this window the time of peak firing rate for 791 

either left or right turns (whichever was greater), using the neuron’s average firing rate across all 792 

arms. TDSI was then computed across each maze arm within a narrow window of ± 0.1 s (200 793 

ms) centered on this peak firing time. 794 

795 

TDSI significance was assessed using a permutation test. For each neuron and maze arm, left 796 

and right turn trials were pooled together and randomly reassigned into two groups whose sizes 797 

matched the original sample sizes. TDSI was then recomputed within the narrow window centered 798 

on the same actual peak firing time. This procedure was repeated 1000 times to generate a 799 

shuffled distribution of TDSI values. For right turn cells, the p -value was defined as the fraction of 800 

shuffled TDSI values that were greater than or equal to the observed TDSI, or less than or equal 801 

to the observed TDSI for left turn cells. Turn cells were defined as neurons whose TDSI retained 802 

the same sign and remained significant (p < 0.05) regardless of which maze arm the animal 803 

approached the bifurcation from. 804

Second, t rajectory deviations were quantified using the IdPhi measure (the integrated absolute 1085 

angular velocity) 28 calculated over the animal’s trajectory (Figure 6C). Briefly, average trajectories 1086 

for ipsi and contra sweep trials were first smoothed (Gaussian, 1 sample) and heading angle was 1087 

derived. IdPhi was defined as the sum of the absolute change in heading velocity across each 1088 

average trajectory, with higher values indicating greater deviations in heading. For each session, 1089 

IdPhi was averaged across left and right turn trials (Figure 6C). 1090
