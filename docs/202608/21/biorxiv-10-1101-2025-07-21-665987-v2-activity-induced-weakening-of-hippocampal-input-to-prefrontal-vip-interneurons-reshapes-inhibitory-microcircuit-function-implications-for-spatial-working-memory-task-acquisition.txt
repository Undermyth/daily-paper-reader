SUMMARY 1

Long-range projections from ventral hippocampus (vHPC) to medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) 2

support cognitive functions including spatial working memory (SWM). vHPC input targets 3

multiple prefrontal interneuron classes, yet how hippocampal input recruits these populations in 4

vivo , whether this recruitment is plastic, and how such plasticity influences cognition remain 5

unknown. Here, we combined optical stimulation of mouse vHPC inputs with calcium recordings 6

from discrete mPFC interneuron populations, revealing persistent activity-induced reweighting of 7

hippocampal recruitment across prefrontal inhibitory microcircuits. Ex vivo electrophysiology and 8

computational modeling implicated weakened monosynaptic hippocampal drive onto vasoactive 9

intestinal polypeptide (VIP)-expressing interneurons in this circuit reconfiguration. Prior vHPC 10 

input stimulation and the schizophrenia-associated Df(16)A +/– mutation, which also yielded 11 

reduced hippocampal input onto VIP interneurons, produced convergent alterations in task-12 

related VIP interneuron activity associated with poorer SWM task learning. These findings 13 

implicate hippocampal input to VIP interneurons as a key locus of plasticity capable of 14 

reconfiguring inhibitory microcircuit activity linked to impaired working memory task learning. 15

INTRODUCTION 1

Functional interactions between the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex support adaptive 2

behavior across diverse cognitive and emotional domains (Eichenbaum, 2017; Marek et al., 3

2018a; Shin and Jadhav, 2016; Sigurdsson and Duvarci, 2016; Zhang et al., 2021). By 4

integrating information about past experience with ongoing behavioral demands, hippocampal-5

prefrontal interactions help guide contextually appropriate decisions and actions (Elston and 6

Wallis, 2025; Shin and Jadhav, 2016; Yu and Frank, 2014). Reflecting their role in adaptive 7

behavior, hippocampal-prefrontal interactions are dynamically regulated by experience (Padilla-8

Coreano et al., 2019; Parfitt et al., 2017; Tang et al., 2022), and their disruption has been linked 9

to cognitive dysfunction in numerous neuropsychiatric disorders, including schizophrenia 10 

(Bähner and Meyer-Lindenberg, 2017; Cunniff et al., 2020; Godsil et al., 2013; Mukai et al., 11 

2015; Shah et al., 2018; Sigurdsson et al., 2010). 12 

In rodents, direct projections from ventral hippocampus (vHPC) to medial prefrontal 13 

cortex (mPFC) contribute to hippocampal-prefrontal oscillatory synchrony, influence prefrontal 14 

representations of behaviorally relevant information, and support learning and performance 15 

across multiple cognitive tasks, including spatial working memory (SWM) (Benchenane et al., 16 

2010; Burette et al., 2000; Schoenfeld et al., 2023; Spellman et al., 2015; Yamamoto et al., 17 

2014). Notably, vHPC inputs can induce persistent changes in vHPC-mPFC functional 18 

connectivity and cognitive function when repeatedly activated (Izaki et al., 2004; Laroche et al., 19 

2000, 1990; Park et al., 2021; Ruggiero et al., 2021; Zheng and Zhang, 2015), suggesting that 20 

activity within this pathway can reshape prefrontal circuit function over extended timescales. 21 

Moreover, persistent alterations in vHPC input to mPFC have been linked to cognitive 22 

dysfunction in rodent models of neuropsychiatric disease (Cunniff et al., 2020; Mukai et al., 23 

2015; Phillips et al., 2019; Sigurdsson et al., 2010; Tripathi et al., 2020). Together, these 24 

findings suggest that repeated vHPC activity can persistently reshape prefrontal circuit function. 25 

However, the mechanisms through which this occurs remain poorly understood. 26 

vHPC axons provide direct excitatory input to multiple classes of mPFC interneurons 27 

(Ährlund-Richter et al., 2019; Canetta et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2020; Sun et al., 2019), positioning 28 

these populations to regulate how hippocampal signals influence prefrontal network function. 29 

Vasoactive intestinal polypeptide (VIP)-expressing interneurons preferentially inhibit other 30 

interneuron populations and thereby provide a powerful source of cortical disinhibition (Fu et al., 31 

2014; Lee et al., 2013; Pi et al., 2013). In mPFC, VIP interneurons contribute to working 32 

memory maintenance, support task-related neural representations, and regulate the influence of 33 

hippocampal signals on prefrontal activity during behavior (Bae et al., 2025; Kamigaki and Dan, 34

2017; Lee et al., 2019). Another major target of vHPC input is the somatostatin (SST)-1

expressing interneuron population, which preferentially innervates pyramidal neuron dendrites 2

and is well positioned to regulate integration of long-range excitatory inputs (Kepecs and Fishell, 3

2014; Markram et al., 2004; Rudy et al., 2011). Paralleling established roles for vHPC input, 4

SST interneurons contribute to hippocampal-prefrontal synchrony, spatial information encoding, 5

and SWM performance (Abbas et al., 2018). Altogether, these findings suggest that activity-6

dependent changes in how vHPC inputs recruit distinct interneuron populations may provide a 7

mechanism by which experience persistently reshapes prefrontal microcircuit function. Yet, how 8

hippocampal input recruits these populations in vivo , whether this recruitment exhibits activity-9

dependent plasticity, and how such plasticity influences cognition-related neural activity and 10 

behavior remain unclear. 11 

The present study therefore asked whether repeated activation of vHPC inputs 12 

persistently alters recruitment of distinct prefrontal interneuron populations and how such 13 

plasticity influences circuit activity during cognition. To address this, we combined optogenetic 14 

stimulation of vHPC inputs with cell-type-specific recordings in mPFC. We found that vHPC 15 

inputs differentially recruit mPFC neuronal populations, with particularly robust engagement of 16 

VIP interneurons. Repeated stimulation of vHPC inputs produced cell-type-specific changes in 17 

neuronal recruitment, weakening VIP interneuron responses while enhancing SST interneuron 18 

activity. Slice electrophysiology and computational modeling identified reduced monosynaptic 19 

vHPC input onto VIP interneurons as a likely driver of these microcircuit changes. During early 20 

acquisition of a SWM task, mice with prior vHPC input stimulation exhibited elevated delay-21 

period VIP interneuron activity that was associated with poorer task performance. Likewise, VIP 22 

interneurons in Df(16)A +/– mice, a model of schizophrenia-associated 22q11.2 deletion 23 

syndrome with impaired SWM task acquisition, exhibited elevated delay activity during early 24 

training and received proportionally fewer monosynaptic inputs from vHPC. Together, these 25 

findings identify activity-dependent reweighting of hippocampal recruitment across prefrontal 26 

inhibitory microcircuits and suggest that diminished hippocampal recruitment of VIP 27 

interneurons contributes to maladaptive prefrontal circuit activity associated with impaired 28 

working memory task learning. 29

To characterize how vHPC input recruits distinct mPFC interneuron populations in vivo ,1

we used an all-optical approach. The red-shifted opsin ChrimsonR was expressed in vHPC 2

projection neurons and the Ca 2+ indicator GCaMP6f in VIP or SST interneurons in mPFC. vHPC 3

terminals were stimulated with red light while interneuron activity was measured simultaneously 4

by fiber photometry through the same optical fiber implanted in mPFC (Figure 1A-C; S1-3). 5

Recruitment of VIP and SST interneurons by vHPC input was first characterized during 6

an initial input-output (IO) session. During IO sessions, vHPC terminals in mPFC were 7

stimulated using a range of pulse frequencies and train lengths (two rounds of 18 stimulation 8

trains ranging from 1–40 pulses at 5–40 Hz; Figure S3B). VIP interneurons exhibited robust 9

responses that scaled with increasing stimulation frequency and pulse number (Figure 1D; 10 

S4A), with maximal responses observed during the strongest stimulation condition (40 pulses at 11 

40 Hz). In contrast, SST interneurons displayed comparatively weak responses that became 12 

detectable only at higher stimulation frequencies and pulse numbers (Figure 1E; S4G). 13 

To determine whether recruitment of these interneuron populations by vHPC input 14 

exhibits activity-dependent plasticity, we developed a multi-day stimulation paradigm designed 15 

to assess both the induction and persistence of stimulation-induced changes in neuronal 16 

responses. Male and female mice underwent six input-output (IO) sessions across a 50-day 17 

period to longitudinally characterize responses to vHPC input stimulation (Figure 2A). A subset 18 

of these mice received an additional high-frequency stimulation (HFS) protocol to test whether 19 

repeated activation of vHPC inputs could drive persistent changes in interneuron response 20 

profiles. The HFS protocol consisted of 100 stimulation trains. Each train consisted of 40 pulses 21 

delivered at 40 Hz, corresponding to the IO condition that evoked the largest responses, with a 22 

30-second inter-train interval to allow Ca² ⁺ signals to return to baseline. This stimulation was 23 

administered over 12 days during the first three weeks of the experimental timeline, in addition 24 

to the six IO sessions (IO+HFS). The paradigm was informed by prior evidence linking vHPC 25 

input activity to hippocampal-prefrontal gamma oscillatory synchrony (Spellman et al., 2015), as 26 

well as pilot experiments demonstrating relative stability of neuronal responses within individual 27 

HFS sessions but pronounced changes across repeated stimulation sessions (Figure S3C). 28 

VIP interneuron responses to vHPC input stimulation gradually declined across IO 29 

sessions in mice receiving IO stimulation alone (Figure 2B,C; S4A,E). Modest reductions in 30 

peak response amplitude across IO days (Figure 2B,C; S4A,E) were accompanied by more 31 

pronounced reductions in activity during the stimulation period and delayed response kinetics 32 

(Figure S4A-D). In IO+HFS mice, VIP interneuron responses to vHPC input stimulation were 33 

rapidly and robustly attenuated (Figure 2B-D; S4E). This attenuation persisted across 34

In striking contrast, SST interneuron responses to vHPC input stimulation during IO 9

sessions in a separate cohort of mice. HFS alone recapitulated the plasticity, yielding marked 23 

attenuation and enhancement of VIP and SST interneuron responses, respectively (Figure S5). 24

drive bidirectional, cell-type-specific changes in interneuron recruitment. 26

stimulation period (Figure S6D-F). No sex differences were detected in any recorded cell 33

Prior vHPC input stimulation reduces vHPC monosynaptic connectivity with mPFC VIP 4

interneurons 5

The pronounced, bidirectional changes observed in in vivo VIP and SST interneuron 6

recruitment raised the possibility that repeated vHPC activation alters monosynaptic 7

hippocampal drive onto these populations. To test this hypothesis, we assessed monosynaptic 8

vHPC connectivity onto mPFC VIP and SST interneurons using whole-cell electrophysiology. 9

Because HFS alone was sufficient to induce robust plasticity in vivo , mice received either 12 10 

HFS sessions or no stimulation (NS) prior to ex vivo whole-cell electrophysiology recordings 11 

(Figure 3A-C). One day after the final stimulation session, optogenetically evoked excitatory 12 

postsynaptic currents (oEPSCs) were recorded from identified VIP and SST interneurons in 13 

acute mPFC slices. Monosynaptic currents were isolated using TTX and 4-AP and evoked by 14 

brief photostimulation of ChrimsonR-expressing vHPC terminals (Figure 3D). AMPA receptor 15 

(AMPAR)- and NMDA receptor (NMDAR)-mediated currents were measured separately using 16 

standard biophysical and pharmacological approaches (Linders et al., 2022; Petreanu et al., 17 

2009) (Figure 3E; S7A,C). 18 

Consistent with a weakening of vHPC recruitment of VIP interneurons observed in vivo ,19 

both AMPAR- and NMDAR-mediated oEPSCs were markedly reduced in VIP interneurons from 20 

HFS mice relative to NS controls (Figure 3F,G), with no accompanying change in 21 

AMPAR/NMDAR ratios (Figure S7B). In contrast, neither AMPAR- nor NMDAR-mediated 22 

currents were altered in SST interneurons from the same HFS and NS animals (Figure 3H,I); 23 

AMPAR/NMDAR ratios likewise were unchanged in SST interneurons (Figure S7D). These 24 

findings identify a selective reduction in monosynaptic excitatory drive onto VIP interneurons as 25 

a potential synaptic mechanism underlying the cell-type-specific plasticity induced by repeated 26 

vHPC activation. 27

To evaluate whether reduced vHPC input onto VIP interneurons could plausibly account 6

for these population-level changes, we developed a computational model incorporating the four 7

principal neuronal classes examined experimentally. The model consisted of 36 integrate-and-8

fire neurons, including VIP (“disinhibitory”), SST (“feedback”), PV (“feedforward”), and pyramidal 9

(PYR) neurons, connected according to canonical patterns of cortical microcircuit connectivity 10 

informed by experimental studies (Canetta et al., 2020; Petreanu et al., 2009; Pfeffer et al., 11 

2013) (Figure S8). All neuron classes received excitatory drive from an external source 12 

representing vHPC input. During each 10-second simulation, vHPC input was delivered 13 

simultaneously to all neuronal populations for 1 second. Model parameters were modestly 14 

adjusted to yield stable network dynamics and qualitatively reproduce the relative response 15 

magnitudes observed experimentally during the initial IO session (40 pulses at 40 Hz; Figure 16 

4B). We then systematically varied the strength of vHPC input onto VIP interneurons while 17 

holding all other parameters constant. 18 

As expected, reducing vHPC input onto VIP interneurons progressively decreased 19 

evoked VIP interneuron activity in the model (Figure 4C,D). Importantly, this manipulation also 20 

produced a marked enhancement of SST interneuron activity. Consistent with our in vivo 21 

photometry data, PV interneuron activity and pyramidal neuron activity likewise declined as 22 

vHPC input onto VIP interneurons was reduced (Figure S6). Thus, canonical patterns of cortical 23 

microcircuit connectivity were sufficient to reproduce the qualitative pattern of cell-type-specific 24 

plasticity observed experimentally based solely on a reduction of vHPC input onto VIP 25 

interneurons. Together with the slice electrophysiology findings, these results identify reduced 26 

monosynaptic vHPC input onto VIP interneurons as a plausible circuit mechanism capable of 27 

driving the broader microcircuit plasticity induced by repeated vHPC activation. 28

VIP and SST interneurons exhibit distinct activity dynamics during SWM task 14 

performance 15 

Using photometry data from all training days and mice, Ca 2+ activity was derived using a 16 

validated spectral unmixing procedure (Brockway et al., 2023; Meng et al., 2018; Sciolino et al., 17 

2022) and aligned to five key task events: Sample Start, Sample End, Delay, Choice Start, and 18 

Choice End (Figure 5D,E; S11A-D). VIP and SST interneurons exhibited both aligned and 19 

distinct average activity profiles across SWM task events. Both populations showed similar 20 

patterns of task-related modulation during sample and choice periods, with increases at the start 21 

of these periods and decreases around goal approach and reward consumption; however, they 22 

differed in their temporal profiles. During the delay period, average VIP interneuron activity 23 

gradually increased whereas SST interneuron activity progressively decreased (Figure 5E; 24 

S12A,B). 25 

Because VIP and SST interneuron activity was correlated with movement speed during 26 

task performance (Figure 5E, S12C), training-related changes in activity during task periods 27 

accompanied by movement could reflect alterations in locomotor behavior rather than neural 28 

processes associated with task learning. Indeed, movement speed changed substantially 29 

across training during the sample and choice periods but remained stable throughout the delay 30 

period (Figure S13). We therefore focused subsequent analyses on delay-period interneuron 31 

activity to identify neural dynamics associated with successful SWM task acquisition. 32

To examine whether delay-period VIP interneuron activity relates to trial outcome, and 1

how this relationship evolves across SWM training, we compared activity during correct and 2

incorrect trials. During early training, VIP interneurons showed a decrease in activity during the 3

final portion of the delay period ("distal delay") on correct trials (Figure 6A,B). This decrease 4

was smaller during incorrect trials and was absent during late stages of training. Indeed, distal 5

delay VIP interneuron activity was elevated in incorrect relative to correct trials early in training, 6

increased across training during correct trials, and increased less prominently during incorrect 7

trials (Figure 6B; S15A). Collectively, these findings indicate that VIP interneuron activity during 8

the distal delay period distinguishes subsequent correct and incorrect choices specifically during 9

the early stages of SWM task acquisition. This relationship was not observed in SST 10 

interneurons, whose distal delay activity did not differ between correct and incorrect trials across 11 

training (Figure S15B). Notably, VIP and SST activity both differed between correct and 12 

incorrect trials during choice reward consumption (Figure S12); this activity did not differ by 13 

stimulation condition or training phase (data not shown). 14

Prior vHPC stimulation selectively alters distal delay VIP interneuron activity during early 16 

learning 17 

Having identified a training-dependent relationship between distal delay VIP interneuron 18 

activity and trial outcome, we next examined whether prior vHPC stimulation altered this activity 19 

pattern. During early training, prior HFS selectively elevated distal delay VIP interneuron activity 20 

during correct trials, abolishing the activity decrease observed in NS mice (Figure 6C). In 21 

contrast, distal delay VIP interneuron activity during incorrect trials was largely unaffected by 22 

prior HFS (Figure S15C). Consequently, VIP interneuron activity during correct trials in HFS 23 

mice resembled the activity pattern observed during incorrect trials. Consistent with these 24 

observations, training-related increases in distal delay VIP interneuron activity during correct 25 

trials were significantly blunted in HFS mice relative to NS controls (Figure 6D; S14). 26 

Importantly, the magnitude of distal delay VIP interneuron activity during correct trials 27 

early in training negatively correlated with subsequent task performance, such that mice 28 

exhibiting higher activity showed poorer SWM acquisition (Figure 6E). In contrast, distal delay 29 

VIP interneuron activity during late training was unrelated to performance. These findings 30 

suggest that elevated VIP interneuron activity during the distal delay period in early training, 31 

promoted by prior vHPC input stimulation, is associated with less effective SWM task learning. 32

If elevated distal delay VIP interneuron activity contributes to impaired SWM acquisition, 1

then a mouse model with known SWM learning deficits might exhibit a similar phenotype. To 2

test this possibility, identical recordings were performed in Df(16)A +/– mice, a model of 22q11.2 3

deletion syndrome with established impairments in hippocampal-prefrontal connectivity and 4

SWM task acquisition (Mukai et al., 2015; Passecker et al., 2026, 2025; Sigurdsson et al., 2010; 5

Tamura et al., 2017). Paralleling their wildtype counterparts (NS mice), Df(16)A +/– mice 6

expressing ChrimsonR in vHPC and GCaMP6f in mPFC VIP interneurons were trained on the 7

SWM task without any prior stimulation (Figure S9). 8

Consistent with prior reports, Df(16)A +/– mice showed impaired SWM task acquisition 9

relative to wildtype controls (Figure S15D). Strikingly, recapitulating the phenotype observed 10 

following prior vHPC stimulation, Df(16)A +/– mice showed elevated distal delay VIP interneuron 11 

activity during early correct trials relative to NS mice, and this activity remained elevated 12 

throughout training (Figure 6F). Consequently, Df(16)A +/– mice displayed blunted training-related 13 

increases in distal delay VIP interneuron activity in correct trials compared with NS mice (Figure 14 

6G; Figure S15E). Furthermore, as observed in NS and HFS mice, greater distal delay VIP 15 

interneuron activity in NS and Df(16)A +/– mice during early, but not late, training predicted poorer 16 

SWM task acquisition (Figure 6H). 17

Df(16)A +/– mice exhibit reduced synaptic targeting of vHPC inputs onto VIP interneurons 19 

The convergence of behavioral and physiological phenotypes between HFS-treated and 20 

Df(16)A +/– mice raised the possibility that both manipulations alter connectivity between vHPC 21 

inputs and VIP interneurons. To test this hypothesis, electron microscopy was used to quantify 22 

the postsynaptic targets of monosynaptic vHPC inputs in wildtype and Df(16)A +/– mice (Figure 23 

6I,J). vHPC terminals were identified in serial ultrathin sections and their postsynaptic dendrites 24 

classified as VIP-positive or VIP-negative. The proportion of vHPC synapses formed onto VIP-25 

positive dendrites was significantly reduced in Df(16)A +/– mice relative to wildtype controls 26

Activity-dependent reshaping of hippocampal recruitment across prefrontal inhibitory 14

Our findings reveal robust and persistent activity-dependent plasticity in hippocampal 16 

recruitment of prefrontal inhibitory microcircuits. Rather than uniformly altering responses across 17 

neuronal populations, repeated stimulation suppressed VIP interneuron responses, enhanced 18

Heightened delay-period VIP interneuron activity in early training may impair SWM task 4

learning 5

By analyzing VIP interneuron dynamics from the onset of SWM task training, we 6

identified two behaviorally relevant features of their delay-period activity: (1) activity was higher 7

in incorrect than correct trials in early training; and (2) heightened activity during early correct 8

trials, promoted by prior vHPC input stimulation and recapitulated in Df(16)A +/– mice, was 9

associated with poorer SWM task acquisition. Together, these observations suggest that 10 

elevated distal delay VIP interneuron activity represents a maladaptive prefrontal microcircuit 11 

state during early learning. 12 

One possible explanation follows from the well-established disinhibitory role of VIP 13 

interneurons within cortical microcircuits. By suppressing other inhibitory interneurons, VIP 14 

interneurons amplify pyramidal neuron activity, a mechanism thought to strengthen task-15 

relevant cortical representations during well-learned working memory tasks (Fu et al., 2014; 16 

Kamigaki & Dan, 2017; Lee et al., 2019; Pi et al., 2013). Early in learning, however, before 17 

animals have acquired the task rule, these activity dynamics are still emerging (Meyers et al., 18 

2012; Qi et al., 2011; Tang et al., 2022). Under these conditions, elevated VIP interneuron 19 

activity may instead amplify immature or task-irrelevant network dynamics, increasing the 20 

likelihood that these activity patterns will influence subsequent choice behavior. Conversely, 21 

lower delay-period VIP interneuron activity in early training may limit the amplification of these 22 

immature network dynamics, reducing their influence on decision-making. Consistent with this 23 

interpretation, VIP interneuron activity during the distal delay was higher in incorrect than correct 24 

trials early in training (Figure 6A,B). In our delayed non-match-to-sample task, lower delay-25 

period VIP interneuron activity may minimize interference with decision-making, allowing mice to 26 

follow their innate tendency to explore the newly available arm (Lalonde, 2002) and thereby 27 

make the correct choice. 28 

The same framework may also explain why elevated delay-period VIP interneuron 29 

activity specifically during early correct trials predicted poorer task learning. Early in training, 30 

correct choices often occur by chance, or through innate exploratory behavior, rather than by 31 

informed application of the task rule. Consequently, correct trials can occur before delay-period 32 

cortical activity reliably encodes task-irrelevant information. Under these conditions, elevated 33 

VIP interneuron activity may amplify immature cortical representations that happen to precede 34

Potential neuroadaptations underlying elevated delay-period VIP interneuron activity 11 

during early learning 12 

Unexpectedly, prior vHPC input stimulation, despite weakening monosynaptic 13 

recruitment of VIP interneurons by hippocampal input, increased distal delay-period VIP 14 

interneuron activity in early SWM training. Strikingly, Df(16)A +/– mice, whose VIP interneurons 15 

show reduced vHPC input synaptic targeting, exhibited a similar elevation in delay-period VIP 16 

interneuron activity. Together, these observations suggest that neuroadaptations downstream of 17 

diminished hippocampal recruitment of VIP interneurons underlie this behaviorally relevant 18 

activity pattern. For example, weakening vHPC input may induce compensatory reweighting of 19 

excitatory drive onto VIP interneurons by other brain regions, such as the mediodorsal thalamus 20 

(MD) (Canetta et al., 2020). MD inputs are known to directly target VIP interneurons and sustain 21 

task-relevant cortical representations across delay periods (Bolkan et al., 2017; Mukherjee et 22 

al., 2021; Schmitt et al., 2017). Thus, enhanced VIP interneuron recruitment by MD inputs could 23 

maladaptively amplify delay-period VIP interneuron activity during early training. Alternatively, 24 

neuroadaptations in prefrontal dopamine signaling may contribute to the heightened delay-25 

period VIP interneuron activity in early learning. Dopamine has long been implicated in 26 

sustaining cortical dynamics during working memory (Arnsten, 1997; Durstewitz et al., 2000; 27 

Sawaguchi and Goldman-Rakic, 1991), and abnormally heightened mPFC D1 receptor 28 

signaling in VIP interneurons, whether arising downstream of repeated vHPC input activation 29 

(Gurden et al., 2000; Sorg et al., 2004) or through the Df(16)A +/– mutation (Choi et al., 2018), 30 

could preferentially enhance distal delay-period VIP interneuron activity (Anastasiades et al., 31 

2018; Bae et al., 2025) and thereby impair SWM learning (Zahrt et al., 1997). The precise 32 

mechanisms of the altered delay VIP interneuron activity remain speculative and warrant future 33 

investigation. 34
