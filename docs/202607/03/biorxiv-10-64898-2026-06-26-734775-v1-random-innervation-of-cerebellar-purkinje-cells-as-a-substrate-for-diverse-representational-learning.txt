## ABSTRACT 9

Although the cerebellar microcircuit is among the most well-characterized 10 

systems for studying neural computation, recent connectomic analyses highlight 11 

deviations from canonical models, raising fundamental questions about connectivity, 12 

function, and learning in the system. A key feature of the circuit is the convergence of 13 a vast number of parallel fibers (PFs) onto Purkinje cells (PCs). Prevailing models of 14 cerebellar computation assume all-to-all connectivity at this intersection, whereby a 15 single PC “samples” from all PFs. However, experimental evidence suggests that each 16 PC is innervated by only a subset of accessible PFs. This partial sampling creates 17 differential connectivity that may reflect an organizational principle of cerebellar 18 learning, but its implications are poorly understood .19 Based on electron microscopy (EM) reconstructions, we show that PF 20 innervation of PCs is largely consistent with a Bernoulli model where connections are 21 randomly and independently distributed within anatomical constraints. In further 22 support of a random model, we observe that connections of the ascending branches 23 of granule cells are not predictive of connections of their PFs, nor is connectivity 24 correlated across separate spatial encounters of a PF with the same PC. In a model 25 of the cerebellar circuit, we then address the possibility that partial connectivity is a 26 

substrate of learning, i.e., a fixed, random mask that enforces diversity between PCs. 27 

We find that when considering “ensembles” of PCs, random partial connectivity can 28 indeed outperform all-to-all connectivity. Our results provide a theoretical framework 29 for understanding the role of partial connectivity between cerebellar PFs and PCs and 30 may have implications for cerebellum-like systems and beyond. 31

A thorough understanding of this partial wiring is still 55 lacking. 56 To address this gap, we leverage electron microscopy (EM) reconstructions that 57 map the cerebellar synaptic wiring diagram at the level of individual neurons ( Fig. 1a, 58

This 60 suggests that no select groups of PFs are systematically over- or under-sampled, nor 61 are there groups of PCs characterized by specific PF innervation patterns. We next 62 show that connections of GrC ascending branches are not predictive of the wiring of 63 their parallel fibers, further supporting an unstructured model of GrC-PC connectivity. 64 We then extend canonical models of the cerebellar cortex to study the impact 65 of partial connectivity on cerebellar learning. Previous work has well established that 66 PCs are organized into sagittal zones, in which several PCs receive highly correlated 67 climbing fiber inputs (Fakharian et al., 2025; Kostadinov et al., 2019; Llinas et al., 1974; 68 Oscarsson, 1979; Zeeuw et al., 1997) and converge onto neurons in the cerebellar 69 nuclei (Apps & Garwicz, 2005; Person & Raman, 2012; Sugihara et al., 2009; Zeeuw 70 et al., 1994). Combining both 72 partial connectivity and Purkinje cell groups in a unifying framework, we propose that 73 PF subsampling diversifies the inputs to otherwise redundant “ensembles” of PCs. In 74 this modeling framework, we demonstrate that such partial PF-PC connectivity can be 75 exploited to increase robustness in cerebellar learning tasks. 76 77

Parallel fibers converge in a Bernoulli-like manner 99 

To better understand whether innervation of PCs by PFs follows a particular 100 logic whereby certain PFs target specific PCs, we analyzed the structure of PF-PC 101 convergence. We included only those PCs that were touched by at least 100 PFs (total 102 reconstructed PFs in the dataset: 4,485). The median number of touches was 947 per 103 PC, and each PC formed synapses with roughly 50% of its touches (mean connection 104 probability: 49.5% ± 4% SD; Fig. 2a ). Using this probability, we defined a Bernoulli null 105 model in which each touch was converted into a connection with a probability equal to 106 the empirical population value ( Fig. 2c , Methods). 107 The Bernoulli model closely matched the distribution of empirical connection 108 probabilities, indicating that no subset of PFs is systematically over- or under-sampled 109 by PCs ( Fig. 2d 1, Monte Carlo test, p = 0.12). Similarly, principal component analysis 110 of the connectivity matrix revealed no leading components that exceeded those of the 111 Bernoulli model ( Fig. 2d 2) (Caron et al., 2013; Li et al., 2020). To assess correlations 112 in sampling between PCs, we calculated mutual information (Shannon, 1948) on 113 sampling of shared touches for pairs of PCs. Again, mutual information did not differ 114 significantly from the Bernoulli model, suggesting that the sampling pattern of one PC 115 provides little information about that of another ( Fig. 2d 3, Monte Carlo test, p = 0.36). 116 Further, we found no elevation in Hamming similarity ( Fig. 2d 4, Monte Carlo test, p =117 0.16), increased shared input between pairs of PCs, or correlation of input vectors 118 (Supplementary Fig. 2b ). Ascending branches and parallel fibers of local granule cells wire independently 141 

Local GrCs have been hypothesized to provide stronger and more focal 142 excitatory drive to PCs than the numerous, but individually weaker unitary inputs from 143 distant PFs (Bower & Woolston, 1983; Gundappa -Sulur et al., 1999). We therefore 144 next investigated the connectivity of local GrCs. The empirical GrC-PC connection 145 probability is, on average, larger than that for PFs. This difference can be mostly 146 attributed to their ascending branches ( Fig. 3a 1, c ). Again, we created a Bernoulli 147 model, now adjusted to the elevated connection probability. Like remote PFs, the 148 connectivity of local GrCs largely agreed with the Bernoulli model ( Fig. 3a ). The 149 adjusted model also reconciles a previously observed elevation in Hamming similarity 150 for local GrCs ( Fig. 3a 3) (Nguyen et al., 2023). 151 A longstanding debate has concerned the different roles of the ascending 152 branches of local GrC axons compared to their parallel fibers (Cohen & Yarom, 1998; 153 Conti & Auger, 2024; Gundappa -Sulur et al., 1999; Huang et al., 2006; Lu et al., 2009; 154 Santamaria et al., 2002). To better distinguish how these different compartments 155 contribute to GrC connectivity, we split local GrC axons into ascending branches (ABs) 156 and parallel fibers (gPFs) ( Fig. 3b ). As expected from their ascending trajectory, ABs 157 exhibited fewer touches but a relatively high connection probability ( Fig. 3c ). This is 158 likely due to repeated encounters with the same PCs, as ABs also tend to form more 159 synapses with each unique partner ( Supplementary Fig. 2f 2). In contrast, gPF 160 connectivity was comparable to that of remote PFs ( Fig. 3c, Supplementary Fig. 2f 3). 161

Diverse parallel fiber sampling can enhance learning 245 We began our investigation of network performance with a binary classification 246 task, where the network was trained to learn random associations between mossy 247 fiber inputs and binary labels and then tested on its ability to generalize to noisy 248 versions of these patterns ( Supplementary Fig. 4a ) (Babadi & Sompolinsky, 2014; 249 Cayco-Gajic et al., 2017; Litwin-Kumar et al., 2017; Xie et al., 2023). To isolate the 250 diversity produced by subsampling, we first studied the setting in which PF-PC weights 251 are initially uniform. In this case, ambiguity in the ensemble is due to differences in 252 partial wiring alone. We found that, across different levels of generalization difficulty, 253 partial connectivity outperformed full connectivity ( Fig. 5a 1). Analysis of performance 254 as a function of β showed that the postsynaptic component in the learning rule is 255 essential to exploit partial connectivity ( Fig. 5a 2). The advantage of subsampling can 256 be understood as a tradeoff between the dimensionality of PF subsets and that of PC 257 ensembles ( Fig. 5a 3) (Litwin-Kumar et al., 2017). 258 When then initialized with random weights, the ensembles still performed 259 markedly better than the individuals, but the impact of partial connectivity was 260 diminished and depended on the initial weight variance ( Supplementary Fig. 4c ). This 261 was expected, as a random weight distribution provides an additional source of 262 heterogeneity between ensemble members. We reasoned that weights that are initially 263 diverse across ensemble members may rapidly converge when ρ is large, thereby 264 reducing ensemble heterogeneity. To test this, we initialized the weights randomly and 265 then trained the network repeatedly on different training sets without re-initialization, 266 simulating an on-line learning setting more consistent with biology. In this case, partial 267 connectivity preserved ensemble diversity and thereby improved performance, 268 whereas with full connectivity, ensemble diversity was lost ( Fig. 5b ). 269 Finally, we evaluated the model in regression tasks, the second primary form of 270 supervised learning ( Supplementary Fig. 4d ). While less established in a modeling 271 context, regression tasks have recently been investigated in cerebellum-like 272 architectures suggesting that they provide a more suitable framework for some 273 cerebellar functions such as motor control (Xie et al., 2023). Building on this setup, we 274 asked whether, and how, partial connectivity could be beneficial for performance. ## DISCUSSION 288 

Although first observed decades ago (Napper & Harvey, 1988), the partial wiring 289 between PFs and PCs has received little attention and remains largely enigmatic. We 290 found that across different modalities of analysis, in the EM dataset, PF-PC 291 connectivity appears largely unstructured when spatial distributions of the cells are 292 accounted for. This holds true at the level of remote PFs as well as axons of local 293 GrCs. In an ensemble model of cerebellar learning, we demonstrated that random, 294 partial connectivity can lead to more robust model performance through diversification 295 of individual PC responses. 296 

Random PF-PC connectivity 297 

Key to the analysis of partial connectivity was the ability to characterize 298 “touches” between PFs and PC dendrites. To define touches, one needs to impose a 299 distance threshold between a presynaptic axon and a postsynaptic dendrite. This 300 raises the question of what distance threshold is most appropriate to study the 301 structure of PF-PC connectivity (Stepanyants et al., 2002). Choosing this threshold 302 comes down to the question of what we learn about connectivity from knowing that a 303 PF and a PC with a given minimal distance are not connected. Since we are interested 304 in detecting potential cell-specific selectivity that may be rooted in physiological 305 activity, the threshold should arguably lie within the range of PC spine growth. It is 306 therefore reasonable to choose a touch-threshold below ~1 μm. Here, we use a 307 threshold of 160 nm for most analyses but examined the effects across different 308 thresholds ( Supplementary Fig. 1 ). 309 While many statistics are precisely reproduced by the Bernoulli model, some 310 moderate but distinct deviations persist. Specifically, PF-PC connection probability 311 depends weakly on height in the molecular layer (Nguyen et al., 2023), and PCs show 312 a slightly elevated variance in connection probability. Both effects are linked to the 313 morphology of the PC dendritic trees ( Supplementary Fig. 2a ). Given the many 314 biological factors that can weakly influence connectivity (e.g., differences in the 315 angulation of PC dendritic trees) and the limitations of our touch metric (e.g., touches 316 onto thick dendritic branches where PFs are unlikely to form synapses), it is not 317 surprising that some measures are not fully captured by the Bernoulli model. Thus, it 318 seems unlikely that these deviations reflect deeper underlying structure. 319 A recent study has proposed that, during development, pairs of PCs share less 320 PF input than predicted by chance (Dhanyasi et al., 2025), an observation that does 321 not match our findings in the adult mouse. This may reflect ongoing developmental 322 processes. However, this finding is noteworthy from a combinatorial perspective: PCs 323 sampling from a shared pool of PFs have very limited leeway to undershare inputs in 324 a pairwise manner, unless the number of PCs or their connection probability is small. 325 In Dhanyasi et al. (2025), the distance to determine potential synaptic partners 326 (analogous to touches) is on the order of microns. If too many cells are included in the 327

Functional implications 333 We can distinguish three interpretations of the results of our connectivity 334 analysis: 335 1) Partial connectivity is indeed unstructured (i.e., the network is functionally 336 invariant to reshuffling individual connections within constraints) and is a purposeful 337 feature of the cerebellar cortex. This suggests the role of partial connectivity may be a 338 substrate, as opposed to a result of learning. This is the interpretation that we focused 339 on here and one that naturally motivates an ensemble framework, although it is not a 340 necessity for our ensemble model. 341 2) Partial connectivity is indeed unstructured, merely reflects anatomical 342 constraints and offers no functional benefit. This implies that a natural bound on the 343 total number of inputs for each PC exists. This possibility cannot be excluded, but 344 would require reconsidering the effective PF expansion ratio, or otherwise describe 345 how clusters of PCs cooperate. In general, our arguments about diversification of PCs 346 through partial connectivity would still apply under this scenario. 347 3) Partial connectivity is structured, (i.e., the network is not functionally invariant 348 to reshuffling individual connections), but the underlying structure is such that it goes 349 undetected with our analysis. Since it is impossible to prove that connectivity is 350 unstructured, we cannot exclude this possibility. However, we can ask if there is a 351 plausible and parsimonious hypothesis that aligns empirical connectivity with 352 structured wiring: 353 While there are not many functional interpretations of partial PF-PC 354 connectivity, previously, it has been suggested to be the result of learning-induced 355 synaptic pruning (Aziz et al., 2014; Morizawa et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2014 but see 356 Black et al., 1990; Ho et al., 2021). In this view, what we observed as touches would 357 correspond to eliminated synapses and the connectivity would be structured by the 358 definition given above. 359 The theoretical motivation for this idea stems from the work by Brunel et al. 360 (2004) showing that at maximum storage capacity at least half of PF-PC synapses are 361 silent ( Supplementary Fig. 5c ). It is important to distinguish between “touches” and 362 the notion of “silent synapses”. In our analysis, a touch is purely a spatial relationship. 363 By contrast, silent synapses are synapses that are electrophysiologically undetectable 364 or non-functional (Isope & Barbour, 2002). Thus, our findings do not contradict 365 predictions about the prevalence of silent synapses. However, if initially silent 366 synapses were subsequently eliminated, touches would in fact be an expression of 367 former silent synapses. 368

We performed simulations to assess whether the observed connectivity could 369 be reproduced by pruning silent synapses following information storage. In this model, 370 PCs initially share PF input according to their empirical touches ( Supplementary Fig. 371 

5). We found that correlations in climbing fiber input introduce similarity in PF sampling 372 between PCs. Depending on the geometry of the PF code, even without shared 373 climbing fiber input, such correlations in the modeled connectivity persist 374 (Supplementary Fig. 5d, e ). Together with the evidence that the fraction of silent 375 synapses is likely much larger than 50% (Ho et al., 2021; Isope & Barbour, 2002) and 376 the requirement that pruned synapses must remain as “potential” connections, it 377 seems unlikely that partial PF-PC connectivity is the expression of a stored memory. 378 Further, if partial connectivity is a result of learning and, hence, dependent on 379 PF activity and cimbing fiber input, one would expect correlated connectivity at 380 repeated encounters of a given GrC/PF with dendrites of the same PC. Such a 381 correlation was absent in our analysis ( Fig. 2f, 3d ). If connectivity was heavily 382 determined by a local postsynaptic signal, correlations between different dendritic 383 branches of the same PC could be less pronounced. Yet, given that such branches 384 are typically innervated by the same climbing fiber, an entire lack of such correlation 385 would still be unexpected. 386 Although this is only one example scenario in which connectivity derives from 387 learning, our findings point to a more general discrepancy between the correlations 388 induced by shared innervation of PCs and the lack of structure in the empirical 389 connectivity. Hence, any theory in which partial connectivity is learning-induced must 390 explain how correlations are hardly imprinted in the network despite shared PF and 391 correlated climbing fiber input of adjacent PCs. 392 393 

Ensemble model 394 

Established theories of cerebellar learning do not account for the fact that PCs 395 sample only a fraction of PFs within reach. The lack of discernible structure in PF-PC 396 wiring motivated us to explore subsampling as a substrate for, rather than a product 397 of, learning. Since a priori subsampling for individual PCs can only lead to information 398 loss, this naturally prompted us to consider ensembles. 399 An ensemble framework is reasonable only if there exist groups of PCs that 400 subserve the same task. Of particular importance for our ensemble framework are 401 functional experiments showing that climbing-fiber-evoked responses in PCs are 402 highly co-tuned within sagittal zones (Ikezoe et al., 2023; Kostadinov et al., 2019) and 403 that this co-tuning provides a principled basis for grouping PCs (Fakharian et al., 2025; 404 Herzfeld et al., 2015). The anatomical substrates of this co-tuning are likely electrical 405 coupling and shared input between the inferior olivary neurons as well as shared 406 climbing fiber innervation between PCs within sagittal zones (Groenewegen & Voogd, 407 1977; Llinas et al., 1974; Moatamed, 1966; Oscarsson, 1979; Schild, 1970; Sugihara 408 et al., 2001; Zeeuw et al., 1997). In a real-time setting, however, feedback arising through 425 interactions with the environment, for instance, cannot be easily assigned to each 426 individual PC, making such decorrelation non-trivial. We suggested a solution to this 427 problem based on differential susceptibility of PCs to a common teaching signal. This 428 was achieved by incorporating the activity of PCs (postsynaptic) into the learning rule 429 of the model. 430 The postsynaptic component of this learning rule is supported by studies in 431 cerebellar slices (Bonnan et al., 2021; Eilers et al., 1997; Finch et al., 2012; Gaffield 432 et al., 2018; Rowan et al., 2018). Addressing PF connectivity across adjacent microzones, which 475 receive distinctly tuned climbing fiber inputs, will require extending the ensemble 476 framework presented here.
