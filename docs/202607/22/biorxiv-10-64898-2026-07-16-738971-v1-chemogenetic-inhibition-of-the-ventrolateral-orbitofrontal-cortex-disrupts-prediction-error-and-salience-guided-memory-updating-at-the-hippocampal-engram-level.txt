Abstract 54 Rationale : Adaptive behavior requires revising existing memories when outcomes deviate from expectations. The 55 orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) is implicated in representing outcome expectancies, but its role in memory-updating, 56 beyond value-based learning, remains unclear. Objectives : Here, we tested whether the ventrolateral OFC (VLO) 57 contributes to hippocampal-dependent memory-updating in the Objects in Updated Locations (OUL) task, where 58 novelty-driven exploration signals successful updating of spatial information. Methods : Using inhibitory 59 DREADDs, in male and female (Swiss Webster and C57BL/6) mice, we suppressed VLO activity during the 60 Updating session, when new object-location information was presented. Results : Control mice preferentially 61 explored the newly updated location whereas VLO-inhibited mice failed to show this preference during Updating 62 and again at Test. Critically, VLO inhibition when no updating demand was present did not impair novelty 63 preference or memory for original spatial configurations at Test, indicating preserved novelty detection and retrieval 64 abilities. Conclusions: These results demonstrate that VLO activity is selectively required when novelty must be 65 interpreted relative to prior experience, further implicating this region in evaluating change rather than detecting it. 66 To determine whether these impairments reflected a deficit in memory-updating beyond altered salience attribution, 67 we tagged neuronal ensembles in the dorsal dentate gyrus recruited during Updating, followed by quantification of 68 overlap between Updating-tagged cells and ensembles reactivated at Test. We found that behavioral deficits were 69 correlated with reduced ensemble overlap, indicating that VLO activity supports memory-updating at the 70 hippocampal engram level. These findings extend OFC models beyond reward contexts and identify a cortical 71 contribution to predictive spatial memory processes. 72

1. Introduction 89 

Memory-updating is thought to occur when retrieved representations are rendered labile by prediction 90 

errors, allowing new information to be incorporated into existing memory traces (Exton-McGuinness et al., 2015; 91 

Groves et al., 2025; Milton et al., 2023; Nader et al., 2000; Wahlheim & Zacks, 2025). Adaptive behavior in 92 

dynamic environments depends on the brain’s capacity to update memories when expectations are violated 93 

(Lourenco & Casey, 2013). The orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) plays a critical role in assigning salience to outcomes, 94 

predicting and representing expected rewards and their associated values (Rolls et al., 2020), and incorporating new 95 

information when predictions are no longer valid (Sakaki et al., 2011; Takahashi et al., 2009). Although traditionally 96 

examined in the context of value-based decision making (Zhou et al., 2021), emerging evidence indicates that the 97 

OFC also contributes to memory-updating by signaling reward predictions, guiding representational change, and 98 

interacting with mnemonic systems (Mızrak et al., 2021; O’Neill & Schultz, 2013; Stalnaker et al., 2018). 99 

Mechanistically then, the OFC supports flexible updating of expected outcomes when environmental conditions 100 

shift, allowing for re-evaluation of previously learned associations. Understanding how the OFC supports these 101 

memory dynamics is essential for elucidating the neural mechanisms underlying cognitive flexibility and its 102 

disruption in neuropsychiatric disorders. 103 

The ventrolateral OFC (VLO) projects to many subcortical structures (Cavada et al., 2000; Reiten et al., 104 

2023), is linked to behavioral flexibility (Butkovich et al., 2025) and integrates sensory, reward, and associative 105 

information (Rolls et al., 2020; Rolls & Grabenhorst, 2008). VLO inactivation impaired flexible behavior by 106 

disrupting the ability to integrate outcome expectancies (Sarlitto et al., 2018). Thus, it is anatomically and 107 

functionally poised to alter episodic representations. Our pilot studies suggest the VLO, in comparison to the medial 108 

OFC (MO), is involved in guiding choice behavior when novel object-locations are present (Figure 1A, 109 

Supplemental Figure 1). However, the causal contribution of the VLO to memory-updating remains unclear. Given 110 

its putative role in integrating associative and mnemonic information, we tested whether neural ensembles within 111 

this region are necessary for a hippocampal-dependent spatial memory-updating task (Exps. 1 & 2) and whether its 112 

inactivation disrupts updating of hippocampal representations (Exp 2). 113 

The Objects in Updated Locations (OUL) Paradigm (Kwapis et al., 2020), can be used to examine 114 

hippocampal-dependent spatial memory-updating. In this task, mice first learn the locations of several identical 115 

objects during Training. During Updating, one object is moved to a new location, creating a prediction error 116

Here, we employed inhibitory DREADDs (Designer Receptors Exclusively Activated by Designer Drugs) 123

2.1 Animals 133 

All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with protocols 3509 and 3513 approved by the 134 

Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Loyola University Chicago. A total of 73 mice (Swiss 135 

Webster mice: Pilot: n=9, 7M, 2F; Exp 1: n=40, 20M, 20F) and 24 inbred (C57BL/6 mice: Exp 2: n=24, 12M, 12F) 136

the light part of the cycle under red light to eliminate glare in the testing chamber. Cages containing huts and nesting 141 

material for enrichment were changed weekly. Swiss Webster mice were fed standard chow (Teklad 8604 chow) and 142 

water ad libitum. C57BL/6 mice were fed a diet containing 40 mg/kg doxycycline (DOX) (Bio-Serv F4159). 143

2.8 Training 205 

Following the final day of Habituation, the Training phase of the experiment began which lasted 2 days 206 

(Figure 1D). Mice were placed in the apparatus with two objects in positions A1 (southeast zone - ZSE) and A2 207 

(southwest zone - ZSW) and were allowed to explore freely for 5 minutes (Figure 3A). The objects remained in the 208 

same positions throughout Training. A discrimination index (DI) was used to assess each animal’s ability to 209 

distinguish between the two objects as well as to assess whether they displayed a preference for any object-location. 210 

To calculate the DI, the time spent exploring each object-location was used in the following formula: (A2-211 

A1)/(A2+A1)*100. The DI is represented on a scale of 0 (no preference) to +/-100 [preference for one object-212 

location; A1(-), A2(+)]. Exploration was scored when the animal’s nose entered a 1 cm area surrounding the object, 213 

defining the zones. 214 

2.9 Updating 215

5 minutes (Figure 1E). Experimental (hM4Di) and control (mCherry) mice were further divided into two groups: 217

μm) were taken per hemisphere from three different slices yielding ~6 total z-stacks per animal. Data from each 268 

hemisphere was then pooled and the means for the 6 z-stacks were computed. These means were then used to obtain 269 

a group mean. Percentage of immunoreactive (eYFP or mCherry) neurons, including overlaps, was defined as a 270 

proportion of total DAPI-labeled cells (e.g., eYFP +/DAPI*100). Chance overlap was calculated as the percentage of 271 

the first immunoreactive neuron multiplied by the percentage of the second immunoreactive neuron (i.e., 272 

%eYFP*%cFos). Overlaps were normalized to chance by dividing the proportion of overlaps (i.e., 273 

overlaps/DAPI*100) by this chance calculation. 274

3. Results 293 

3.1 Viral Strategy and Experimental Design 294 

Our virus strategy consisted of targeting the VLO with inhibitory DREADDs in outbred Swiss Webster 295 

mice (Figure 1A). Only correctly targeted animals were included in the data analysis (Figure 1A). Our paradigm was 296 

adapted from Kwapis et al., (2020) who developed the OUL task to assess memory-updating. In this task, mice were 297 

exposed to four identical objects in specific locations (Figure 1B). Our design consisted of 3 days of Habituation 298 

(Figure 1C) followed by 2 days of Training (Figure 1D). The following day, mice received injections of DCZ 15 299 

minutes prior to the Updating session (Figure 1E) and the next day they were tested for memory retention of the 300 

original and updated information (Figure 1F). 301

4. Discussion 489 

We showed that VLO inhibition using inhibitory DREADDs selectively disrupted memory-updating in the 490 

OUL task without impairing novelty-seeking or memory for original spatial information. Mice typically demonstrate 491 

a robust preference for novelty (Berlyne, 1950; Clinton et al., 2007; Ennaceur & Delacour, 1988; Lueptow, 2017). 492 

Consistent with this innate adaptive preference, mice spend more time exploring novel vs. familiar stimuli, a 493 

principle used in the OUL paradigm (Kwapis et al., 2020) and other tests e.g., novel object recognition (Lueptow, 494 

2017), social preference (Beery & Shambaugh, 2021; Choleris et al., 2009; Ervin et al., 2015), spontaneous 495 

alternation (d’Isa et al., 2021), and the hole board (File & Wardill, 1975). However, this preference can vary with 496 

strain, age, and environmental enrichment (Arakawa, 2023; Dickson & Mittleman, 2021; Murai et al., 2007). We 497 

saw this evident in our control groups. The disruption we observed did not reflect a global loss of novelty 498 

preference. This emerged only when inhibition occurred during exposure to new information (A2→A3). Thus, VLO 499 

inhibition interfered with novelty-driven behavior only when novelty indicated a change to previously learned 500 

spatial information. Mice still recognized and explored novelty when it appeared in a stable context but failed to do 501 

so when novelty needed to be interpreted relative to an existing memory trace. This dissociation suggests the VLO 502 

contributes to recognizing and acting on novel information in service of updating, rather than to novelty processing 503 

per se. 504 

Behavioral control measures did not account for these deficits. Baseline exploration during Habituation was 505 

comparable across groups, with no consistent quadrant preferences or locomotor differences. Although acute saline 506
