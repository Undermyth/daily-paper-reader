Abstract 18 Forward planning involves mentally simulating future choices. Such mental simulation may 19 constitute an independent cognitive operation, or it may be intricately linked to canonical 20 cognitive operations supporting working memory of choice options, covert attentional 21 information sampling, forming memory models of problem structure and effort control for 22 pursuing future goals. Here, we test the relative independence of planning in non-human 23 primates with an object-sequence planning task assessed together with an assay of simpler 24 cognitive tasks. The sequence task unexpectedly masked objects at future serial positions 25 probing NHP’s to plan ahead serial future choices. We found that five of six subjects planned 26 ahead 2-3 ordinal positions. Planning depth was predicted by knowledge of the serial structure 27 but constituted a unique cognitive factor that was distinct from sequence memory, object 28 memory, and learning efficacy. Similarly, planning was segregated from working memory 29 capacity, attention, inhibitory control and effort control. These findings suggest that planning 30 ahead constitutes a separate construct not reducible to other domain-general cognitive 31 operations, and emerges in NHP’s that successfully form an internal model of the problem 32 structure. 33

Introduction 34 

The ability to plan ahead endows subjects with flexibility to reach goals that require more than 35 one step ahead of the current behavioral state. Planning can thereby be defined as the selection 36 of a sequence of choices to reach a favorable outcome (Mattar and Lengyel, 2022). While this 37 definition of planning suggests that specialized mental search and inference algorithms exist 38 that implement efficient planning (Botvinick and Toussaint, 2012; Mattar and Daw, 2026), 39 basic planning processes involve a combination of simpler canonical cognitive operations. The 40 simpler processes that may underlie the ability to plan includes ( i) the ability to form an internal 41 model of possible future choices, ( ii ) the use working memory to activate and maintain versatile 42 future options, ( iii ) the covert sampling of information that prioritizes, i.e. attends, relevant 43 options while suppressing non-favored options, and ( iv ) the subjective willingness to exert 44 effort and spent cognitive resources to mentally consider future options until a versatile future 45 option is identified. Combining these simpler canonical processes may naturally reflect mental 46 planning operations. According this view planning may have evolved from tying together 47 evolutionary processes that favor improvements in structure learning, working memory, 48 attentional control and effort control (Scarf et al., 2011). Despite this possibility, it has 49 remained unresolved how separable the abilities in these more basic cognitive processes are 50 from the ability to plan ahead. 51 52 Studies aiming to identify the core set of cognitive factors that explain performance variance 53 on executive control tasks in humans have not found planning to be a distinct factor. Rather, 54 planning abilities are subsumed within a common cognitive control factor, or spread across 55 more basic cognitive factors that include working memory updating, shifting and inhibitory 56 control processes (e.g. (Engle et al., 1999; Miyake et al., 2000)). Similarly, when planning has 57 been studied in monkeys, the limitations of planning abilities are rarely linked to constraints in 58 planning abilities per se. Rather, limitations to plan ahead not much more than about two items 59 in nonhuman primates are considered to reflect limitations in other cognitive operations such 60 as limitation in working memory (WM) capacity that constrain the number of future items that 61 can be maintained online (Scarf et al., 2011; Templer et al., 2019; Wen et al., 2025b); limitation 62 in attentional control and inhibition that constrain the ability to suppress sub-goals that are non-63 favorable and that would increase the distance from a desired future goal (Fragaszy et al., 2003; 64 Beran et al., 2015); and to overall lower levels of cognitive effort control that is needed to spent 65 cognitive resources on considering future steps instead of directly acting out (Mattar et al., 66 2025). These considerations are plausible but raise an unanswered question whether planning 67

3abilities are separable at the cognitive behavioral level from abilities in WM capacity, 68 attentional control, inhibitory control, and effort control. 69 70 Here, we address this question by testing how six nonhuman primates learn object sequences 71 and, once learned, continue performing the sequence when objects are masked. Masks were 72 introduced in 90% of trials after the first, second, or third correct choice of the sequence to 73 engage subjects to spontaneously engage in forward planning to maximize the likelihood to 74 complete the sequence and receive reward. We found that five of six subjects planned ahead 75 by at least two objects after mask onset. The planning depth was predictable from the level of 76 knowledge of the sequence structure, but was not associated with how fast subjects learned the 77 sequence, how well they recalled individual objects of the sequence, or by their working 78 memory capacity, attentional control, inhibitory control and effort control as measured with 79 separate tasks. The results show that NHP’s spontaneously engage in planning and suggest that 80 planning is a distinct cognitive skill that is not reducible to other cognitive factors. 81

Results 83 

Six rhesus macaques performed a sequence learning and planning task across 45-47 sessions 84 (subject B: 45; F: 46; J: 47; K: 45; R: 45; S: 46) in which they learned to choose four objects 85 in a pre-determined, fixed order, ignoring a fifth distracting object ( Fig. 1A ). Subjects had 20 86 attempts, or trials, to complete a sequence. When a sequence was completed with ≤6 errors per 87 trial fluid reward was provided. Otherwise, the trial terminated and a new trial started with the 88 same objects at random locations. For each new sequence the first five trials showed objects 89 unmasked. Trials 6–20 introduced grey square masks in 90% of trials. The masks overlayed all 90 remaining objects after the first, second, or third correct ordinal choice ( Fig. 1B ). Each session 91 tested five sequences, followed by other tasks that tested effort control and abilities in 92 inhibitory control and attention ( Fig. 1C ). 93 94 Subjects learned the sequences, completing the first sequence on average in 1.57 (SE = 0.04) 95 trials and continuing to reduce the number of errors in subsequent trials ( Fig. 1D ,E). From trial 96 5 onwards objects were masked once subjects correctly chose the first, second, or third ordinal 97 position, which required subjects to complete the sequence without visible objects. Despite the 98 masks, subjects continued completing sequences on trials 11–20 near ceiling across all 99 conditions (completion rates for no-mask: 0.90 ± 0.01; masked at position 2, i.e. mask-2: 0.91 100 ± 0.01; mask-3: 0.92 ± 0.01; mask-4 : 0.92 ± 0.01) ( Fig. 1D ). Planning depth is predicted by knowledge of serial order 169

Planning and sequence memory is distinct from other canonical cognitive constructs 229 

Our next question was whether planning and sequence learning are related to other higher-230 order cognitive processes. We tested the relationship of planning depth with four domain-231 general cognitive operations: inhibitory control, working memory, attention, and effort control 232 (Fig. 5 ). Inhibitory control was measured with an antisaccade task that required inhibiting a 233 pro-saccade response ( Fig. 5A ). All subjects showed lower accuracy and slower response times 234 in the antisaccade than the prosaccade condition ( Fig. 5B ,C). Working memory capacity was 235 measured with a continuous working memory updating task that measured how many objects 236 subjects can add to their working memory before committing an error ( Fig. 5D ). Subjects 237 updated on average 7.4 stimuli (SE = 0.15) before committing an error (using a 75% accuracy 238 threshold) ( Fig. 5E ). Individual subjects had working memory capacities ranging from 6.04 (± 239 0.21 stimuli, monkey J) to 10.75 (± 0.31 stimuli, monkey F). When subjects made errors, they 240 most likely erroneously chose an object that was added to working memory in earlier trials 241 (Fig. 5F ), consistent with a general memory decay. Selective attention was measured with a 242 visual search task. Accuracy finding a target declined with increasing numbers of distractors 243 when the distractor shared features with the target (high target-distractor similarity, slope = 244 −0.021 ± 0.001, p < .001), while accuracy was rather independent of distractors when they 245 differed from the target (slope: −0.002 ± 0.0005) ( Fig. 5H,I ). Effort control was measured with 246 a task requiring subjects to choose between two options that differed in reward magnitude (1-247 5 drops of juice visualized using increasing number of tokens) and effort requirement (number 248 of touches needed to inflate a ballon visualized as contours inside the balloons) ( Fig. 5J ). When 249 obtaining a larger reward required additional effort, animals became progressively less likely 250 to choose the larger-reward option as the effort difference increased ( Fig. 5K ; binomial GLM 251 slope = -0.293 ± 0.006, p < .001). Conversely, animals became progressively more likely to 252 choose the higher-effort option as the reward advantage of that option increased ( Fig. 5L ;253 binomial GLM slope = 0.320 ± 0.007, p < .001) (Fig. 5K,L). Planning ahead about two steps reflects a spontaneous choice strategy 307 

We used serial object-order learning as the paradigm to probe planning ahead by introducing 308 masks at variable times during sequential performance. At what stage the masking was 309 introduced during the sequence was pseudorandomized in order to probe how subjects 310 prospectively prepared their choices as opposed to using masks as a fixed cue that would have 311 triggered a recall operation (Raby and Clayton, 2009). Five of the six subjects engaged in 312 prospectively planning for at least two positions ( Fig. 2D ) with a model estimating planning 313 horizons of 2-3 ordinal positions ahead ( Fig. 2E-H ) and factor analysis suggesting that 314 successful planning ahead to choose masked object locations differs from the ability to choose 315 unmasked objects ( Fig. 4 ). This inferred planning depth likely does not reflect the limit of 316 future planning, because the task paradigm did not enforce maximal planning requirements, 317 but rather probed for planning either early or late during serial performance (with masks after 318 the 1 st , 2 nd , or 3 rd choice) and included trials without planning requirements (no mask trials). 319 With this mixture of trials subjects could have chosen low planning depth and still have 320 received above-chance completion rate across trials, suggesting that planning ahead was a 321 strategic choice subjects engaged with spontaneously. This feature of our task paradigm 322 enabled analysis of the choice strategies ( Fig. 2H ) which showed that two subjects (S and J) 323 showed a planning depth of three objects as their most preferred strategy, one subject (K) 324 planned ahead 2 steps in the majority of sessions, two subjects (B and F) only occasionally 325 showed planning depth above 1 step and one subject did not plan ahead (R). This planning 326 variability is reminiscent on prior studies that trained nonhuman primates the serial order of 327 images and then masked all objects after the first correct choice (Scarf et al., 2011). In this 328 paradigm planning depth was two items after mask onset in most subjects while one subject 329 reached above chance performance on the fourth item (Scarf et al., 2011). Similarly, three of 330 four rhesus monkeys learning 5-object sequences have been found to anticipate the 4 th object 331 in a sequence and rearrange it to the second position, providing evidence for position-specific 332 coding of objects and some planning foresight of at least four objects (Wen et al., 2025b). The 333 planning horizons of 2-4 in rhesus monkeys’ contrast with reports in chimpanzees, with some 334 individuals being able to recall eight and more locations of learned digit symbols in the right 335 numerical order after they were only briefly presented before being masked (Inoue and 336 Matsuzawa, 2007). While there are likely genuine limits of considering future choice options 337 in rhesus monkeys relative to chimpanzees (Raby and Clayton, 2009; Redshaw and Bulley, 338 2018), it is noteworthy that several chimpanzees showed prospective serial recall of learned 339

Planning depth emerges from an internal knowledge model 345 

We found with path analysis that knowledge of the object sequence was the strongest direct 346 predictor of planning, while the ability to recall individual objects and the ability to efficiently 347 learn object sequences only indirectly contributed to account for planning depth ( Fig. 3 ). The 348 close relation of knowing the serial order and perform the serial order when items were masked 349 was corroborated in the larger-scale exploratory factor analysis that grouped together planning 350 and sequence knowledge while separating them from item-wise memory, working memory, 351 learning, attention, and effort control ( Fig. 6A ,B). These findings corroborate previous 352 observations that the flexible mental re-arrangement of object order – which involves a form 353 of planning ahead – was more likely when monkeys encoded serial object order using an 354 abstract position code (Wen et al., 2025b). When monkeys learn serial relations among objects 355 they initially use associative memory of item pairs, similar to chaining neighboring items 356 (Terrace, 2005), but can develop the ability to represent the ordinal positions of objects 357 consistent with a more abstract position code (Wen et al., 2025b), or by forming an abstract 358 relational rule code (Sammeroff and Hampton, 2025). The more abstract representation of 359 ordered information will facilitate planning operations. This suggestion resonates with 360 computational frameworks that assume mental forward planning originates from internal 361 models of the object order (Mattar and Daw, 2026). The internal models might be encoded as 362 object-specific state transitions as in successor representation models, outcome probabilities of 363 object positions as in inference models of planning (Botvinick and Toussaint, 2012; Solway 364 and Botvinick, 2012), or as synaptic weights in multi-layer neural networks implementing a 365 non-spatial cognitive map of the serial order of the object sequence (Behrens et al., 2018; 366 Gershman, 2018; Whittington et al., 2022). All these computational architecture share the 367 assumption that the ordinal object position within a sequence structure is represented in some 368 abstract form to allow mental simulating choices of objects within that structure representation 369 without executing the choices. Our finding of the importance of learned knowledge of the 370 sequential order of objects is consistent with core assumptions of these models and provides 371 predictions about the neuronal correlates of planning operations. In particular, our results 372 suggest that neurons implementing planning ahead will recruit activity from neuronal 373

Planning ahead only indirectly relates to WM capacity, attention and effort control 379 

We found that planning depth was not related to working memory capacity of individual 380 subjects and was neither correlated with attentional abilities of interference control during 381 visual search, with the efficiency to inhibit prepotent response in the antisaccade task or the 382 willingness to exert effort to obtain higher reward in an effort control task ( Fig. 6 ). These 383 findings were unexpected and suggest that the ability to consider 2-3 future choices prior to the 384 onset of masks – which is the basic metric reflecting planning in our study - is a distinct 385 cognitive operation that is not reducible to a simple combination of working memory 386 maintenance of choice options, covert attentional information sampling, suppressing non-387 favorable choice options and the availability of cognitive resources to engage in mental 388 simulations without immediately acting. As a note of caution, the absence of correlations of 389 these simpler cognitive operations and planning is a null finding and does not rule out that more 390 complex planning tasks will recruit these other functions, or that dramatically larger sample 391 sizes beyond the six NHP’s available for this study may reveal relationships among cognitive 392 abilities. Beyond these limitations, our findings provide evidence for the absence of a direct 393 relationship of working memory capacity and planning. Rather, working memory capacity was 394 associated with better object level recall in the sequence learning task (latent factor correlation 395 of Φ = 0.45, p = .004, Fig. 6B ). According to the path analysis results object-level memory 396 influences how well serial relationships can be learned (path link: 0.467, p<0.001) ( Fig. 3B ), 397 suggesting that subjects’ working memory capacity positively influence object-level memory 398 and thereby indirectly facilitates forming serial associations among objects. Our findings thus 399 suggest that once working memory supports object level memory and enables sequence 400 knowledge, subject will more likely engage in planning. 401 402 The outlined scenario is consistent with our main conclusion that planning is as a distinct 403 operation, while at the same time outlining an indirect route for working memory capacity to 404 facilitate planning operations. This hypothesized, indirect link of mental planning operations 405 and working memory capacity reconciles various previous reports linking those processes. For 406 example, in NHPs, mentally manipulating the serial order of objects correlate with lower (i.e. 407

Univariate predictor screening. To identify which behavioral metrics predicted planning 649 depth, we conducted univariate predictor screening using separate LME models for each z-650 scored predictor against planning depth with subject as a random intercept ( Fig. 3A ). 651 652 

Structural path analysis. To test the causal architecture linking learning, memory, and 653 planning, we conducted an observed-variable recursive path analysis with four constructs 654 ordered by their hypothesized processing sequence: Learning Speed → Item-wise Memory → 655 Sequence Knowledge → Planning. For each construct, a score was computed using the mean 656 of the z-scored behavioral metrics that defined the composite score. Learning Speed comprised 657 the metrics first completion trial, trial to criterion, cumulative learning errors, and early error 658 rate (reverse-scored so that higher values indicate faster learning). Item-wise Memory 659 comprised position 1 accuracy and pre-mask accuracy. Sequence Knowledge comprised 660 maximum consecutive correct, perfect trial rate, and touch efficiency index. Planning 661 comprised planning depth. 662 663 Each endogenous node was regressed on all preceding nodes simultaneously using LME 664 models with subject as a random intercept, yielding direct path coefficients for all six forward 665 links. Total effects were decomposed into direct and indirect components using path tracing 666 rules ( Fig. 3B,C ). Confidence intervals for indirect effects were obtained via cluster bootstrap 667 (2,000 resamples at the subject level), resampling entire subjects with replacement and re-668 estimating all path coefficients at each iteration. Bootstrap p-values were computed as twice 669 the proportion of bootstrap samples in which the indirect effect crossed zero. 670 671
