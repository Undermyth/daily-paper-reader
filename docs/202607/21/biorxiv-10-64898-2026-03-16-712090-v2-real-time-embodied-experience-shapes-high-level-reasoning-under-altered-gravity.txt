Abstract 22 

A critical aspect of human cognition is the ability to use our knowledge about the laws 23 

of physics to make predictions about physical events. Whether this ability is based on 24 

abstract processes or is grounded in our body-environment interactions remains an open 25 

debate. We used physical reasoning under altered gravity as a model system to show that 26 

humans’ real-time embodied experience modifies their high-level physical reasoning. 27 

Specifically, we tested participants in computerised reasoning games, while disrupting their 28 

gravitational signalling using Galvanic Vestibular Stimulation (GVS). Participants failed more 29 

and had suboptimal strategies under the GVS condition compared to no-GVS in games 30 

requiring reasoning about terrestrial gravity. However, the effects of GVS were reduced 31 

when the games included reasoning about altered gravity. Our findings demonstrate how the 32 

physical experience of the body shifts high-level cognitive skill as reasoning, suggesting that 33 

humans’ mental representation of the world is grounded in adaptable physical mechanisms. 34

Introduction 36 

Physical reasoning—the intuitive ability to predict how objects and environments 37 

behave under the laws of physics—is a fundamental component of human cognition 1,2 . Even 38 

the simplest daily activities, as moving a cup away from a table’s brink or catching a ball in 39 

mid-air, require humans to harness cognitive resources to anticipate and respond to their 40 

physical environment. These daily activities are supported by the ability to use knowledge of 41 

real-world physical laws to predict environmental changes, understand which action drives a 42 

successful interaction with their environment and interpret and extract valuable insights from 43 

failed actions. Yet, despite the importance of physical reasoning for human survival, the 44 

question is still open regarding its underlying mechanisms. 45 

A contentious debate in cognitive psychology and neuroscience has revolved around 46 

whether human reasoning is an abstract, modular process 3,4 that relies on amodal symbols, 47 

rules and conventions 3 , or whether it is fundamentally grounded in human bodily interactions 48 

with the environment 5-8 . In other words, is physical reasoning a result of a ‘high-level’ 49 

rational, logic-based inference or is it honed and refined through ‘low-level’ embodied 50 

sensorimotor experience? This is not a trivial debate. The implications extend far beyond 51 

psychological research, informing fields such as physics education 9-12 , instructional design, 52 

artificial intelligence and robotics. This is particularly relevant given the continuing challenge 53 

of replicating human-like common sense, intuitive reasoning, and adaptive behaviour in 54 

artificial systems 13 .55 

Traditionally, cognitive researchers argue that physical reasoning is grounded in 56 

innate knowledge of the relationships between cues and outcomes and is largely detached 57 

from the real-time sensorimotor experiences that shape cognition 14-17 . Similarly, Bayesian 58 

models of cognition suggest that individuals use priors grounded in an intuitive knowledge of 59 

Newtonian physics, informed by past observations, to refine real-time physical 60 

predictions 15,18,19 . Under perceptual uncertainty, these priors integrate with new sensory data 61 

to forecast causal relationships and potential outcomes of actions 20-22 .62 

An alternative view on physical reasoning has emerged in the last decades, which 63 

argues that cognitive processes are fundamentally grounded in bodily experiences and 64 

interactions with the physical world 3,5 . For example, humans’ prediction of the trajectory of 65 

moving objects (e.g., a ball thrown towards them) relies on their need to physically act upon 66 

this prediction (e.g., catch the ball). Moreover, the potency of physical reasoning in this 67 

context is affected by individual variations in skills 23,24 . A seasoned athlete with robust 68 

physical prowess may be more adept at foreseeing the course of a swiftly thrown ball, 69 

granting them a competitive edge in timely reactions 25,26 . This interplay between prediction 70 

and action, underpinned by physical reasoning, allows humans to efficiently navigate 71

Gravity’s ubiquitous presence on Earth offers a unique framework for investigating a 74 

central question in cognitive science concerning whether physical reasoning arises from 75 

high-level, abstract inferential processes or is instead grounded in, and shaped by, 76 

embodied sensorimotor experience. All living species on our planet have been continuously 77 

exposed to a constant gravitational acceleration of 9.8 m/s 2 (1g) which has influenced the 78 

way we successfully interact with the environment 27,28 . The vestibular system in the inner ear 79 

continuously monitors the position of the head with respect to gravity. By integrating 80 

vestibular signals with visual, proprioceptive, and visceral cues, the brain creates an internal 81 

model of terrestrial gravity, which provides a fundamental prior for reasoning about motion 82 

and spatial orientation and predicting trajectories, weight, and stability of objects in everyday 83 

contexts 28 . Developmental evidence suggests that aspects of this gravity prior are innate, as 84 

events that defy expected gravitational behaviour violate the expectations of human 85 

toddlers 29 and animals 30 .86 

However, it remains unclear to what extent real-time embodied experience can 87 

recalibrate this internal mental model. In other words, does immediate sensorimotor 88 

exposure to a novel gravitational environment alter high-level physical reasoning, or is this 89 

gravity model largely fixed? Addressing this question is important because it speaks to the 90 

flexibility of the human mind: if brief physical experiences can update an internal gravity 91 

model, then one of the most abstract mental skills is grounded in recent bodily states. If not, 92 

reasoning may rely on - and be limited by - a more ‘hard-wired’ prior. Here, we address this 93 

question by conducting two pre-registered studies, guided by a simple logic: if real-time 94 

embodied experience affects the mechanisms underlying physical reasoning, then altering a 95 

person’s gravitational cues while solving a physical reasoning task should affect their internal 96 

gravity model (and thereby their task performance). 97 

In the first study, we tested participants under terrestrial gravity with and without 98 

perturbation of vestibular gravity cues. Healthy adult participants completed the Virtual Tools 99 

task 20 —a set of computerized reasoning games in which they had to reason about the 100 

movement of objects in a virtual 2D environment (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Video 1). 101 

While participants performed the reasoning tasks, we transiently perturbed 102 

gravitational signalling using disruptive Galvanic Vestibular Stimulation (GVS) 31 , which 103 

introduces controlled noise into vestibular afferent processing. In contrast to genuine 104 

alterations of gravitational input achieved through spaceflight and terrestrial analogues (e.g., 105 

parabolic flights, centrifugation, and head-down bed rest), this approach selectively 106 

modulates neurovestibular signalling without eliciting concomitant changes in other 107 

physiological systems, including cardiovascular, respiratory, or musculoskeletal function. 108

Participants engaged in a set of games that systematically varied in their reliance on 112 

gravity-based predictions, enabling us to test whether disrupting vestibular input selectively 113 

impairs reasoning in gravity-dependent contexts or instead produces a more general 114 

cognitive disruption. This design allowed us to isolate the specific contribution of gravitational 115 

processing to physical reasoning. The Virtual Tools task minimises motor demands and 116 

performance depends primarily on tool selection and spatial placement. We therefore 117 

focused on high-level physical reasoning measures (success rate, mean number of 118 

attempts, and mean attempt duration) alongside strategy indices, operationalised as trial-to-119 

trial changes in tool selection and placement within each game, rather than movement 120 

kinematics. Because participants were not instructed explicitly to respond as quickly as 121 

possible, time per attempt could potentially reflect several processes, including deliberation, 122 

hesitation, task engagement, or individual speed-accuracy trade-offs, rather than better or 123 

worse reasoning. We therefore treated time per attempt as a secondary descriptive measure 124 

of attempt, rather than a direct index of performance efficiency. Better performance and 125 

more efficient strategies in the Sham condition compared to the GVS condition in gravity-126 

dependent games would suggest that an intact real-time experience is necessary for high-127 

level reasoning about physical dynamics. Alternatively, a similar or better performance in the 128 

GVS condition would suggest that disrupting the real-time experience of gravity does not 129 

selectively degrade reasoning performance. In our pre-registration, we hypothesised that 130 

when participants do not experience changes in gravity (sham stimulation), they would 131 

perform better in physical-reasoning tasks that are based on terrestrial gravity. 132 

In the second study, we aimed to test whether the hindering effects of real-time 133 

embodied experience can be reduced in case of reasoning about altered gravity. To that 134 

end, we manipulated the gravity within the Virtual Tools games, which required reasoning 135 

about hypogravity (0.5 g) or hypergravity (2 g) while each participant experienced GVS or 136 

Sham condition. This meant that object movements were influenced by gravity at either a 137 

higher or lower acceleration than Earth’s standard gravity (9.8 m/s²). We calculated the 138 

performance and strategy measures as in study 1. Then, for each measure, we computed a 139 

Gravity-Index (GI) by calculating the ratio between GVS-performance to Sham-performance 140 

(see Methods). This GI is a direct quantification of the effects of real-time vestibular noise on 141 

physical reasoning. The use of an index, calculated in the same way in both studies, allowed 142 

us to compare the relative effect of GVS across the two experiments rather than relying on 143 

differences in raw performance between the two participant samples. For success rate, a 144 

higher GI in study 2 compared to study 1 would suggest that the effects of the real-time 145

Discussion 319 

Our study tests a body-dependence in human high-level reasoning. Real-time 320 

vestibular perturbation via GVS was found to significantly impair participants’ reasoning 321 

about object motion under Earth gravity, yet the same perturbation was different when 322 

reasoning in altered-gravity environments. This paradoxical dual effect suggests that high-323 

level cognitive judgments about physics are not fixed computations but are dynamically 324 

modulated by the body’s ongoing sensory state. In essence, the brain’s internal “physics 325 

engine” appears to be embodied and context-sensitive—robustly tuned to terrestrial gravity 326 

under stable conditions, but capable of flexibly reconfiguring its assumptions when sensory 327 

cues signal a novel gravitational context. 328

Our data lend strong support to theories of embodied cognition, which posit that high-329 

level reasoning is deeply rooted in the sensorimotor systems of the body 35 . Rather than 330 

viewing physical reasoning as an abstract process operating in isolation, our findings show it 331 

to be exquisitely sensitive to the state of the body and its interaction with the environment. 332 

The evidence points to a context-dependent effect of GVS. Disrupting the vestibular sense 333 

degraded performance in the familiar 1g context, indicating that steady bodily feedback 334 

normally scaffolds accurate intuitive physics. Yet the same disruption produced reduced or 335 

relatively more favourable effects in altered-gravity context, implying that the brain can 336 

rapidly reweight sensory inputs to meet task demands 36 . This ability to reconfigure cognitive 337 

strategies based on bodily context reflects a form of neural plasticity on short timescales. It 338 

resonates with evidence that vestibular pathways are not only crucial for balance but also 339 

project widely to cognitive centers involved in spatial memory, attention, and executive 340 

function 27,37 . In effect, our “common sense” physical reasoning may emerge from neural 341 

computations that evolved for sensorimotor control. For example, humans possess an 342 

internalised sense of gravity through lifelong experience, and this embodiment of gravity 343 

guides both our actions and intuitions 38 . The present results show that this embodied 344 

knowledge is not rigid: when the ground truth changes (as in altered gravity), the mind-body 345 

system can adapt by drawing on alternative cues (visual, proprioceptive, or even erratic 346 

vestibular signals) to revise its predictions. Such sensorimotor integration ensures that 347 

cognition remains grounded and can cope with environmental novelty. More broadly, the 348 

context-dependent reversal we observed—where perturbing the body helps rather than 349 

hinders cognition under changed physics—underscores a core principle of embodied 350 

intelligence: mental processes exploit bodily feedback and will adjust their operation to 351 

maintain alignment with the external world. 352 

In contrast to spaceflight, parabolic flight or other terrestrial analogues that induce 353 

full-body alterations of gravitational input, the present study selectively perturbs vestibular 354 

signalling (Study 1) and combined vestibular and visual gravity cues (Study 2), while all other 355 

physiological systems remain anchored to a stable 1g environment. Although this may 356 

initially appear as a limitation relative to more ecologically valid gravitational transitions, 357 

evidence from parabolic flight and related analogue research suggests that fully veridical 358 

multisensory feedback is not always necessary to elicit adaptive effects. For example, 359 

Kunavar, et al. 39 demonstrated that applying gravitational compensation to only a segment of 360 

the arm can improve motor control in genuinely altered gravitational contexts, despite partial 361 

proprioceptive manipulation and multisensory conflict between proprioceptive and vestibular 362 

signals. This indicates that targeted or partial sensory perturbations can still engage adaptive 363

Our findings also invite interpretation through the lens of predictive coding and 371

these predictions, GVS allowed new sensorimotor information to recalibrate cognitive 394

the flexible physics reasoning observed here, with vestibular signals serving as a key 396 

“teaching signal” when our default assumptions about the physical world no longer hold. 397

concepts like gravity. This challenges the notion of intuitive physics as a fixed, encapsulated 468

Galvanic Vestibular Stimulation (GVS) 599 

Vestibular stimulation was delivered using a commercial stimulator (Good Vibrations 600 

Engineering Ltd., Nobleton, Ontario, Canada), controlled via LabVIEW. Carbon rubber 601 

electrodes (16 cm²) coated with electrode gel were placed bilaterally over the mastoid 602 

processes and secured with adhesive tape. Prior to electrode placement, the application 603 

sites were cleaned, and electrode gel was applied to minimize impedance. The stimulation 604 

protocol used a stochastic waveform, consisting of an alternating sum-of-sines voltage with 605 

dominant frequencies at 0.16, 0.32, 0.43, and 0.61 Hz. This stochastic GVS induces a sense 606 

of postural instability without producing consistent or directional illusory motion. To prevent 607 

compensatory effects from the non-stimulated side, stimulation was delivered binaurally. A 608 

Sham stimulation condition was also implemented, which produced similar skin sensations 609 

under the electrodes as the GVS, to control for non-vestibular specific effects. The Sham 610 

electrodes were placed on the left and right sides of the neck, approximately 5 cm below the 611 

GVS electrodes, which were positioned above the mastoid bones. The maximum intensity 612 

for both stimulation conditions was set at 1 mA. These parameters, similar to the ones used 613 

in previous studies 31 , were selected to optimize vestibular disruption 55,56 , eliciting a mild 614 

sensation of dizziness that dissipated immediately after stimulation 57 . These settings have 615 

been shown to mimic spatial disorientation, and suprathreshold GVS is considered an 616 

analog to spaceflight. In this experiment, the maximum duration of GVS was 14 minutes (14 617 

games × 1-minute maximum), which has been shown to be well-tolerated, with no lasting 618 

effects beyond the stimulation. The cross-study design allowed to maintain the total GVS 619 

exposure per participant below 20 minutes, in line with safety guidelines with the GVS 58 .620

Data and statistical analyses 622 

Data and statistical analyses were carried out with MATLAB R2020a (MathWorks), 623 

the R software environment (version 4.3.3) for statistical computing and graphics and Python 624 

(Jupyter Notebook version 6.4.1) for clustering analyses. 625 

Practice games were not included in the analysis. The completion of a minimum of 626 

10 games under each stimulation was set as a requirement for participants’ data to be 627 

included in the analysis. This threshold had been defined to ensure the data were sufficient 628 

to assess the effects of the GVS versus Sham stimulations. For each participant and each 629 

game, we quantified the success rate, the number of attempts needed and the time per 630 

attempt as performance measures. We then quantified the tool switching between attempts 631 

and the distance between tool positioning in successive attempts as strategy measures. 632 

For each study, for our three performance measures, we conducted one-sided 633 

independent t-tests for game level comparisons under the GVS and Sham stimulations and 634 

one-sided paired t-tests for comparing participants’ performance under each stimulation. For 635 

strategy measures, we used two-sided t-tests as we had no direction in our hypotheses. 636 

Each game was treated as a distinct physical-reasoning problem, and the t-tests were used 637 

to localize the games in which the stimulation effect was expressed, not to compare games 638 

with one another or to draw broad conclusions from isolated contrasts. Therefore, in this 639 

case, no multiple-comparison corrections are required. 640 

For investigating the impact of the stimulations at each gravity impact level, we 641 

conducted mixed-effect models tailored to the distribution of the dependent variables. Two 642 

outlier games, ‘Spiky’ and ‘SeeSaw’, were excluded from these analyses. We used gravity 643 

impact level and stimulation type as fixed factors and participant and game as random 644 

effects. The categorical predictors were sum-coded prior to analysis. For the performance 645 

measures, we used a logistic regression for success, a zero-truncated negative binomial 646 

model for number of attempts and a linear regression applied on a log-transformed variable 647 

for time. For the strategy measures, we conducted an ordered beta regression model for the 648 

switching rate, and a linear regression on log transformed distance. Pairwise contrasts 649 

between stimulation type were restricted to a single a priori comparison within each gravity 650 

impact level. Consequently, no multiple-comparison adjustment was required. For Study 2, 651 

we tested whether hypogravity and hypergravity should be analysed separately by adding 652 

gravity level (0.5g vs 2g) to the mixed-effects models. Gravity level was not a significant 653 

predictor for any performance or strategy measure, and this was consistent with previous 654 

Virtual Tools work showing no reliable difference between hypo- and hypergravity effects. 655 

On this basis, we aggregated the two conditions as “altered gravity” for the main analyses. 656

Dirichlet Process Gaussian Mixture Model 717 

The distance and tool switching measures reduce each participant’s behaviour to two 718 

averages; they therefore do not capture the multivariate structure of spatial placements, 719 

whether participants move between distinct placement strategies across successive 720 

attempts, or whether the overall placement distributions differ systematically between 721 

stimulation conditions. To quantify strategic switching behaviour, we employed a Dirichlet 722 

Process Gaussian Mixture Model approach adapted from Allen, et al. 20 . The Dirichlet 723 

Process Gaussian Mixture Model is well suited to characterize the spatial and sequential 724 

organisation of tool-use strategies because it identifies strategy clusters directly from x/y 725

transitions between spatial strategies across attempts (in relation to the environment in the 727

stimulation condition and trial level, we fitted separate mixture models that captured the 729

identified strategy. 731 

Strategic switches were defined as transitions between different spatial strategies on 732 

consecutive attempts within the same game. A switch occurred when the cluster differed 733 

between two sequential attempts by the same participant. Given the probabilistic nature of 734 

the Dirichlet Process, we repeated the entire clustering procedure across 1,000 iterations. 735 

For each iteration, we fitted a logistic regression model with switch probability as the 736 

dependent variable and both stimulation type (GVS vs. Sham) and attempt number as 737 

predictors, extracting the p-value for the stimulation type effect. From this bootstrap analysis, 738 

we identified the iteration that produced results closest to the modal p-value distribution by 739 

creating kernel density estimates of these stimulation effect p-values across all 1,000 740 

iterations. We selected the iteration whose p-value was nearest to the peak density value, 741 

representing the most probable statistical outcome under our clustering approach. We report 742 

the results from this selected iteration and its corresponding logistic regression model. 743

Leave-one-out Kernel density analysis 745 

While the Dirichlet Process Gaussian Mixture Model enables to assess strategic 746 

switching behaviour under the GVS and Sham stimulations, it does not address whether 747 

participants adopted systematically different strategies across stimulation conditions. To 748 

determine whether the placement patterns contain sufficient condition-specific structure to 749 

classify stimulation condition above chance, we implemented a leave-one-out kernel density 750 

classification approach based on the [x,y] coordinates of each attempt. For each participant, 751 

we excluded their data and used the remaining participants' placements to construct two 752 

separate two-dimensional kernel density estimates (KDEs): one for the GVS group and one 753 

for the Sham group. These KDEs captured the spatial distribution of tool placements within 754 

each condition. 755 

Next, we estimated the probability density of the left-out participant's placements 756 

under each of the two KDEs. These estimates were used to compute a relative likelihood, 757 

reflecting how much more likely a given placement was under the correct group distribution 758 

compared to the incorrect one. Relative likelihood values were binarized into classification 759 

accuracy scores: a value of 1 was assigned if the placement was more likely under the 760 

correct group, and 0 otherwise. Accuracy scores were then averaged across all trials for 761 

each participant to produce a single accuracy value. 762

This control analysis was run over 1,000 iterations, each time generating KDEs from the 765 

permuted data and computing classification accuracy in the same leave-one-out manner. 766 

The resulting distribution of accuracies served as a baseline against which the observed 767
