# Abstract 22 

The hippocampus is characterized by a stereotypical macroscopic structure, where the nuclei 23 

are densely and heterogeneously packed among different subregions of the hippocampus. De- 24 

spite the fact that tissue-specific cellular organization has been implicated in neural function, it 25 

has been technically challenging to quantitatively analyze mesoscopic cellular organization in the 26 

hippocampus due to its high cellular density. To overcome this technical hurdle, we developed 27 

Computational Biophysical Histomorphometry Software (CBHS), an automated image-analysis 28 

pipeline, aimed at quantifying nuclear shape and the order of the cellular ensemble in high- 29 

density areas. When applied to the subfields of hippocampus, we found that denser regions, 30 

most notably the dentate gyrus, were the most positionally, but least orientationally ordered. 31 

Nuclear shape exhibited a dependence on the local environment in a packing-dependent man- 32 

ner. This association was cell-type specific, with neurons, but not astrocytes displaying nuclear 33 

shape that varied with neighbour proximity, although astrocytes demonstrated greater intrinsic 34 

shape variance. The results reveal the presence of reproducible mesoscale cell packing order 35 

in hippocampal tissue, and are consistent with a nucleus-driven mechanical coupling between 36 

neighbouring cells. The present study provides a quantitative framework with which to under- 37 

stand mesoscopic tissue organization, thus enabling the formulation of testable hypotheses for 38 

future investigation. 39

# INTRODUCTION 42 

Different brain areas show intrinsic differences in their overall topological properties. These prop- 43 

erties emerge from the collective organization of individual cells. However, such organization 44 

remains sparsely quantified and rarely connected to the functional properties of the respective 45 

area. Recently, the functional significance of collective cellular organization in a brain area- 46 

dependent fashion has gained traction, including its potential role as a source of dysfunction in 47 

disease (Zhang et al., 2023; Das et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024; Ferme et al., 2025). The hip- 48 

pocampus is one of the more investigated areas in terms of its cellular organization (Bayer et al., 49 

1982; West et al., 1991; Keuker et al., 2003). It comprises three main subfields, Cornu Ammo- 50 

nis 1 (CA1), CA3, and the dentate gyrus (DG), that differ in function and cellular organization. 51 

At the macroscopic level, disease-induced topological alterations in the hippocampus are well 52 

documented, predominantly through tissue autopsy and functional magnetic resonance imaging 53 

(fMRI). Reduced functional connectivity, altered graph properties of the neural network, as well 54 

as changes in the density of hippocampal tissue have been linked to cognitive decline, mem- 55 

ory deficits and neurological diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease (Allen et al., 2007; Wang 56 

et al., 2006; Tijms et al., 2013; Berron et al., 2021). Conversely, molecular disease mecha- 57 

nisms and targeted treatments are predominantly investigated at the microscopic scale, where 58 

individual cell morphology, subcellular structure and molecular environment are directly accessi- 59 

ble. What remains poorly understood is how cellular-level changes connect to the macroscopic 60 

whole-tissue neuroimaging signatures. Fixed-tissue histological assessments offer a window to 61 

assess mesoscale changes between these two scales, thereby providing the resolution to quan- 62 

tify cellular ensembles while reflecting the aggregate micro-mesoscopic structural properties that 63 

shape macroscopic signatures. Bridging all three scales is essential for translating cellular and 64 

molecular findings into interpretable macroscopic signatures of disease. 65 

At the mesoscale, cell packing and density have been shown to be valuable qualitative read- 66 

outs of broader changes in hippocampal structure across several disease models (Wishart et 67 

al., 2010; Baglietto-Vargas et al., 2010; Arranz et al., 2014; Schoene-Bake et al., 2014). It has 68 

thus been established that the significance of mesoscopic tissue organization is associated with 69 

brain dysfunction. However, this association is primarily established through qualitative or semi- 70 

quantitative methodologies, without the establishment of a link to other scales. It is believed that 71 

mature post-mitotic neurons in the CNS do not migrate or move, outside of a few microns over 72 

prolonged time periods (Rakic, 1974). However, when many tightly packed neurons move by 73 

a few microns, the cumulative effect on the spatial organization of the collective tissue can be 74 

significant. For example, stereological studies have shown that the number of granule cells and 75 

their packing density in the DG increase from juvenile to adult life, and that age-related changes 76 

in subfield volume and neuron number can be partially decoupled (Bayer et al., 1982; West et 77 

al., 1991; Keuker et al., 2003). These results indicate that cellular spatial organization might 78 

be more dynamically altered than previously assumed, not following a fixed trajectory, but rather 79 

reacting to external circumstances, such as experiences and disease. 80 

To investigate such a dynamic system at the mesoscale, different metrics can be used, which 81 

all relate to the distance between cells, their angles and their shape. Metrics, such as the 82 

radial distribution function or local nematic order, enable the quantification of the spatial cellular 83 

organization in tissue. Furthermore, they can provide insights into the relationship between the 84 

spatial organization and the density of cells in the area of interest. Intriguingly, hippocampal 85 

neurons exhibit very high nuclear volume fractions within their cell bodies (Bayer et al., 1982; 86 

West et al., 1991; Keuker et al., 2003). Nuclei in these areas are also known to dynamically 87 

change their morphology based on disease and functional states (Das et al., 2023). Tissues 88 

with similarly high nuclear volume fraction and similarly tight packing, such as the zebrafish 89

However, a unified quantitative framework for the mesoscale analysis of hippocampal structure 99 

is currently lacking. 100 

To address this technical and knowledge gap and to investigate the relationship between cel- 101 

lular packing order and density in the highly-packed hippocampal tissue, we developed the Com- 102 

putational Biophysical Histomorphometry Software (CBHS). CBHS is an all-in-one pipeline for 103 

the analysis of confocal images, integrating background correction, CellPose-based nuclear seg- 104 

mentation, 3D reconstruction, fluorescence intensity correction and automated marker-positive 105 

cell filtering. It also provides a unified framework for quantifying the nuclear shape, density, and 106 

cellular packing order of nuclei across hippocampal subfields (Pachitariu et al., 2025; Kleinberg 107 

et al., 2022; Weigert et al., 2020). Using CBHS, we characterized the mesoscale organization of 108 

the hippocampus quantitatively and found that, similar to the zebrafish retina, cells in the mouse 109 

hippocampus, show an order in their cellular organization beyond a theoretical density thresh- 110 

old. Furthermore, the shape of the nuclei changes under the influence of the local environmental 111 

structure. Ultimately, this provides a basis for analysing dynamic and potentially functionally rel- 112 

evant changes in the cellular organization of these neuronal ensembles. These findings revealed 113 

the principles for investigating the trajectories of brain subregion-specific cellular and tissue or- 114 

ganization, which could be critical for tissue-specific function and plasticity. 115

Mice handling, tissue processing and immunohistochemistry 117 

All procedures relating to mouse care and treatment were approved by the Government of Sax- 118

months were used. Mice were euthanised with a lethal dose of sodium pentobarbital (WDT). 120 

After death had been confirmed, the animals were transcardially perfused with ice-cold 1× PBS, 121 

followed by 50 mL of freshly prepared 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; Sigma-Aldrich) in PBS. Fol- 122

# RESULTS 225 

To investigate the mesoscale organization of tissues, it is necessary to be able to resolve in- 226 

dividual cells/nuclei while simultaneously quantifying their spatial relationships. Hippocampal 227 

subfields present several inherent challenges for automated nuclear analysis. First, the uneven 228 

cellular distribution produces non-uniform background intensity and autofluorescence (i). Sec- 229 

ond, the tight packing of nuclei make it difficult to conduct instance segmentation, particularly 230 

where adjacent objects overlap (ii). Third, optical sectioning truncates the polar caps of nuclei in 231 

3D because intensity in the surrounding plane exceeds that at the top and bottom of each body 232 

(iii), leading to systematic under-representation of nuclear volume. To address these limitations 233 

while enabling quantitative analysis of the spatial tissue organization across diverse cellular en- 234 

sembles, we developed CBHS, a pipeline that combines per-slice background correction across 235 

the Z -stack, 2D segmentation of DAPI-stained nuclear images with a CellPose CPSAM model 236 

fine-tuned using hand-annotated nuclei in the DG, mask-guided edge enhancement of the raw 237 

DAPI volume prior to 3D reconstruction with Stardist 3D, and an in-house mask-cleaning and 238 

interpolation step termed Yukimatsu (Figure 1, for an overview). 239

Subsequently, we addressed the second challenge(ii), instance separation in densely packed 271 

tissue. Initially, we applied CellPose for segmenting the DAPI-stained nuclei, (Figure 3) (Stringer 272 

et al., 2021). Although the original CellPose Segment Anything (CPSAM) model was capable of 273 

segregating nuclei fairly well, we observed limitations in denser regions. To improve accuracy, 274 

the CPSAM model was further fine-tuned for CBHS using 4,249 hand-drawn nuclei from nine 275 

training images. The model was then benchmarked against the untuned baseline on five held- 276 

out images containing 1,446 annotated nuclei, using precision, recall, and the F-score (Figure 277 

3A–D). Precision is the proportion of detected nuclei that matched a hand-annotated nucleus 278 

and penalizes false detections, while recall is the proportion of hand-annotated nuclei recovered 279 

by the model and penalizes misses. The F-score is their harmonic mean of two and is domi- 280 

nated by whichever is lower. This last property matches the cost structure of the downstream 281 

topology analysis, in which false detections (splitting a single nucleus into two) and missed nu- 282 

clei propagate symmetrically into the different topological metrics used (see below). Fine-tuning 283 

consistently improved every metric: precision rose from 0.78 to 0.86, recall from 0.86 to 0.89, 284 

and F-score from 0.81 to 0.87, with the CBHS-tuned model superior on all five validation images 285 

for precision and the F-score (rank-biserial rrb = 1 .00 ) and on four of the five for recall ( rrb = 0 .87 ) 286 

(Figure 3B-D) (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p = 0 .0625 for precision, and F-score and p = 0 .125 for 287 

recall). 288 

Following 2D segmentation, the boundaries of DAPI-positive ROIs were enhanced in each 289 

Z-stack slice based on their corresponding masks. This procedure increased the apparent sepa- 290 

ration between adjacent nuclei, thereby facilitating downstream three-dimensional instance sep- 291 

aration. These edge-enhanced DAPI-stained nuclear image stacks were then reconstructed with 292 

Stardist 3D (Figure 3E) (Pachitariu et al., 2025; Kleinberg et al., 2022; Weigert et al., 2020). A 293 

qualitative assessment revealed that residual artifacts could distort the resulting 3D masks. To 294 

account for this, we introduced the Yukimatsu algorithm, a label-wise post-processing routine ap- 295 

plied in two stages. The first stage restores the axially truncated nuclear caps (the issue iii): First, 296 

each labeled object is up-sampled along the z-axis by trilinear interpolation. After which, each 297 

object is Gaussian-smoothed, and subjected to anisotropic morphological closing with a larger 298 

structuring element along the z-axis than within the imaging plane. Lastly, they are returned 299 

to the native slice spacing by maximum-projection downsampling that preserves the recovered 300 

caps. The second stage resolves under-segmented, touching nuclei: an Euclidean distance 301 

transform seeds a watershed at the internal distance maxima, after which a set of purely geo- 302 

metric guardrails — a minimum child-volume (size) filter, a sphericity/elongation criterion, and a 303 

neck-thickness criterion at the shared boundary — merges spurious fragments back into single 304 

objects or filters them out, thereby suppressing false divisions. 305

# DISCUSSION 583 

In the present study, to investigate the mesoscale structure of the hippocampus, we developed 584 

an automated image-analysis pipeline, termed CBHS. Its background-correction step reduces 585 

background variability across the image and thereby enables reliable per-ROI background nor- 586 

malization. CellPose-based refined nuclear segmentation achieves automated delineation com- 587 

parable to hand-drawn ROIs, while Stardist provides high-quality 3D reconstruction. However, 588 

our analyses revealed that 3D reconstruction adds little information beyond 2D for group-based 589 

comparisons of nuclear shape. After segmenting each nuclei, identifying marker-positive cells 590 

is essential for understanding how individual cell types contribute to the mesoscale structure. 591 

The histogram-based filtering implemented in CBHS achieves an accuracy comparable to that 592 

of manually-annotated ROIs. Together, these components allow CBHS to perform automated 593 

analysis of high-quality images from densely packed brain regions, such as the hippocampus. 594 

Building on this pipeline, we quantitatively characterized the mesoscale organization of the 595 

hippocampus through its positional and orientational order. Our findings revealed that the cel- 596 

lular arrangement of the hippocampal subfields differ intrinsically, with the denser regions, most 597 

notably the DG, being the most positionally ordered. Intriguingly, the orientational order followed 598 

the opposite trend, although with a much lower effect size. This suggests that local nematic or- 599 

der is not a reliable indicator of the nuclear organization in the hippocampus, probably because 600 

its orientation is also dependent on nuclear shape, which ultimately reduces effect sizes to neg- 601 

ligible levels. Notably, the increase in positional order emerged above a statistically resolved 602 

density breakpoint, located near ≈ 0.4 in the denser DG and CA1 area. This led us to the hy- 603 

pothesis, that above this breakpoint, the density-driven constraint of cells on each other drives 604 

order. In line with this idea, examining nuclear shape in relation to the local environment re- 605 

vealed a scale-dependent picture: the window-averaged nuclear shape became more circular at 606 

higher density, yet individual nuclei were smaller, less rounded, and more elongated the closer 607 

their neighbours were. This apparent paradox can be reconciled by recognizing that the two 608 

measurements are aggregated at different scales and conditioned on different quantities. The 609 

window average is dominated by the majority of moderately spaced nuclei, which pack more 610 

uniformly, and therefore appear rounder on average, as a region fills. In contrast, the elongation 611 

is concentrated in the minority of nuclei in closest contact, where direct crowding is greatest. 612 

This dependence on the local environment prompted us to ask whether the relationship between 613 

environment and nuclear shape is cell type specific. By comparing neurons with astrocytes, 614 

which themselves differ fundamentally in the variance of their nuclear shape, we found that only 615 

neurons exhibited a dependence of nuclear shape on the local environment (Figure 7). Several 616 

features of these findings strike us as particularly intriguing. First, the emergence of the cellu- 617 

lar packing order above a statistically resolved density breakpoint, ≈ 0.4 in the more ordered 618 

DG and CA1 regions, is reminiscent of the density- or packing-driven transitions described for 619 

jammed and active matter, in which global spatial order only arises once a critical local density 620 

is exceeded (Kim et al., 2024; Zaccone, 2025). This breakpoint (a nuclear coverage of ≈ 0.4 621 

in these regions) sits well below the saturating coverage of the tissue ( ≈ 0.6) suggests that hip- 622 

19

pocampal nuclear arrangements are not merely passively packed. They may reorganize their 623 

mesoscale structure once crowding becomes sufficient to constrain neighbouring nuclei, a be- 624 

haviour that would not be expected from cells positioned independently of one another. Second, 625 

the cell-type specificity of the shape–environment relationship is equally striking: although astro- 626 

cytes display greater intrinsic variance in nuclear shape, only neurons adapt their nuclear shape 627 

with the local environment. This dissociation argues against the coupling being merely a trivial 628 

consequence of denser regions containing systematically different cells, and instead points to 629 

a genuine, cell type-restricted responsiveness of neuronal nuclei to their surroundings. Taken 630 

together, these observations support the idea of a mechanical interaction between neighbouring 631 

nuclei that contribute to the emergence of mesoscale order. However, we emphasize that this is 632 

only one of several possible explanations. The same correlations could arise from cell-intrinsic 633 

or developmental programmes that co-vary with regional density, or from differences in base- 634 

line deformability between neurons and astrocytes, even in the absence of any direct mechan- 635 

ical coupling. Distinguishing among these possibilities will require perturbative or longitudinal 636 

approaches rather than the static, observational data presented here. Nevertheless, because 637 

nuclear shape and cellular deformability have been implicated in neuronal function and gene 638 

regulation (Wittmann et al., 2009), the hypothesis of a mechanical contribution to mesoscale 639 

order remains an attractive and experimentally feasible hypothesis for future investigations. The 640 

phenotype descriptions presented in this study could now be used to investigate mesoscale dif- 641 

ferences across disease conditions, as well as for functional investigations, for example in an 642 

evolutionary context. 643 

Limitations of the study 644 

CBHS is intended as an image-analysis tool for investigating the mesoscale structure of the 645 

hippocampal subfields. Although it enables accurate quantification of topological order, marker 646 

intensity, and nuclear shape, the present study establishes neither a causal relationship between 647 

these features nor their biological relevance. It likewise does not address whether mesoscale 648 

order is able to adapt across conditions such as ageing or disease; the organisation reported 649 

here may instead represent a static, phenomenological description of these neuronal ensem- 650 

bles. Resolving these questions, and in particular distinguishing functional order from incidental 651 

structure, will require future studies. 652
