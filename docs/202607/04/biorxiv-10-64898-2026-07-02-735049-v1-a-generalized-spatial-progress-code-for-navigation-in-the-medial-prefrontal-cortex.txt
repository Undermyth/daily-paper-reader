SUMMARY 21 

Goal -directed navigation requires an internal estimate of progress toward a desired outcome, yet how the 22 

brain constructs and represents such a signal remains unclear. Here, we designed a multi -route navigation 23 

paradigm with geometrically distinct paths and imaged medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) activity as mice 24 

performed goal -directed route choice behavior. We found that a substantial fraction of mPFC neurons fired 25 

at consistent positions along a normalized start -to -goal axis, independent of path geometry, running 26 

direction, and task context. At the ensemble level, mPFC activity reliably decoded spatial progress but 27 

carried minimal information about path identity. This progress code emerged with task familiarity and 28 

required input from the hippocampal CA1. Inactivation of either mPFC or CA1 →mPFC projection did not 29 

disrupt learned path preference but impaired navigational fluency in trained mice. These findings reveal a 30 

hippocampal -prefrontal mechanism for representing abstract, context -generalized spatial progress within a 31 

goal -directed navigational episode, supporting fluent navi gation across variable routes. 32

INTRODUCTION 38 

Efficient goal -directed spatial navigation in natural environments requires individuals to recognize 39 

allocentric space, choose optimal paths and estimate relative progress from start to goa l. Previous studies 40 

have identified the hippocamp us, entorhinal cortex and related brain structures as core regions to build an 41 

internal allocentric cognitive map (Behrens et al., 2018; Buzsáki and Moser, 2013; Epstein et al., 2017; 42 

O'Keefe and Dostrovsky, 1971; Schiller et al., 2015) . The concerted activity of place cells, grid cells, border 43 

cells and corner cells within this circuit dynamically represents an individual’s precise location (Behrens et 44 

al., 2018; Fyhn et al., 2004; Moser et al., 2008; Moser et al., 2017; O'K eefe and Dostrovsky, 1971; Solstad 45 

et al., 2008; Sun et al., 2024) . The retrosplenial cortex and parahippocampal cortex have also been 46 

recognized as essential for scene recognition and landmark anchoring (Epstein, 2008; Yoder et al., 2011) .47 

In addition, several brain regions, including the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), the hippocampus (HPC), 48 

and the secondary motor cortex have been proposed to function in route choice, as evidenced by biased 49

However, efficient navigation in natural environments with multiple paths also requires individuals to 51 

estimate relative progress from start to goal, raising an unsolved issue: “How much have I accomplis hed?” 52 

The mPFC is an important high -level cognitive region critical for spatial navigation. Previous studies 53 

have revealed that mPFC receives anatomical projections from the hippocampus (Eust on et al., 2012; Ge 54 

et al., 2023; Jay and Witter, 1991; Qiu et al., 2024; Sauer et al., 2022; Spellman et al., 2015; Tavares and 55 

Tort, 2022) and that its disruption impairs spatial navigation in both rodents (de Bruin et al., 1994; Granon 56 

and Poucet, 1995) and humans (Ciaramelli, 2008) . In addition, mPFC neurons exhibit diverse 57 

representations of task space, including current spatial location (Ma et al., 2023; Zielinski et al., 2019) , goal 58 

locations (Hok et al., 2005; Vogel et al., 2022) , specific trajectory routes (Fujisawa et al., 2008; Ito et al., 59 

2015) , task sequences (Tang et al., 2023) , and value -based decision making (Veselic et al., 2025) . However, 60 

it remains unclear how mPFC contributes to route choice behavior in naturalisti c space with multiple paths 61 

and whether it estimates the relative navigational progress. 62 

Here, we designed a route choice paradigm that incorporates multiple geometrically distinct paths to 63 

mimic real -world spatial navigation . By imaging mPFC neural activi ty when mice were freely navigating a 64 

multi -path maze, we addressed: (1) whether mPFC is involved in route choice behavior in multi -path space; 65 

(2) whether mPFC estimates the relative navigational progress; and (3) the origin of the progress 66 

representation . Our results reveal that the mPFC provides a spatial -progress scaffold: not a map of physical 67 

space, and not a simple path selector, but an abstract representation of goal -directed advancement, 68 

illustrating how the brain integrates diverse spatial experie nces into a unified internal metric of progress for 69 

flexible and efficient spatial navigation .70

RESULTS 72 

The mPFC supports navigational fluency but not path preference of learned route choice behavior 73 

To investigate route choice behavior in mice within a naturalistic and complex spatial environment, we 74 

established a route choice paradigm equipped with two water ports and four geometrically different paths: 75 

a long curve, a zigzag path, a direct path, and a short curve ( Figures 1A and S1A). Mice were motivat ed to 76 

select different paths and navigate back and forth between the two water ports. Different paths represent 77 

distinct travel strategies: for instance, the direct path provide s the shortest and straightest trajectory, 78 

whereas the long curve is the longes t and most circuitous . The whole task lasted 15 sessions (15 79 

min/session, 1 session/day) in which the proportion of incomplete trials and trial duration decreased 80 

gradually (Figure s S1B and S1C ). Route choice behavior showed two relatively stable stages du ring training, 81 

which were defined as the naive (sessions 3 –5) and trained (sessions 13 –15) stages ( Figure s 1A and S1B–82 

S1D ). Compared with the naive stage , mice in the trained stage completed more trials with a lower 83 

proportion of incomplete trials, shorter trial duration, and reduced choice time in the route choice regions in 84 

a session (Fig ures 1B–1E and S1E–S1G ). Trained mice also displayed a lower proportion of random pause, 85 

indic ating enhanced task execution stability (Fig ures 1F, S1H, and S1I ). I mportantly, whereas route choice 86 

was uniformly distributed during the naive stage, trained mice exhibited a significant bias toward the direct 87 

path while eschewing the long curve (Fig ures 1G and 1H ), resulting in a higher path preference index 88 

(Figure 1I). Together, these results indicate that training -induced task familiarity optimizes route choice 89 

behavior, supporting efficient goal -directed spatial navigation. 90 

To examine the role of the mPFC in route choice behavior, we bilaterally expressed the inhibitory 91 

DREADD human Gi -coupled M4 muscarinic receptor (hM4Di ‑mCherry) in the mPFC and administered the 92 

clozapine N -oxide (CNO; 10 mg/kg, i.p.) before each task session to inactivate mPFC activity ( Figure 1J). 93 

While no difference was observed between the saline and CNO groups in the naive stage, mice treated 94 

with CNO completed fewer trials, exhibited a higher proportion of random pause and longer route choice 95 

time in the trained stage (Fig ures 1K–1M ). Notably, the proportion of incomplete trials, path preference, and 96 

inter -trial interval remained unaffected after inactivation of the mPFC (Fig ures 1N–1P, S1J, and S1 K). Thus, 97 

the mPFC primarily facilitates fluent navigation rather than determ ining detailed path selection. 98

To decipher how the mPFC encodes spatial navigation in a complex multi -path environment, we expressed 101 

the fluorescent calcium indicator GCaMP6s in mPFC excitatory neurons and imaged their activity using a 102 

miniature fluorescence microscope in both naive and trained stages ( Fig ures 2A and S2A –S2F; Video S1). 103 

Notably, a subset of neurons was found to maintain a stable and sequential firing pattern across 104 

co nsecutive trials, regardless of the running direction or the chosen path (Fig ure 2B; Video S2). To compare 105 

neural activity across trajectories with different geometries, we linearized each trial into normalized spatial -106 

progress bins, thereby registering mP FC activity by relative spatial progress rather than absolute spatial 107 

position (Fig ures S2G and S2H ; STAR Methods ). We next compared the progress -aligned response 108 

profiles between two running directions and found a clear activation band of the leftward trials in the trained 109 

stage, which resulted in a clear diagonal correlation coefficient band that was absent in the naive stage 110 

(Fig ures S3A and S3B ). The correlation coefficients of neuronal activity between two running directions 111 

were signi ficantly higher after training (Fig ure S3C) and exceeded those obtained after reversing or shuffling 112 

the activity in the leftward trials (Fig ure S3D). Similar increases were also observed across different paths 113 

and across repeated trials of the same path t ype both within and across days (Fig ures S3E and S3F ).114 

Together, these results indicate that training drives the emergence of a progress -aligned representation in 115 

the mPFC that generalizes across running directions and geometrically distinct paths. 116 

The hig hly consistent neuronal activity across different paths led us to hypothesize that the mPFC 117 

encodes spatial progress, distinct from elapsed time or distance to goal. To test this, we aligned the activity 118 

dynamics of individual neurons according to three va riables: elapsed time from trial onset, distance to goal, 119 

or spatial progress ( normalized distance from onset to offset ) ( Fig ures 2C and S4). Alignment by elapsed 120 

time caused the responses to shift toward trial offset as trial duration increased, whereas a lignment by 121 

distance to goal caused them to shift toward trial onset as path length increased. By contrast, alignment by 122 

spatial progress resulted in similar relative peak positions across path geometries and running directions 123 

(Fig ures 2C and S4). We next quantified response consistency across the three alignment conditions using 124 

a jitter -based analysis ( STAR Methods ). In the trained, but not naive, stage, jitter was lowest when activity 125 

was aligned by spatial progress compared with the other two condition s (Fig ures 2D and 2E ). Thus, the 126 

activity patterns of these neurons were better explained by a representation of spatial progress from start 127 

to goal, rather than by elapsed time or distance to goal. We therefore termed these neurons spatial -progress 128 

cells. 129 

To identify the spatial -progress cells , we selected neurons that showed place -cell -like tuning within 130 

individual paths and peaked at similar normalized positions across geometrically distinct paths (Fig ure S5A;131 

STAR Methods ). Using these criteria, we iden tified a large proportion of spatial -progress cells in the trained 132 

stage. Compared with alignment by elapsed time or distance to goal, alignment by spatial progress 133 

produced narrower response fields and higher correlations between single -trial activity and trial -averaged 134 

activity (Fig ures 2F and 2G ), indicating that spatial progress best accounted for the activity of these neurons. 135 

Training significantly increased the proportion of spatial -progress cells in both running directions, 136 

comprising approximately 25% of the recorded population in the trained stage (Fig ures 2H and 2I ). These 137 

neurons showed no significant spatial clustering within the imaging field of view (Fig ures S5B and S5C ). 138 

Although their progress fields were primarily clustered near trial offset in the naive stage and became 139 

enriched near both trial onset and offset in the trained stage, they spanned the full navigational trajectory 140 

(Fig ures 2J and 2K ). Progress -field width was approximately 15% of the normalized path in the trained 141 

stage a nd was significantly greater than in the naive stage (Fig ure 2L). In addition, the proportion of spatial -142 

progress cells active in both running directions increased after training and significantly exceeded chance 143 

levels, whereas no significant bidirectiona l overlap was observed in the naive stage (Fig ures 2M and 2N ). 144 

Together, these results demonstrate that the mPFC encodes spatial progress at the single -cell level, and 145 

that training increases both the prevalence and direction -generalization of spatial -prog ress cells. 146

To test whether spatial progress could be reliably decoded from mPFC activity, we trained linear 168

Overall, these findings demonstrate that mPFC activity encodes a sequential, route -invariant representation 181

The mPFC spatial -progress coding generalizes across routes an d contexts 184 

In our paradigm, mice exhi bited a strong path preference in the trained stage, with relatively few choices of 185 

the long curve, raising the possibility that uneven path sampling could limit the assessment of spatial -186 

progress generalization. To add ress this, we modified the paradigm into a no -choice task, in which only one 187 

path was accessible per session and the open path alternated across four consecutive sessions (Figure 188 

4A). In this setting, the normalized peak positions of individual neurons rem ained aligned across paths and 189 

differed from shuffled controls ( Figure 4B). Correlations of peak positions and binned neuronal activity were 190 

significantly higher across path pairs and running directions than in shuffled datasets ( Figures 4C, 4D and 191 

S10 A–S1 0C ). Using the same criteria as in the free -choice task, we found that approximately 16% of 192 

neurons showed consistent spatial -progress coding across paths and sessions ( Figures 4E, 4F, S10 D, and 193 

S10E ). Moreover, SVM models trained on trials from three paths accurately decoded spatial progress in 194 

the remaining path, with significantly lower decoding error than shuffled controls ( Figure 4G). These results 195 

indicate that mPFC spatial -progress coding generalizes across paths even when path availability is 196 

ex perimentally constrained. 197

progress cells were enriched before the turn point ( Figure 4N), possibly reflecting the behavioral salience 204 

of this decision p oint, a pattern not observed in the route choice task ( Figure 2K). Consistent with the route 205

CA1→mPFC inactivation impairs navigational efficiency and progress coding in mPFC 216 

Because spatial -progress recognition during navigation may depend on cognitive map -based self -location ,217 

we asked whether hippocampal input to the mPFC contributes to this coding. To selectively manipulate the 218 

CA1→mPFC projection while imagin g mPFC activity, we expressed hM4Di -mCherry in the ventral 219 

hippocampal CA1 neurons projecting to the mPFC and GCaMP6s in mPFC excitatory neurons ( Figure s 5A220 

and 5B; STAR Methods ). After 15 training sessions, mice received three consecutive sessions of sali ne 221 

followed by three consecutive sessions of CNO administration to inhibit the CA1→mPFC projection ( Figure 222 

5A). Inactivating the CA1→mPFC projection reduced the number of trials, increased the proportion of 223 

random pause, and prolonged route choice time ( Fi gure s 5C–5E). In contrast, the path preference ,224 

proportion of incomplete trials , and inter -trial interval were not significantly affected (Figure s 5F–5I), 225 

resembling the behavioral effects of direct mPFC inactivation (Figure s 1K–1P ). Thus, CA1→mPFC input 226 

supports navigational fluency rather than path preference in the route choice task. 227 

We next assessed whether CA1→mPFC inactivation altered spatial -progress coding in mPFC (Figure 228 

6A) . At the population level, progress -aligned activity became less correlate d across running directions, 229 

across different path types, and across repeated trials of the same path type within and across days 230 

(Figure s S11A –C). At the single -cell level, some spatial -progress cells maintained similar peak positions 231 

after CNO treatment, whereas others showed unstable response profiles across trials and paths ( Figure 232 

6B), resulting in higher response jitter and loss of the progress cell identity ( Figure s 6C and 6D). In addition, 233 

the proportion of spatial -progress cells was markedly decreased ( Figure s 6D and S11 D), and approximately 234 

half of the previously identified spatial -progress cells lost progress coding following CA1→mPFC 235 

inactivation ( Figure s 6D and 6E). However, the overall distribution and width of progress fields among the 236 

remaining neurons were not significantly changed ( Figure 6F). These results indicate that CA1→mPFC 237 

input is required for stable spatial -progress coding in mPFC. 238 

Consistently, neural manifold analysis revealed that CA1→mPFC inactivation disrupted the sequent ial 239 

ring -like geometry characteristic of progress trajectories observed during saline sessions ( Figure s 6G, 6H,240 

S11 E, and S11F ). Progress stages that were clearly segregated under saline became intermixed after CNO 241 

treatment, and the ratio of intra -stage to inter -stage distances increased significantly ( Figure s 6I and S11 G). 242 

Decoding error for spatial progress also increased after CNO treatment in both running directions, whereas 243 

path decoding remained near chance and was not significantly changed ( Figure s 6J, 6K, S11 H, and S11 I). 244 

Together, these findings show that CA1→mPFC input is necessary for maintaining a coherent and 245 

sequential mPFC representation of spatial progress, thereby supporting fluent goal -directed navigation 246 

without directly determining path choice. 247

DISCUSSION 249 

Efficient goal -directed navigation requires animals not only to locate themselves in space, but also to 250 

monitor their progress from start to goal. However, how the brain constructs and represents such a signal 251 

remains unclear. Here, using a custom -designed multi -route navigation paradigm , we identify spatial -252 

progress cells in the mPFC that fire at consistent positions along a normalized start ‑to ‑goal axis , whether 253 

the path is long or short, straight or curved. Critically, the formation of these cells depends on training and 254 

their maintenance relies on the input from the hippocampal CA1. Notably, inactivation of either the mPFC 255 

or the CA1→mPFC projection did not alter learned path preference, but decreased the na vigational 256

represents abstract progress within an ongoing goal -directed episode, thereby supporting fluent navigation 258

Spatial -progress cells are distinct from hippocampal place cells , whose firing fields are anchored to 260 

absolute spatial positions in the environment (Alme et al., 2014; O'Keefe and Dostrovsky, 1971; Ziv et al., 261 

2013) . Although mPFC neurons have also been shown to encode current spatial location (Ma et al., 2023; 262 

Zielinski et al., 2019) , the spatial -progress cells found in our study cannot be explained by place -specific 263 

firing, as they exhibit multiple spatially isolated firing fields that are located at similar spatial -progress stages. 264 

More importantly, the number of firing fields equals the number of visited paths. Furthermore, because the 265 

lengths and geometries are different for each path and mice dynamically changed the running speed and 266 

head direction during traveling at the same progress stage, the activity patterns of these neurons could not 267 

be explained by running speed or head direction. An a lternative interpretation is that mPFC neurons might 268 

encode elapsed time (Heldman et al., 2025) or distance to goal (Howard et al., 2014) , as has been described 269 

in the hippocampus , rather than spatial progress per se. To exclude this possibility, we aligned the activity 270 

of individual neurons with these behavioral variables . W hen aligned by time of trial onset, mPFC activity 271 

shifted progressively toward the trial offset as trial distance increased. Conversely, when aligned by 272 

distance to goal, responses shifted to the trial start as trial length increased. In contrast , when aligned by 273 

spatial progress, the neurons consistently fired at si milar normalized positions across trials of varying 274 

durations and lengths. In addition, the response jitter was significantly lower under progress alignment than 275 

under the other two al ignment conditions . Thus, mPFC neurons represent an abstract progress variable 276 

distinct from absolute spatial position and other behavioral correlates of navigation. 277 

Another interesting question concerns the origin of spatial -progress cells in mPFC. From a cognitive 278 

perspective, recognizing spatial progress must rely on both task  familiarity  and perception of external space. 279 

For example, estimating progress on a journey from home to the company with multiple possible routes 280 

requires not only prior experience with the length and geometry of each route but also knowledge of one’s 281 

own position. Unlike hippocampal place cells, which stabilize within minutes after exposure to a novel 282 

environment (Priestley et al., 2022) , progress -aligned activity of most neurons was disorganized across trials 283 

or paths even for several days in the naive stage. The proportion of spatial -progress cells significantly 284 

increased only after mice became proficient, along with the formation of sequential neural trajectories in 285 

UMAP space. Furthermore, when the CA1→mPFC projection was inactivated, approximately h alf of the 286 

spatial -progress cells lost their coding identity, and the sequential ring ‑shaped manifold was disrupted. 287 

These results support that spatial -progress coding in mPFC is not generated de novo but depends on 288 

learning of the task and the transformat ion of concrete spatial information supplied by the hippocampus. 289 

Our conclusion that inactivation of mPFC impairs navigational fluency and efficiency without altering 290 

path preference challenges both our original hypothesis and the well ‑established role of mPFC in decision 291 

making (Euston et al., 2012; Klein -Flügge et al., 2022) . Previous studies conducted on the delayed non -292 

match -to -place T -maze task (Benoit et al., 2020; Spellman et al., 2015) have shown that mPFC inactivation 293 

leads to decreased choice accuracy in the choice phase. In th at task, mice must remember the arm visited 294 

in the sample phase and then choose the opposite arm in the choice phase. In contrast, mice in our route 295 

choice task develop path preference based on differences in path length and geometry. Moreover, the 296 

motivat ional structure differs between the tasks. The T -maze paradigm imposes more severe penalties for 297 

errors (reward omission and time delays), whereas our task entails milder costs for suboptimal choices, 298 

primarily in the form of increased energy expenditure a nd longer travel time associated with longer paths. 299 

Last, the post -inactivation difference in choice behavior between the two tasks may arise from distinct 300 

memory demands, with our task relying on long -term memory and the T -maze task on short -term working 301 

memory. Importantly, our behavioral results align with the evidence that mPFC activity fails to decode the 302 

selected path but robustly encodes spatial progress. Together, these results suggest that the mPFC does 303 

not affect well ‑learned, stable path preference in the route choice task. 304 

The mPFC is widely recognized as a key region for higher -order cognitive aspects of spatial navigation. 305 

Neurons in this region have been shown to encode basic spatial variables, including current spatial 306 

location (Ma et al., 2023; Zielinski et al., 2019) and different trajectory routes (Fujisawa et al., 2008; Ito et al., 307 

2015) , akin to hippo campal place cells. Other works have revealed more abstract, task -related 308 

representations, including goal locations (Hok et al., 2005; Vogel et al., 2022) and task sequences (Tang et 309

code for spatial progress, thereby supporting efficient goal ‑directed navigation. 321

neural mechanism by which animals generate a flexible estimate of “how much have I accomplished” during 323 

goal -directed spatial navigation. Moreover , these findings reveal how the brain constructs a flexible readout 324 

of o ngoing spatial progress , transforming a static representation of the environment into a unified and 325 

abstract internal metric of progress. By extracting an invariant measure of progress from diverse 326 

experiences (e.g., different directions or paths), this me chanism supports flexible and efficient navigation 327 

in complex environments. 328

Route choice paradigm 719 

Spatial navigation in a natural environment often involves multiple routes to reach the destination. To better 720 

understand the neural mechanisms underlying route choice behavior, a custom route choice paradigm was 721 

designed ( Figure 1A). The maze was constructed from white acrylic (3.2 mm thick), which comprises a 722 

central circular arena (60 cm radius) and two rectangular boxes (25 cm × 10 cm). Each box is equipped 723 

with a water -delivery port (port A or B) located on the wall opposite the arena. In addition, four distinct paths 724 

with different geometries are arranged on the central circular arena, connecting the two reward boxes. 725 

These paths are named as follows: a long curve, a zigzag pat h, a direct path, and a short curve, with 726 

canonical trajectory lengths of 210 cm, 165 cm, 135 cm, and 153 cm, respectively. The four paths vary in 727 

length and geometry (e.g., the direct path was the shortest and straightest , whereas the long curve was the 728 

longest and most circuitous). The width of both boxes and paths is 10 cm, and the height of the walls is 15 729 

cm. Water delivery was controlled by a microcontroller and triggered by lick detection via a infrared sensor. 730 

Mice received a water reward only upon completing a full traversal to the opposite port; returning to the 731 

same port without reaching the opposite side did not trigger reward delivery. 732 

Mice were motivated to select different paths and navigate back and forth between two water ports. A 733 

trial was defined as a complete traversal from one port to the other. Only trials performed without 734 

backtracking or retreating were classified as complete trials and used for analysis. For simplicity, trials from 735 

port A to port B and those from port B to port A were referred to as rightward and leftward trials, respectively. 736 

Trials, running direction, and chosen path were automatically identified using custom code based on 737 

movement tracking. 738 

For each trial, six behavioral events were extracted using custom code: (1) trial onset (initiation of 739 

movement after reward consumption), (2) leave reward (exit from the start box) , (3) path start (entry into 740 

the chosen path), (4) path end (exit from the path), (5) enter reward (entry into the reward box), and (6) trial 741 

offset (a rrival at the reward port). Event timestamps were manually confirmed by a second experimenter. 742

Trajectories linearization and spatial segmentation 847 

To better quantify progress ‑related neuronal activity, we developed a pipeline to transform the curved 848 

movement trac e into linearized positions along spatial progress. To do so, we derived a standard trial 849 

trajectory for each path, and the movement traces of individual trials were projected onto their 850 

corresponding standard trajectories to obtain normalized positions al ong spatial progress. In detail, the 851 

movement traces of mice were first tracked. Second, the movement traces were resampled using piecewise 852 

cubic Hermite interpolating polynomial (PCHIP) interpolation and normalized from 0 to 1 using the 853 

cumulative path le ngth. Third, the standard trial trajectory for each path was derived by averaging the 854 

normalized movement traces in each path and smoothed using a smoothing spline interpolation (λ = 0.9). 855 

Last, the movement trajectory in each trial was projected onto its corresponding standard trajectory by 856 

computing the shortest Euclidean distance to all points on the standard trajectory. With this method, we 857 

mapped the mice’s two ‑dimensional movement in different paths onto a one ‑dimensional axis based on the 858 

relative sp atial progress ( Figure S2H). 859 

To compare the neural activity across geometrically distinct paths, the standard trajectory of each trial 860 

was segmented into 20 spatial bins: 2 bins covered the start box, 16 bins spanned the path, and 2 bins 861 

covered the end box ( Figure S2G). With this method, we reconceptualized this linearized axis in terms of 862 

spatial progress at a 5% step, where 0% corresponds to trial onset and 100% to trial offset. This allowed 863 

us to express the neural activity as a function of spatial pr ogress, independent of the geometry or length of 864 

the path. 865

Qua ntifying the response jitter across different alignment conditions 886 

To assess the response consistency, w e computed the response jitter of individual neurons under three 887 

alignment conditions. In detail, we first identified the neuronal peak firing positions of individual trials at a 888 

given alignment condition (time from trial onset, distance to goal, or normalized distance from onset to 889 

offset). We then calculated the standard deviation of peak positions across trials. To correct for scale 890 

differences across different alignment conditions, we calculated the chance level of standard deviation 891 

using the peak firing positions from the shuffled dataset. Finally, we defined the response jitter for each 892 

neuron as the ratio of the observed standard deviation to the c hance level. Lower jitter indicates higher 893
