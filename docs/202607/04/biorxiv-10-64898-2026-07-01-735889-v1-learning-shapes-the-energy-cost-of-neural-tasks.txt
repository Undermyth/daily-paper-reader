Abstract The stark difference in energy use between AI systems and biological brains highlights 11 

both the remarkable efficiency of the brain and our limited understanding of the energetics 12 

underlying neural computation. Although efficiency is widely cited as a principle of neural design, 13 

direct measurement of the energy cost of specific neural tasks within their corresponding circuits 14 

has remained limited. Here, by simultaneously measuring intracellular glucose and calcium 15 

dynamics in the same behaving mice in vivo , we use neuronal glucose consumption to define 16 

circuit-level energy costs associated with learning-based behavioral tasks. We found that the post-17 

learning fuel cost per task was significantly lower than pre-learning levels across multiple 18 

hippocampus- and cortex-dependent learning models, with or without changes in bulk calcium 19 

dynamics. This change in fuel cost was not mediated by extracellular glucose transport but instead 20 

reflected a reduction in intracellular glucose consumption and depended on canonical plasticity 21 

mechanisms, including NMDAR signaling and protein synthesis. Together, these findings suggest 22 

that attenuation of task-specific energy cost may represent a general bioenergetic trajectory of 23 

learning and plasticity. This “energy minimization” hypothesis provides insight into how the 24 

biological brain achieves efficiency and offers an orthogonal, complementary perspective to neural 25 

activity-centered frameworks. 26

In contrast to the gigawatt scale energy consumption of AI, the biological brain operates at 28 

incredibly low power. For example, it is estimated that the human brain operates on a modest, 29 

nearly constant 20 W of power, even when performing complex tasks or learning. The striking 30 

energetic difference between these two neural networks highlights a fundamental gap in our 31 

understanding of the biological brain. Moreover, since the brain secures and regulates its own 32 

power supply by controlling organismal physiology and behavior, it must use efficient strategies 33 

to obtain, store, and, most importantly, spend this precious energy. 34 

Energy efficiency has been recognized as a general principle of neural design ( 1-4 ) and a driving 35 

selection force of brain evolution ( 5); for example, many theoretical models suggest metabolically 36 

efficient wiring patterns ( 6, 7 ) and sparse coding ( 8-10 ) lower the energy cost of neural 37 

computation. However, their integration into modern neuroscience frameworks remains limited. 38 

Most brain models treat metabolic cost as a fixed background assumption rather than a dynamic 39 

variable, leaving the dynamic relationship between energy expenditure and learning largely 40 

underspecified. Although “efficiency” is widely cited as a driver of neural principles in ethology, 41 

this has been difficult to experimentally quantify in a functioning neural circuit. Neuroimaging 42 

methods like BOLD/fMRI and FDG-PET are largely based on local brain energy metabolism, but 43 

they are often used as an approximation of neural activity, rather than to be used as a measure of 44 

the energy cost of neural computation ( 11-15 ). Thus, to date, most estimates of the energy cost of 45 

information processing are based on theoretical models rather than direct experimental 46 

measurement, precluding a complete understanding of brain energetics. 47 

Glucose is the primary energy source of the brain under normal physiological conditions, and 48 

activity-dependent changes in neuronal glucose provide a physiologically grounded readout of 49 

local energetic demand ( 16-19 ). Conventional glucose measurements based on enzymatic assays 50 

or radioactive imaging, however, lack either the speed to match the sub-second temporal resolution 51 

of neural dynamics in vivo or the spatial and cellular specificity to resolve circuit-level glucose 52 

changes. The recent development of genetically encoded fluorescent glucose sensors, iGlucoSnFR 53 

(20 ) and its improved version iGlucoSnFR2 ( 21 ), enables sub-second temporal resolution to track 54 

glucose changes in extracellular and intracellular compartments in vivo and in a cell type specific 55 

manner ( 21 ). 56 

By combining iGlucoSnFR2 with in brain fiber photometry in parallel with calcium imaging, we 57 

monitored neural activity-linked intracellular glucose consumption in freely moving, behaving 58 

mice. The high spatial and temporal resolution of this approach also allowed us to use the rate of 59 

intracellular glucose consumption as a surrogate to estimate circuit-selective energy costs 60 

associated with behavior-defined neural tasks. We found that task-specific energy consumption 61 

rises sharply during the initial learning phase but falls as the task becomes routine. This post-62 

learning reduction in energy consumption is consistently observed in multiple well-established 63 

learning-based behavioral paradigms in the hippocampus and cortex and across various time 64

Visual activity transiently lowers intracellular glucose in primary visual cortex neurons 68 

We first used well-established visual stimulation-induced neural activity in primary visual cortex 69 

(V1) neurons as a model to test how sensory-induced activity affects neuronal glucose dynamics. 70 

To monitor glucose in distinct cellular compartments, we expressed cytosolic and extracellular 71 

membrane-targeted configurations of iGlucoSnFR2 under the hSyn promoter in neurons on the 72 

left and right sides of V1, respectively, as previously reported ( 21 ) (Fig. 1, A and B). iGlucoSnFR2 73 

provides improved sensitivity and reliability compared with iGlucoSnFR1, enabling in vivo 74 

monitoring of cytosolic glucose dynamics in the mammalian brain ( 21 ). We confirmed that 75 

intraperitoneal glucose injection leads to an increase in both intracellular and extracellular glucose 76 

in V1 neurons (Fig. 1, C and D), indicating both configurations of the iGlucoSnFR2 sensor were 77 

functional. 78 

For sensory stimulation, mice were first habituated in the dark for 1 hour, then exposed to a set of 79 

visual stimulus (Fig. 1E). GCaMP6m was expressed in neurons on the left side of the V1 while 80 

intra- or extracellular iGlucoSnFR2 was expressed on the right side of the V1 in the same animals 81 

(Fig. 1, F and H). Visual stimulation led to time-locked neural activity, as indicated by GCaMP6m 82 

peaks (Fig. 1G and fig. S1A). As expected, given the coupling between neurons and the 83 

vasculature, this transient activity caused a rapid drop of extracellular glucose which quickly 84 

recovered after visual stimulation (Fig. 1G and fig. S1A). We reasoned that the extracellular 85 

glucose dynamics in vivo (e.g., neuron-vasculature coupling) would be able to maintain 86 

intracellular glucose, at least at the soma, at a stable level ( 22-24 ). However, unexpectedly we 87 

observed an even steeper drop in intracellular glucose levels following visual stimulation (Fig. 1, 88 

I to K, and fig. S1B). This difference between extra- and intra-cellular glucose was more apparent 89 

when the visual stimulus was extended to 30 minutes (fig. S1, D to F), where extracellular glucose 90 

recovered much faster than the intracellular level. To rule out the possibility that this drop was due 91 

to changes in intracellular pH or other non-glucose induced iGluco signals, we used a sensor-dead 92 

cpSFGFP, commonly used to control fluorescence sensors ( 25 ), under the same behavioral 93 

paradigm, but cpSFGFP did not display similar visual stimulation-coupled signal decreases (Fig. 94 

1, J and K, and fig. S1C). Moreover, the iGlucoSnFR2 readout of intracellular glucose could be 95 

lowered by local infusion of a pan-class I GLUT (GLUT 1-4) transporter inhibitor, DRB18 ( 26 )96 

(fig. S2A), suggesting iGlucoSnFR2 is reporting a bona fide glucose drop upon visual stimulation. 97 

We also observed a similar, but less steep drop of intracellular glucose in astrocytes (fig. S2, C to 98 

E) under the same experimental conditions. 99 

Neural activity is known to increase intracellular glucose metabolism ( 27, 28 ). Thus, this apparent 100 

drop in intracellular glucose level likely reflects a transient imbalance in which neural activity-101 

induced glucose consumption exceeds glucose influx. This is supported by the further 102

Learning shapes glucose consumption of CA1 neurons during spatial navigation 110 

In our routine experiments, we noticed that repeated visual stimulation on the same individual 111 

resulted in diminished intracellular glucose drops in V1 neurons, suggesting that learning and 112 

anticipation may influence neuronal glucose consumption. To formally test how learning may 113 

affect neural glucose consumption, we measured glucose levels while mice performed a well-114 

established maze navigation task in which spatial learning can be readily tracked by behavioral 115 

quantification. 116 

In this paradigm, we expressed GCaMP6m in CA1 neurons on one side of the hippocampus and 117 

iGlucoSnFR2 on the contralateral CA1 in the same mouse (Fig. 2, A and B). Mice were trained to 118 

navigate a modified Hebb–Williams-type maze ( 29 ) for a water reward (Fig. 2C), and they quickly 119 

learned this task in 1-3 trials, as reflected by the significant reduction in time to reward from > 1 120 

minute to < 20 seconds (Fig. 2D) and the acquisition of the shortest paths between the entry and 121 

reward (Fig. 2E). The first trial, with the highest “effort” and GCaMP6m activity, is associated 122 

with the sharpest intraneuronal glucose drop (Fig. 2, F to H, and fig. S3A). With subsequent trials, 123 

the navigation task consumes much less intracellular glucose as the mice navigate the maze more 124 

quickly and with lower bulk neural activity (Fig. 2, G, H, and O). This decrease of spatial 125 

navigation related bulk neural activity is consistent with classic electrophysiology studies showing 126 

that in the CA1, the average firing rate (FR) drops between the first and subsequent trials in a new 127 

maze ( 30 ). Our experimental measurements directly support the notion that such sharper, high 128 

SNR coding after learning translates to reduced energy expenditure to complete the same 129 

navigation task ( 8). 130 

Following this post-learning reduction in intracellular glucose consumption, intracellular glucose 131 

levels stabilize and become insensitive to the inhibition of glucose import by DRB18 (Fig. 2P and 132 

fig. S4). This suggests that with tasks becoming routine, there is reduced neuronal glucose 133 

consumption rather than acute changes in fuel delivery or vascular transport. Indeed, in the same 134 

animals, extracellular glucose levels did not show post-learning reversals (Fig. 2, I to K and O, 135 

and fig. S3B). Moreover, intracellular glucose in the ventromedial hypothalamic nucleus (VMH), 136 

a center for regulating systemic glucose metabolism, did not exhibit post-learning changes in 137 

glucose levels during this spatial navigation task (Fig. 2, L to O, and fig. S3C). This indicates that 138 

the post-learning reduction is highly specific to the hippocampus, where spatial learning primarily 139

Glucose consumption rebounds during a renewed spatial navigation task 142 

We next examined glucose consumption during error-driven spatial relearning by modifying a 143 

familiar maze, thereby requiring animals to remap the optimal route to the reward. Mice were 144 

familiarized with the initial maze (Maze 1) over 15 trials to fully stabilize the behaviors (as 145 

reflected by a plateau in the time to reward), and then we blocked the shortest route by introducing 146 

a change point (Maze 2) prior to the 16th trial (Fig. 3A). Following this change, the average time 147 

to reward drastically increased, from 10 seconds to more than 1 minute (Fig. 3B), indicating that 148 

new effort and learning is required. Consistent with this, animals displayed significantly higher 149 

CA1 glucose drops in trial 16 (Maze 2) compared to trial 15 (Maze 1) (Fig. 3C), as revealed by 150 

mapping intracellular iGlucoSnFR2 signal dynamics onto the trajectories of the two trials (Fig. 3D 151 

and fig. S5). Moreover, within trial 16, when the mouse first passed through the corridor leading 152 

to the change point, which would have been interpreted as a “familiar” path, there was minimal 153 

glucose consumption compared to the return pass, at which point this same corridor would be 154 

interpreted as a “new” path (Fig. 3E, color tracks). 155 

Consistent with this observation, when we compared the intracellular glucose consumption before 156 

and after the point at which the maze was modified (change point), in trial 16, the “after” 157 

consumption was significantly higher than the “before” consumption, and there was no difference 158 

in consumption in trial 15 (before the modification was made) (Fig. 3, F and G). Together, these 159 

results indicate that it is the hippocampal representation, or subjective determination, of familiar 160 

versus new spatial information that dictates the energy consumption of the CA1 population, rather 161 

than the sensory information from a particular physical location. 162 

Interestingly, in contrast to the changes in glucose consumption, re-learning did not significantly 163 

change bulk calcium dynamics (Fig. 3H and I). This dissociation suggests that CA1 energetic 164 

demand during spatial learning may not solely reflect changes in mean population activity. Instead, 165 

the increased glucose consumption may arise from metabolically demanding plasticity 166 

mechanisms required for rapid spatial map formation and updating (see discussion). Together, 167 

these findings suggest that CA1 glucose dynamics report the energetic cost of spatial mapping and 168 

remapping, revealing a form of learning-related energy demand that is not readily captured by bulk 169 

calcium activity alone. 170 

Glucose consumption of M1 neurons correlates with performance of motor learning 171 

To determine whether post-learning glucose dynamics generalizes beyond rapid hippocampal 172 

spatial learning, we next examined a slower, cortex-dependent form of learning using the primary 173 

motor cortex (M1)-mediated single-pellet forelimb reaching task, an established paradigm for 174 

motor skill acquisition ( 31-33 ). In contrast to the maze task, which engages rapid CA1 map 175 

formation and updating, forelimb reach learning requires repeated practice over days and is 176

We trained the mice for 7 sessions over 4 days using a modified single-hydrogel-pellet reaching 180 

task adapted from the classic single-pellet reaching protocol ( 31, 32 ), which increased their reward 181 

retrieval success from 20% to 50% (Fig. 4, A and B). AAV expressing either Syn-182 

(cyto).iGlucoSnFR2 or Syn-GCaMP6m was injected bilaterally into the M1, and signals from the 183 

side contralateral to the preferred paw were analyzed to assess motor learning–induced changes 184 

(Fig. 4C and fig. S6A). During the early sessions (1 – 3), each successful reach was associated 185 

with higher glucose consumption (Fig. 4D). Neuronal glucose consumption of each successful 186 

reach significantly decreased as the animal progressed through motor learning session by session 187 

(Fig. 4D). As a negative control, we assessed CA1 glucose consumption, which was not altered 188 

during the motor training (Fig. 4E), again demonstrating that the post-learning change in energy 189 

consumption is task and circuit specific. 190 

As with CA1 neurons during spatial learning, once the motor skill has been learned and glucose 191 

consumption has stabilized at a low level, both the success rate and glucose consumption are 192 

insensitive to local glucose transporter inhibition (fig. S6, B to E). Consistent with a previous 193 

report ( 34 ), under this motor learning paradigm, the bulk M1 GCaMP6m activity did not 194 

significantly change as the mice mastered the motor skill (Fig. 4D). This may reflect the fact that 195 

motor learning reorganizes which M1 neurons are active and when, rather than altering the overall 196 

level of population activity (see discussion) ( 35 ). 197 

Interestingly, the slope of iGlucoSnFR2 signals (the rate of intracellular glucose change across 198 

sessions) showed a significant positive correlation with trial success rate (R² = 0.4384, p = 199 

5.4×10⁻⁶; Fig. 4F), indicating that mice with lower net glucose consumption achieved higher 200 

reaching success. Moreover, this correlation appeared stronger than that between success rate and 201 

session number alone (R² = 0.3136, p = 1.5×10⁻⁶; Fig. 4G), suggesting that the reduction in glucose 202 

consumption itself could be a more direct predictor of learning than elapsed training time. 203 

Different roles of NMDAR signaling and protein synthesis in post-learning glucose changes 204 

The longer learning curve of the forelimb reaching learning paradigm provided an opportunity to 205 

use pharmacological manipulations to explore potential molecular mechanisms underlying energy 206 

optimization. Both NMDAR signaling ( 36 ) and new protein synthesis ( 37 ) play key roles in 207 

plasticity, learning and memory, and blockage of them has been shown to impair motor learning 208 

(37-39 ). 209 

First, we infused the NMDA receptor antagonist AP5 into M1 to test how NMDAR-mediated 210 

mechanisms impact glucose consumption. Consistent with the literature that NMDAR is required 211 

for motor learning ( 38, 39 ), AP5 infusion robustly blocked behavioral performance (Fig. 4, H and 212 

I). Specifically, AP5 abolished the initial increase in glucose consumption in the first learning 213

Next, we examined how post-learning glucose consumption is affected by new protein synthesis. 217 

We adopted a protocol in which locally infusing anisomycin into M1 after each training session 218 

(sessions 1-3) robustly delayed motor learning (Fig. 4J) ( 40 ). With new protein synthesis blocked, 219

Together, these results suggest a two-step mechanism: an early NMDAR-dependent increase in 222 

Ca 2+ entry that likely reflects the energy consumption of new plasticity events, followed by a 223 

subsequent consolidation phase in which protein synthesis plays a key role in remodeling the 224 

circuits to achieve a more energy-efficient processing protocol. 225 

Discussion 226 

Using a genetically encoded glucose sensor in defined neuronal compartments, we directly 227 

measured the fuel cost of task-relevant neural processes in freely moving mice with sub-second 228 

resolution. Across multiple distinct learning paradigms, we found that learning was accompanied 229 

by a reduction in task-directed glucose consumption. This reduction was brain region-specific, 230 

closely tracked behavioral performance, rebounded during relearning, and depended on NMDA 231 

receptor signaling and protein synthesis. Together, these findings establish a quantitative and 232 

experimentally trackable link among energetic cost, neural activity, and learning. 233 

The notion that the brain has evolved for energy efficiency is foundational in neuroscience. 234 

Principles such as sparse coding, minimized wiring, and signal-to-noise optimization are widely 235 

cited as evolutionary consequences of the metabolic constraints faced by neural systems ( 1, 5, 8 ). 236 

However, much of the evidence supporting this principle has come from theoretical models and 237 

from relatively static features of brain organization ( 2-4, 41, 42 ). By measuring in vivo fuel 238 

consumption within defined circuit nodes across diverse behaviors, our study provides 239 

experimental support for the energy-efficiency principle in action. These data suggest that, beyond 240 

the static architectural features of the brain that reflect long-term evolutionary optimization, the 241 

dynamics of learning and plasticity may themselves continuously and actively optimize metabolic 242 

cost. 243 

At present, we do not yet have the ability to image glucose consumption at single-cell resolution 244 

in freely moving animals. Thus, beyond the observed requirements for NMDA receptor signaling 245 

and protein synthesis, the precise mechanisms by which learning alters glucose consumption 246 

remain an open question. Future development of cellular- or subcellular-resolution glucose 247 

imaging will be important for linking these metabolic changes to the extensive body of knowledge 248 

on neural plasticity. For example, in CA1, reductions in average firing rate ( 30 ), refinement of 249 

population tuning, and the formation of place fields ( 43-45 ) could each improve the signal-to-noise 250

Although our glucose measurements do not have the resolution of classical electrophysiological 252 

recordings, our GCaMP6m signals (Fig. 2, G and O) are broadly consistent with known patterns 253 

of neural activity. The rapid reduction in glucose consumption within one to two trials further 254 

suggests that behavioral time-scale synaptic plasticity (BTSP) ( 47 ) may contribute to this process. 255 

In motor learning, M1 neuronal activity becomes more stereotyped and more closely aligned with 256 

movement ( 35, 48 ), both of which could increase the SNR ( 8) and reduce the energetic cost of 257 

coding. At the synaptic level, learning reorganizes synapses by strengthening some connections 258 

while weakening others ( 31, 49, 50 ), and enables nonlinear dendritic computation ( 32, 51-54 ), 259 

processes that may reduce the number of spines or active synaptic elements required to support a 260 

given task and thereby lower energetic demand. These possible mechanisms in CA1 and M1 are 261 

not mutually exclusive and may operate in parallel. The potential contribution of astrocytes and 262 

other non-neuronal cells to learning-associated changes in glucose consumption also remains to 263 

be explored. 264 

Although bulk glucose measurements lack cellular resolution, they are particularly meaningful for 265 

brain energetics because they capture physiologically relevant net fuel use. If the energetic cost 266 

measured in a single circuit is extrapolated to the scale of the whole brain, a brain that can perform 267 

the same task with less fuel would be expected to confer a selective advantage. Given this 268 

evolutionary pressure, and the consistency of post-learning reductions in energetic cost across 269 

behaviors, circuits, and timescales, we propose that energy-cost reduction may be a general 270 

outcome—and potentially an organizing objective—of learning at the cellular and circuit levels. 271 

We term this idea the “energy minimization hypothesis”: learning-induced plasticity moves neural 272 

network structure and dynamics toward configurations in which a neural task, such as transforming 273 

sensory input into an appropriate motor output sequence, can be accomplished at lower energetic 274 

cost. 275 

In simplified terms, learning is a process through which an animal’s experience guides synaptic 276 

plasticity to generate new patterns of neural activity that improve future behavior ( 55 ). How these 277 

new patterns—such as memory ensembles ( 56 ), place fields ( 45, 47, 57 ), and activity trajectories 278 

(35, 54, 58 )—are shaped by distinct plasticity mechanisms and instructive signals remains a central 279 

frontier in neuroscience. The energy minimization hypothesis does not aim to replace these 280 

mechanistic frameworks or directly answer all of these questions. Rather, it offers an orthogonal 281 

perspective on the trajectory of learning and plasticity. From this view, plasticity, broadly defined 282 

as changes in neural activity patterns and circuit organization, may guide circuits toward lower-283 

energy configurations. This provides a simplified energetic lens for understanding brain 284 

computation and neural dynamics alongside the prevailing signal- and information-centered 285 

frameworks. 286 

More broadly, this energetic view may also open new ways to think about intervention. Recent 287 

advances in drugs that potently modulate systemic and brain energy metabolism, including GLP-288 

1 receptor agonists, have revealed unexpected effects on behavior and higher-order brain functions 289

Materials and Methods 423 

Animals 424 

All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the National Institutes of 425 

Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and approved by Scripps Research 426 

Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Male C57BL/6J mice aged 8–10 weeks (Jackson 427 

Laboratory, stock no. 000664) were used for all experiments. Mice were group housed and 428 

maintained on a 12 h light: 12 h dark cycle with food and water ad libitum unless specified. Age-429 

and body weight-matched mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups. 430 

Stereotaxic viral injection 431 

AAV viruses, including AAV9-hSyn-(cyto).iGlucoSnFR2, AAV9-hSyn-432 

(mem).iGlucoSnFR2, AAV9-hSyn-GCaMP6m (Addgene #100841), AAV9-hSyn-cpSFGFP, and 433 

AAV9-Gfap-(cyto).iGlucoSnFR2, were diluted in DPBS to a final titer of 5 × 10^12 vp/mL. A total 434 

volume of 300 nL of the diluted virus was injected into the primary visual cortex (lambda: AP, 435 

−0.5 mm; ML, 2.5 mm; DV, 1.25 mm), CA1 (bregma: AP, −1.8 mm; ML, 1.5 mm; DV, 1.35 mm), 436 

VMH (bregma: AP, −1. 4 mm; ML, 0.5 mm; DV, 5.9 mm), or primary motor cortex (bregma: AP, 437 

1.0 mm; ML, 1.5 mm; DV, 1.4 mm). 438 

Mice were anesthetized with 1.5% isoflurane, and the skull was secured in a stereotaxic 439 

frame (David Kopf Instruments) and leveled using bregma and lambda as reference points. Virus 440 

was infused through a glass pipette at a rate of 100 nL/min using a nanoinjector (Nanoliter 2020 441 

Injector, 300704, World Precision Instruments). After injection, the pipette was left in place for 5– 442 

10 min before being slowly withdrawn. For subsequent fiber implantation, either fiber-optic 443 

cannulas (400 μm core, 0.50 NA, RWD) or optical fiber multiple fluid injection cannulas (OmFC, 444 

Doric Lenses) were lowered to the target site and secured to the skull with dental cement (C&B-445 

Metabond, Parkell). Mice were allowed to recover for at least 2 weeks before experiments. 446 

Fiber photometry 447 

Fiber photometry was used to record GCaMP6m and iGlucoSnFR2 signals in freely 448 

behaving mice. Mice were connected to fiber-optic patch cables through implanted ferrules using 449 

ceramic mating sleeves. Patch cables consisted of single or double optical fibers with a 400 μm450 

core diameter and 0.57 NA (Doric Lenses). The cables were coupled to 470 nm and 410 nm 451 

excitation light sources for signal and reference-channel recording, respectively. Before 452 

experiments, mice were habituated to the tethering system and remained tethered for at least 30 453 

min before recordings began. The fiber photometry acquisition setup has been described 454 

previously ( 59 ). Fluorescence signals were acquired at 20 Hz. Reference-subtracted signals were 455 

expressed as ΔF/F₀ and aligned to behavioral events. 456 

Visual stimulation paradigm 457 

Mice receiving bilateral AAV injections and optic fiber implants targeting the primary 458 

visual cortex were used in this paradigm. Two days prior to recording, animals were habituated to 459 

both the fiber-optic cable connection and the visual stimulation apparatus for 30 min/day. The 460

On the day of recording, mice first acclimated to the darkened chamber with the fiber-optic 465

Maze navigation paradigm 469 

All maze experiments were conducted using a Hebb-Williams maze ( 29 ) (MazeEngineers). 470 

The apparatus consisted of a 60 × 60 cm square arena with 10-cm-high walls. One corner served 471 

as the maze entrance, and the opposite corner contained a telemetry-controlled water reward device 472 

that delivered water for 2 s upon lick activation, followed by a 10-s no-delivery interval. The 473 

internal walls of the maze were movable, allowing different paths to be configured within the same 474 

arena. A camera mounted above the maze was used to record behavior throughout the experiments. 475 

Preliminary training: the goal of preliminary training was to familiarize mice with the maze 476 

environment, the water port location, optic fiber cable connection, and handling, while establishing 477 

a reliable drinking habit confined to the maze. Successful training was defined as the ability to 478 

navigate directly to the water port upon maze entry with minimal fear or exploratory behavior, 479 

even as the internal maze configuration changed. Training was conducted over 4–5 days and 480 

comprised 8–10 sessions delivered at 12-h intervals. Throughout this period, water-restricted mice 481 

received water exclusively within the maze, and session duration was adjusted to ensure a 482 

minimum intake of 700 μL per session. Body weight was monitored daily, and the water restriction 483 

protocol was suspended if weight dropped below 80% of the initial weight. A simplified maze 484 

containing only two internal walls was used during preliminary training to facilitate water port 485 

discovery. Sessions progressed in a stepwise manner. In sessions 1 and 2, mice were placed in the 486 

simplified maze without fiber cable attachment for 15 min to explore the environment and learn 487 

the water port location. Sessions 3 and 4 were identical in structure but with the optic fiber cable 488 

connected, allowing habituation to the tethered condition. Beginning in sessions 5–10, a repeated 489 

handling procedure was introduced: upon each arrival at the water port, mice were permitted to 490 

drink for 10 s before being gently guided back to the entrance for the next trial. This cycle was 491 

repeated continuously over 15 min. Mice were considered ready for formal recording once they 492 

reached the water port and drank more than 20 times within 10 min across two consecutive 493 

sessions. 494 

For formal recording, mice were tested in the maze configuration illustrated in Fig. 2B. 495 

Each session began with the mouse confined to the entrance alley for a 1-min baseline recording 496 

period. The door was then opened to initiate the first trial, during which the mouse freely navigated 497 

to the water port. Upon arrival, the mouse was allowed to drink for 10 s before being gently guided 498 

back to the entrance alley, where the door was closed for a 40-s inter-trial baseline period. The 499 

door was then reopened to begin the subsequent trial. This procedure was repeated for a total of 500

Motor learning paradigm 507 

The single-pellet forelimb reaching task was used as the motor learning paradigm, 508 

performed as previously described ( 31, 33 ). We substituted food rewards with water rewards in the 509 

form of hydrogel pellets following 12 h of water restriction. to avoid the impact of hunger on 510 

systemic glucose level. This modification preserved the task structure and motivational drive while 511 

eliminating the metabolic interference of fluctuating systemic glucose on iGlucoSnFR2 signals 512 

recorded in M1. The training apparatus consisted of a Plexiglas chamber with a narrow vertical 513 

slit, in front of which a small platform was positioned to hold the hydrogel pellet within reaching 514 

distance. 515 

Prior to formal training, all mice underwent habituation to the training chamber and were 516 

assessed for paw preference. During this assessment, hydrogel pellets were placed on the platform 517 

and mice were observed over 10 reaching attempts; the preferred paw was defined as the one used 518 

more frequently across attempts, or as the paw used in five consecutive reaches. Preliminary 519 

habituation then proceeded in a stepwise fashion: water-deprived mice were placed in the chamber 520 

for 15 min every 12 h and provided with 1 mL of hydrogel per session. The first two sessions were 521 

conducted without optic fiber cable connection, followed by two additional sessions with the cable 522 

attached, allowing progressive acclimation to the tethered recording condition. 523 

Formal motor learning recordings spanned 4 days and comprised seven training sessions 524 

administered at 12-h intervals. Each session was terminated upon completion of 50 reaching 525 

attempts or after 20 min, whichever occurred first. Fiber photometry signals and behavioral video 526 

were recorded simultaneously throughout each session, with video timestamps used to align neural 527 

signals to discrete reaching events during post-hoc analysis. A reach was scored as successful when 528 

the mouse used its preferred paw to grasp the hydrogel pellet and bring it directly to its mouth 529 

without dropping it. Overall performance was quantified as the success rate, defined as the 530 

proportion of successful reaches relative to the total number of attempts per session. 531

To enable local pharmacological manipulation of specific brain regions during fiber 533 

photometry recording, optical fiber multiple fluid injection cannulas (Doric Lenses) were 534 

implanted at sites of viral expression. Prior to each experiment, a fluid injector was inserted fully 535 

into the cannula guide tube and connected via polyethylene tubing to a 10-μL Hamilton syringe 536 

driven by a WPI SMARTouch controller. 537 

Drug solutions were infused into the target region (V1, CA1, or M1) at a flow rate of 50– 538 

100 nL/min, with a total infusion volume of approximately 500 nL per site. The following 539

Histology and immunohistochemistry 544 

Mice were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane and transcardially perfused with PBS 545 

followed by 4% PFA. Brains were then dissected and post-fixed overnight in 4% PFA at 4 °C. 546 

After fixation, brains were dehydrated in 30% sucrose for 24 h and embedded in OCT compound 547 

(Tissue-Tek O.C.T. Compound, Sakura). Coronal sections were prepared using a cryostat 548 

(FS800A, RWD). For histological analysis and immunostaining, 50-μm-thick sections were 549 

collected. 550 

For immunostaining of the astrocyte marker S100B, free-floating sections were first 551 

blocked for 1 h at room temperature in blocking buffer consisting of 5% normal donkey serum in 552 

PBS containing 0.3% Triton X-100 (PBST). Sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with 553 

rabbit monoclonal anti-S100B antibody (E7C3A; Cell Signaling Technology, #90393) diluted 554 

1:500 in blocking buffer. After primary antibody incubation, sections were washed three times in 555 

PBST for 15 min each and then incubated for 1 h at room temperature with donkey anti-rabbit IgG 556 

secondary antibody conjugated to Alexa Fluor 647, diluted 1:500 in blocking buffer. Sections were 557 

subsequently washed three times in PBST for 15 min each, stained with DAPI (5 nM in PBS) for 558 

30 min, and mounted in Fluoromount-G (Electron Microscopy Sciences) for imaging with a 559 

confocal microscope (FV4000, Olympus). 560 

Data analysis and quantification 561 

Fiber Photometry Preprocessing: for all paradigms, raw fluorescence signals first 562 

reference-subtracted and subsequently normalized as ΔF/F₀ using the entire recording prior to 563 

further analysis. Trial- or reach-aligned ΔF/F₀ traces were computed relative to a baseline period 564 

defined for each paradigm as described below and were used for heatmap visualization and time-565 

course comparisons throughout. 566 

For fiber photometry data analysis in the visual stimulation paradigm shown in Fig. 1, 567 

traces were aligned to the onset of visual stimulation and averaged across stimulation trials. For 568 

each visual stimulation, the mean signal during the 30-s period preceding the 15-s visual 569 

stimulation epoch was used as the baseline to zero-normalize the onset trace. To compare 570 

(cyto).iGlucoSnFR2, (mem).iGlucoSnFR2, and cpSFGFP signals, the normalized area under the 571 

curve (AUC) during the 15-s visual stimulation period was quantified and used for statistical 572 

analysis. 573 

For fiber photometry data analysis in the maze navigation paradigm shown in Fig. 2 and 3, 574 

preprocessed signals were segmented on a trial-by-trial basis according to behavioral annotations. 575 

Trial onset was defined as the moment the mouse entered the maze (time 0), and trial end as the 576 

moment of water port arrival. For each trial, the mean signal of the 20-s period preceding maze 577 

entry was used as the baseline to zero the signal. For cross-trial comparisons, GCaMP6m, 578

(cyto).iGlucoSnFR2 and (mem).iGlucoSnFR2 signals were quantified as the normalized area 579 

under the curve (AUC) computed from trial onset to trial end. To account for variability in trial 580 

duration, AUC values were normalized by trial length. 581 

Mouse trajectories were extracted from overhead video recordings using DeepLabCut ( 60 ). 582 

The body center was tracked frame-by-frame, and the resulting x–y coordinates were aligned to 583 

behavioral annotations. Trial-specific trajectories from maze entry to water port arrival were then 584 

reconstructed to visualize each animal's navigation path. 585 

For fiber photometry data analysis in the maze transition paradigm shown in Fig. 3, trial-586 

aligned GCaMP6m and (cyto).iGlucoSnFR2 ΔF/F₀ signals were mapped onto the reconstructed 587 

mouse trajectories to visualize the spatial and temporal dynamics of neural and metabolic signals 588 

along the navigation path, enabling direct comparison between trial 15 (familiar configuration) 589 

and trial 16 (novel configuration). For the change-point analysis in Fig. 3, signals immediately 590 

before and after the change point were extracted using windows of up to 3 s with window 591 

boundaries constrained by the start and end of each trial. (cyto).iGlucoSnFR2 responses were 592 

quantified as the slope of the ΔF/F₀ trace obtained by linear regression within each pre - or post-593 

change-point window, whereas GCaMP6m responses were quantified using the normalized AUC 594 

over the corresponding window (re-zeroed to the starting value of the window). 595 

For fiber photometry analysis in the motor learning paradigm, signals recorded from M1 596 

neurons contralateral to the preferred paw were used for analysis. Fluorescence signals were 597 

reference-subtracted and segmented reach-by-reach according to behavioral annotations. Analyses 598 

were restricted to successful reaching events, with time 0 defined as the moment the mouse first 599 

placed its paw on the hydrogel pellet. Successful reach-aligned ΔF/F₀ traces were used to generate 600 

session-averaged time-course plots and heatmaps. The relationship between motor performance 601 

and (cyto).iGlucoSnFR2 dynamics was assessed at the session level: for each mouse and session, 602 

individual reach-aligned (cyto).iGlucoSnFR2 ΔF/F₀ traces were converted to percent ΔF/F₀ and 603 

averaged into a single session-averaged trace. The glucose response was then quantified as the 604 

slope of this averaged trace from 0 to 2 s after movement onset using linear regression. For each 605 

mouse and session, the (cyto).iGlucoSnFR2 slope was paired with the corresponding behavioral 606 

success rate, and two separate linear mixed-effects models were fitted: one testing the effect of 607 

session number on success rate, and the other testing the effect of (cyto).iGlucoSnFR2 slope on 608 

success rate. In both models, the predictor (session number or glucose slope) was included as a 609 

fixed effect, with mouse identity as a random intercept to account for repeated measurements 610 

across sessions within the same animal. Marginal R 2 of the fixed effects was used. 611 

Statistics 612 

Statistical analyses were performed using Prism 10 (GraphPad Software) for paired and unpaired 613 

t tests. The specific statistical tests used and the number of biological replicates are indicated in 614 

the corresponding figure legends. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically 615 

significant. 616
