Abstract 8

Representational drift, the gradual evolution of neural population codes over days , has been widely 9

documented at the level of single neurons. However, whether drift is organized across larger cortical 10 

populations and spatial scales remains unclear. Here, we examined cortex -wide population dynamics under 11 

tightly controlled sensory input and behavior. Using widefield calcium imaging , we longitudinally recorded 12 

excitatory activity in Layers 2/3 (L2/3) or Layer 5 (L5) across 25 cortical areas while mice performed a whisker -13 

based texture discrimination task. Sensory -evoked activity was tracked over five consecutive days during 14 

stable task performance. Population activity patterns reorganized over days, across the cortex, in both 15 

layers. This reor ganization followed distinct laminar motifs: notably L5 responses exhibited a widespread, 16 

monotonic decrease in activity across most cortical areas, whereas L2/3 responses showed spatially localized 17 

and heterogeneous changes that were strongest during the sensory period . These laminar differences 18 

extended beyond the stimulus period, with L5 exhibiting more prolonged temporal engagement than L2/3. 19 

Together, these findings indicate that representational drift can unfold as a process that is coordinated across 20 

cortex with distinct laminar profiles . Thus, drift may reflect a structured feature of cort ex -wide circuit 21 

dynamics. 22

Introduction 24 

Neural activity patterns are often assumed to represent stable internal representations of the external world. 25 

In sensory s ystems, stimuli are thought to be encoded by reliable patterns of neural activity that support 26 

consistent perception and behavior over tim e1–5. However, this assumption of stability has been challenged 27 

by studies showing that neural responses to the same stimulus can change substantially over days, a 28 

phenomenon known as representational drift (RepD; 6–11 ). Importantly, the properties of this drift appear to 29 

vary across brain regions. 30 

Earlier work has revealed several forms of representational drift. In piriform cortex 10 and posterior 31 

parietal cortex 11 , individual neurons exhibit substantial variability across days while population -level statistics 32 

remain relatively stable. In contrast, hippocampal ensembles often reorganize more rapidly, sometimes in 33 

hours, with changes in the composition and sparsity 12,13 . In visual cortex, responses to simple stimuli such as 34 

drifting gratings remain stable across weeks, whereas responses to natural movies reorganize more strongly, 35 

indicating stimulus -dependent drift 8. T hese findings have led to several distinct interpretations of drift, 36 

including the idea that drift temporally separates experiences , or reflects continual learning, generalization, 37 

or redundancy in population codes. Recent work has proposed several functio nal interpretations of 38 

hippocampal drift, including roles in distinguishing temporally distinct but similar experiences 6,14 , supporting 39 

continual learning 14 , enabling generalization 15,16 , and providing redundancy 17 . Conversely, drift may arise 40 

from subtle, often unmeasured, fluctuations in behaviour (e.g., running speed 18 –21 ) or sensory context (e.g., 41 

olfactory cues 22 –24 ). Supporting this latter view, studies in Egyptian fruit bats 15,25 , showed highly stereotyped 42 

flight trajectories that were associa ted with minimal drift in hippocampal CA1 across days 26 .43 

In the neocortex, most studies of representational drift have focused on individual sensory or 44 

association areas and have largely overlooked how representations change across cortical layers. Yet cortical 45 

circuits are organized in layers, with distinct connectivity and function . Superficial layers ( layer 2/3) are 46 

dominated by recurrent intracortical and long -range corticocortical interactions, whereas deeper layers ( layer 47 

5) provide the bulk of the output from cortex , especially to subcortical targets 27 . Several studies suggest that 48 

drift may depend on stimulus class, cortical layer, or cell type. For example, layer -dependent differences in 49 

representational stability have been observed in visual cortex 9,28 , and receptive -field -dependent drift has 50 

been reported in barrel cortex 29 . Additional studies have described tuning instability in non -columnar 51 

somatosensory neurons 30 and differential stability of task -related variables in parietal and re trosplenial 52 

cortex 31,32 . However, because these studies typically focus on single regions or restricted stimulus classes, it 53 

remains unclear whether RepD reflects local circuit dynamics or a coordinated process across larger cortical 54 

po pulations. 55 

Computational approaches have identified several mechanisms that could give rise to 56 

representational drift. These include synaptic processes such as spike timing -dependent plasticity (STDP) ,57 

synaptic turnover coupled with homeostatic normalization, stochastic synaptic -weight 33 fluctuations driven 58 

by white 34 or correlated 35 noise, dropout of nodes or weights in recurrent networks 36 , and implicit 59 

regularization of population activity 13 . An alternative hypothesis posits that RepD may arise from slow 60 

fluctuations in intrinsic neuronal excitability 37 , rather than changes in synaptic connectivity . Supporting this 61 

possibility, recent work using a multisensory virtual -reality system demon strated that hippocampal drift 62 

persisted even when sensory input and behavior were tightly controlled. Moreover, the intrinsic excitability 63 

of individual hippocampal place cells strongly predicted their future stability, with more excitable neurons 64 

exhibiti ng greater long -term representational stability 38 .65

Together, these studies motivated us to test whether representational drift reflects local circuit 66 

dynamics or is a coordinated process that occurs across the cortex, as a whole 39 –45 . To address this question, 67 

we examined cortex -wide population activity while head fixed mice performed a go -no go whisker -based 68 

texture discrimination task with well -constrained behavioral output . Using widefield calcium imaging, we 69 

longitudinally recorded excitatory activity across 25 cortical areas in either layer 2/3 (L2/3) or layer 5 (L5) 70 

over five consecutive days . This approach allowed us to test whether representational drift emerges at the 71 

population level, whether it differs across cortical layers, and to what extent behavioral and sensory variab les 72 

contribute to these dynamics .73

Representational drift is local in L2/3 and global in L5 164 

To examine how representational drift is distributed across cortical areas, we analyzed layer -specific activity 165 

maps (Δ F/F) during the Stimulus period over five consecutive days (Fig. 4a). Comparison of day -to -day activity 166 

maps revealed a consistent decrea se in L5 activity over time, whereas activity in L2/3 changed in a more 167 

heterogeneous manner. Extended activity maps spanning seven consecutive recording days showed similar 168 

results, further demonstrating the continuous progression of the changes described above (Supplementary 169 

Fig. 4a ). To quantify these effects, we compared mean responses between the first 300 (early) and last 300 170 

(late) trials for each layer (Fig. 4b). In L2/3, activity increased significantly in association and motor areas, 171 

including VIS pl and MOs (p > 0.05; signed -rank test; FDR -corrected) , whereas somatosensory areas such as 172 

SSp -bfd remained largely unchanged ( p > 0.05; signed -rank test; FDR -corrected). In contrast, L5 showed 173 

widespread suppression, with significant decreases across most somatosensory, association and visual areas 174 

(p < 0.05; signed -rank test; FDR -corrected) , with only a few exceptions . Together, th ese results indicate that 175 

activity changes in L2/3 are spatially heterogeneous, whereas L5 exhibits a broad and more uniform decrease 176 

in activity. 177 

To quantify RepD within each cortical area, we computed Pearson ’s correlation coefficient o f178 

response activity as a function of time (r; response vs. trial bins) during the Stimulus period (Fig. 4c). Positive 179 

r values indicate increasing activity over time , negative values indicate decreasing activity, and values near 180 

zero indicate temporal stability. Focusing on the three representative areas (SSp -bfd, VISpl, and MOs ) we 181 

observed a consistent pattern in L5 : all three areas exhibited significant negative r values (Fig. 4c; p < 0.05; 182 

signed -rank test), indicating a progressive decrease in activity over trials. In contrast, L2/3 exhibited 183 

heterogeneous region -specific dynamics: SSp -bfd showed no significant drift ( p > 0.05 ; signed -rank test ), 184 

whereas VISpl and MOs displayed significant positive r values ( p < 0.05; signed -rank test), reflecting increasing 185 

activity across trials. Notably, in all three regions , r values were significantly different between layers (p <186 

0.005; rank -sum test) , suggesting that temporal trends in the both layers evolved differently . We extended 187 

this analysis to the entire dorsal cortex by computing r values for each cortical area and each pixel (Fig. 4d). 188 

This analysis revealed striking, layer -specific spatial p atterns , L2/3 exhibited predominantly positive r values 189 

across both frontal and posterior regions, whereas L5 exhibited negative r values across nearly all cortical 190 

areas (Fig. 4d; see also Supplementary Fig. 4b ). Together, these results indicate that repr esentational drift in 191

across areas. 195

Discussion 318 

Here we demonstrate that there is a laminar dissociation in representational drift that is temporally driven, 319 

present at the population level , and independent of behavior. Over days, the P opulation activity patterns 320 

reorganize distinctly in both layers with L2/3 exhibiti ng spatially localized, heterogeneous, and stimulus -321 

linked dri ft, whereas L5 displays a widespread and predominantly suppressive drift that extends beyond the 322 

sensory epoch. Importantly , our work shows that this reorganization in cortical activity is related to trial 323 

number, time in a session, and it is not related t o movement or behavioral parameters. 324 

Representational drift emerges at the cortex -wide population level 325 

Recent technical advances have enabled longitudinal tracking of neuronal activity over days and weeks 11,14,48 .326 

Most long -term studies have focused on single neurons within restricted brain regions, including the 327 

hippocampus 6,7 , primary visual cortex 8,9 , and posterior parietal cortex 9,11 . These studies consistently show 328 

that neuronal tuning properties can gra dually change: neurons may lose responsiveness to a stimulus while 329 

others acquire it, reflecting pronounced single -cell representational drift across contexts, layers, and cell 330 

types. Notably, however, many of these studies report that population -level sta tistics remain relatively stable 331 

despite substantial single -neuron variability 6–8,10,11,49,50 . Stable decoding performance across days has further 332 

supported the view that population representations preserve task -relevant information even as individual 333 

ne urons drift 51 . Thus, single -cell instability and population -level stability have often been considered 334 

complementary, reconciling cellular plasticity with reliable stimulus representation and behavior. 335 

In parallel, large -scale recording approaches have e nabled tracking of cortex -wide activity at both 336 

single -cell resolution 52 and at the mesoscale using wide -field imaging (Gilad and Helmchen 2020; Pollak et 337 

al. 2025; Nietz et al., 2023; Makino and Komiyama 2017). Many of these studies emphasize learning -related 338 

modulation across cortical areas and trial structure. However, such changes are typically interpreted in 339 

relation to performance, learning stage, or behavioral variability. Day -to -day neuronal reorganization that is 340 

invariant to behavioral parameter s, i.e., representational drift per se, has not been systematically examined 341 

at the cortex -wide population level. 342 

Here, under stable behavioral performance across five consecutive days, we show that the population 343 

vector derived from 25 cortical areas chan ges progressively over time. This reorganization becomes evident 344 

only when examining cortex -wide activity patterns, as local recordings alone would not capture the 345 

distributed population shift. Importantly, cortex -wide population drift does not contradict the existence of 346 

single -cell drift; rather, it reveals an additional mesoscale dimension of representational reorganization. 347 

Whether similar cortex -wide drift extends to subcortical circuits, other sensory modalities, or freely moving 348 

behavior remains an i mportant direction for future work. 349

Previous studies have reported representational drift in both L2/3 and L5 within individual cortical regions, 351 

but clear qualitative differences between layers have not been evident 8,9 . Here we demonstrate that although 352

Functional significance of population -level representational drift 369 

At the single -cell level, it remains debated whether representational dri ft reflects biological noise or a 370 

functionally meaningful process 11,14,48,57,58 . At the population level, our findings suggest that representational 371 

drift represents coordinated network reorganization rather than random variability. The localized, stimul us -372 

linked enhancement observed in L2/3 is consistent with adaptive reweighting within recurrent cortical 373 

circuits 40 –42 . Superficial layers are dominated by intracortical and corticocortical connectivity, making them 374 

well positioned for experience -depende nt refinement. We speculate that gradual enhancement in higher -375 

order association areas may reflect circuit -level optimization of sensory integration. One potential 376 

mechanism could involve long -term modulation of inhibitory control, for example via somatost atin 377 

interneurons, leading to decreased inhibition of local L2/3 populations. The pronounced drift in posterior 378 

association areas such as VISpl , VISl , and VISli , regions implicated in higher -order sensory processing (Oz 379 

Rokach et al 2025; Pollak et al 2025; Gilad et al. 2018; Gallero Salas et al. 2021 ; Deitch, Rubin & Ziv, 2021 ;380 

Schoonover et al., 2021 ), further supports the idea that superficial -layer drift reflects refinement of 381 

associative representations. In contrast, the relativ e stability of SSp -bfd suggests that lower -order sensory 382 

representations remain comparatively constrained. 383 

By contrast, the widespread suppression observed in L5 suggests a different functional role. Deep -384 

layer pyramidal neurons constitute the principal co rtical output to subcortical and motor structures 59 –63 .385 

During expert performance, broad cortical output may initially support sensory processing, feedback, and 386 

motor planning. Over time, however, redundant or distributed output may become unnecessary. T he gradual 387 

suppression of L5 responses across multiple areas may therefore reflect increasing efficiency of cortical 388 

output, minimizing redundant signaling while preserving essential motor commands. The relative stability of 389 

frontal areas in L5 may indicat e preservation of motor -related signals, consistent with limited drift in motor 390 

regions 64 –67 .391 

More broadly, the reduction of L5 activity may signal a shift in network recruitment during extended 392 

expert performance. Habitual or well -trained behaviors are thought to involve increased engagement of 393

Before imaging, mice were conditioned to lick for a reward after the go texture, presented in a trial structure 471 

similar to the task itself. Imaging began only mice reliably licked following the response cue (typically after 472 

the 200 –400 trials on the firs t day). On the first imaging day, mice were presented with the ‘go ’ texture and 473 

after 50 trials the ‘no -go ’ texture was gradually introduced (starting at 10% probability and increasing by 10% 474 

approximately every 50 trials 40 ) until reaching 50% by the end of the day. After ~100 trials, we increased the 475 

no -go probability to 80% and required three consecutive CR trials before returning to 50% probability 75 . This 476

Quantification of Representational Drift. To quantify representational drift, population activity vectors 542 

across cortical areas were compared across days using correlation -based metrics (i.e., a vector of 25 values 543 

for each of the 5 days, representing the ΔF/F for a specific time frame/epoch). This can be done for a specific 544 

time epoch, for example within the Stimulus period (e.g. Fig 2) or for each time frame separately (e.g., Fig. 545 

3). These analyses were do ne for each mouse and layer separately. 546 

We quantified representational drift at the population level using several metrics: 547 

• Cross -day correlation relative to day 1 : We quantified Pearson ’s correlation (R ²) between the 548 

response vector on day 1 and the respo nse vector on each subsequent day (Fig. 2c). Lower R² values 549 

indicate reduced similarity between response vectors across days 46 .550 

• Rate of change in R² across days: Using the R² values computed between day 1 and each subsequent 551 

day (yielding a vector of fi ve R² values), we quantified the slope of the best linear fit (Fig. 2d). More 552 

negative slope values indicate a stronger degree of representational drift. 553 

• Correlation angle across days: As an additional metric of representational drift, we calculated the 554 

angle between the linear fit relating day 1 to each subsequent day and the line representing a perfect 555 

correspondence (diagonal line; Fig. 1e). An increasingly negative slope of the linear fit across days 556 

indicates a progressive divergence in representatio ns, consistent with representational drift. 557 

• Inter -day correlation matrices : To assess representational stability across days, we calculated 558 

Pearson ’s correlation coefficients (r) between response vectors from all possible pairs of recording 559 

days, yielding a symmetric 5 × 5 correlation matrix (Fig. 2f). Reduced correlation values with increasing 560 

distance from the diagonal indicate progressive dissimilarity between temporally distant days, 561 

consistent with representational drift. To further quantify this effec t, correlation values were plotted 562 

as a function of inter -day interval (Fig. 2g), and the slope of the corresponding linear fit was extracted 563 

as a measure of drift magnitude. 564 

• Temporal drift measures : The slope extracted from the inter -day correlation analy sis was calculated 565 

independently for each time frame, yielding a temporal profile of drift dynamics across the trial (Fig. 566 

3a,b). More negative slope values indicate a stronger temporal expression of representational drift at 567 

a given time point. 568 

• Drift with in individual cortical areas: To characterize long -term response changes within a given 569 

cortical area, we calculated the Pearson correlation coefficient (r) between response magnitude 570 

(averaged across the Stimulus period) and recording day across 30 trial bins for each cortical area (Fig. 571 

4c). This analysis was further extended to individual time frames, yielding a temporal profile of 572 

correlation values throughout the trial structure (Fig. 5a,b). Positive r values indicate progressive 573 

increases in activity across days, whereas negative r values reflect long -term decreases in response 574 

amplitude. 575
