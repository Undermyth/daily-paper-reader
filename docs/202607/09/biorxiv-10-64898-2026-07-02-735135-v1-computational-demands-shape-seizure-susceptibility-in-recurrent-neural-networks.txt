Abstract 11 

Brain areas differ in their inherent susceptibility to focal seizures, but the principles governing this risk 12 

remain unclear. While prior work has focused on anatomical and physiological factors, here we observed a 13 

fundamental contribution from the computations performed by the underlying neural network. Handcrafted 14 

and trained recurrent neural networks supporting continuous representations respond to seizure perturba- 15 

tions with higher activity and earlier performance decline relative to matched networks stabilizing discrete, 16 

well-separated states. Consistent with this prediction, in vivo recordings revealed that medial entorhinal 17 

cortex, whose grid cells exhibit continuous attractor dynamics, drives acute epileptiform discharges with 18 

stronger involvement and smoother state trajectories compared to CA3, a hippocampal subfield associated 19 

with discrete memory storage. Moreover, selective synaptic silencing demonstrated that this difference in 20 

seizure responses depends on intact entorhinal connectivity. Thus, the computations that enable neural 21 

networks to process information also influence their vulnerability to pathological transitions. 22 

# Introduction 23 

Seizures are states of excessive neural activity associated with neurological deficits 1. Focal seizures originate 24 

in a single brain area, and identifying their sites of onset and understanding their underlying pathogenesis 25 

remains an important clinical goal. While in some cases their anatomical selectivity reflects chronic pathological 26 

changes localized to one brain region, focal seizures can also arise from acute perturbations to otherwise healthy 27 

brains. Moreover, their onset can be focal even with brain-wide perturbations such as body temperature 28 

changes, drug administration, and electrolyte disturbances 2–4 , which indicates that brain regions have inherently 29 

different susceptibilities to developing seizures. The factors that govern this intrinsic vulnerability and its 30 

variation across the brain are not fully known. Several properties involving local anatomy and physiology 31 

have been implicated in the initiation of acute seizures, including neuronal excitability, network disinhibition, 32 

and excitatory circuit motifs 5–11 . While these properties may contribute to regional differences in seizure 33 

susceptibility, direct comparisons between brain areas and connections to broader conceptual frameworks have 34 

been lacking. 35 

Brain areas differ not only in anatomy and physiology but also in the tasks performed by their underlying 36 

neural networks. Task computations have inherent demands on network dynamics, which may also shape 37 

pathological dynamics under ictogenic conditions. This idea is especially relevant for recurrent neural networks 38 

(RNNs), whose connectivity can amplify perturbations into seizures 13 . RNN dynamics can be conceptualized by 39 

an energy landscape across network state space in which states with lower energy are more preferred (Fig. 1A). 40 

RNNs with a continuum of low-energy states can maintain and track continuous variables. Alternatively, RNNs 41

more continuous features will provide easy dynamical paths through which excessive activity can be amplified. 44 

In contrast, the energy barriers in landscapes with more discrete features will constrain excessive activity 45 

and prevent its spread to other states represented by other neurons. The hippocampal-entorhinal system 46

We explore seizure susceptibility in three RNN systems that perform continuous and discrete computations 51 

(Fig. 1B). First, we simulate continuous and discrete attractor networks, whose synaptic weights are designed to 52 

create precise energy landscapes. Second, we train spiking RNNs to perform either a continuous autoencoding 53 

version or a discrete classification version of the same memory task, letting optimization rather than handcrafted 54 

connectivity shape network dynamics. Third, we test these ideas experimentally through Neuropixels recordings 55 

in mouse MEC and CA3. Across these three systems, we measure population-level responses and observe internal 56 

network state dynamics as they are destabilized by acute seizure perturbations, allowing us to ask whether the 57 

computations that empower their performance also determine how they fail. 58

leaky integrate-and-fire dynamics with parameter values matched between continuous and discrete attractors. 63

These networks indeed function as continuous and discrete attractors. Continuous attractors with similar 74 

architectures have been used to model entorhinal grid cells 25,26 , and accordingly, our network maintains contin- 75

state, and the complete 2D attractor manifold can be traversed through uniform translations of the bumps. Our 77

one such assembly, and every other similarly spaced square sublattice represents another attractor state. 79 

To these functional networks, we apply a disinhibitory perturbation with a current that effectively lowers the 80 

resting membrane potential of the inhibitory neurons. With a small perturbation, the continuous attractor no 81 

longer maintains a stable grid as its bumps grow, drift, and intermittently merge (Fig. 2E, left; Supp. Fig. 1). 82 

This corresponds to an increase in population activity relative to that of the discrete attractor (Fig. 2D,F), 83 

which still maintains stable attractor states (Fig. 2E, right). Under high disinhibition, the continuous attractor 84 

produces activity wavefronts that continually propagate across the neural sheet (Fig. 2G, left; Supp. Fig. 1). 85 

Neurons participate sequentially in these wavefronts, which permits a high level of population activity despite 86 

each neuron’s relative refractory period after spiking and resetting its membrane potential (Fig. 2H). Mean- 87 

while, the discrete attractor mixes multiple assemblies (Fig. 2G, right). The dense recurrent excitation within 88 

each assembly causes neurons to spike and become relatively refractory in synchrony, so the network exhibits 89 

population bursts instead of sustaining high population activity (Fig. 2H). 90 

Overall, disinhibitory current disrupts both networks’ ability to maintain their original attractor states, as 91 

quantified by grid and assembly scores (Fig. 2I; Methods). Continuous attractors experience earlier performance 92 

degradation, indicating that their computations, which require a higher degree of symmetry, are more unstable. 93 

They also respond with higher population activity (Fig. 2J,K). The greater vulnerability of continuous attractor 94 

networks persists over a wide range of network parameters and largely holds across alternative perturbations 95 

involving synaptic strength (Supp. Fig. 2). 96 

## RNNs trained on continuous and discrete tasks 97 

To explore the generality of our observations beyond attractor networks with handcrafted weights, we turn to 98 

trained RNNs, whose connectivities are defined through task optimization. Our RNNs have a recurrent layer 99 

of spiking leaky integrate-and-fire neurons, which receive inputs from a layer of rate-based neurons and send 100 

outputs to another layer of rate-based neurons of equal width (Fig. 3A; Methods). The input and output layers 101 

are linearly ordered with periodic boundaries. The RNNs are trained to perform one of two memory tasks 102 

(Fig. 3B). In both tasks, the input layer presents a bump of activity centered around a particular position for 103 

20 time steps. In the continuous memory task, the RNN must produce an activity bump at the same position 104 

in the output layer for time steps 21 to 100. In the discrete memory task, the RNN must determine whether the 105 

input bump is centered in the top or bottom half of the input layer, and then it must produce an activity bump 106 

centered within the correct half of the output layer for time steps 21 to 100. Thus, the location of the output 107 

bump is the only difference between the two tasks; it is determined via a continuous or a discrete input–output 108 

mapping. 109 

The RNNs are trained end-to-end with backpropagation through time and surrogate gradients (Methods; 110 

Supp. Fig. 3). The total loss equals a mean-squared-error task loss plus regularizer terms that encourage 111 

the continuous- and discrete-trained RNNs to solve their tasks using the same level of recurrent activity and 112

## High-density neural recordings in MEC and CA3 141 

To test whether our simulation results apply to biological neural networks, we recorded simultaneously from 142 

mouse MEC layers II and III (L2/3) and CA3 using Neuropixels probes (Fig. 4A; Supp. Fig. 6; Methods). In 143 

our simulations, we applied an acute, uniform perturbation to both continuous and discrete networks for a fair 144 

comparison between them. We experimentally implemented this manipulation with an acute intraperitoneal 145 

(i.p.) injection of pentylenetetrazole (PTZ), a GABA A receptor antagonist 27,28 . To study the intrinsic suscep- 146 

tibilities of these brain regions and reduce confounding from external input or behavior, we recorded under 147 

urethane anesthesia, a state which preserves physiological activity in the hippocampal-entorhinal circuit 29–31 . 148 

Upon a high-dose injection of PTZ, epileptiform discharges were visible in the local field potential (LFP) 149 

of both MEC and CA3 in wildtype (wt) mice (Fig. 4B). To examine the differential contributions of MEC 150 

and CA3 to this pathophysiology, we virally expressed the tetanus toxin light chain gene (tetX) specifically in 151 

superficial MEC excitatory neurons (tetMEC) or in CA3 pyramidal cells (tetCA3) in separate animal cohorts 152 

(Fig. 4C,E; Supp. Figs. 6 and 7; Methods). Tetanus toxin prevents neurotransmitter release at axon terminals, 153 

which blocks synaptic transmission without impairing action potential generation. Under PTZ, tetMEC mice 154 

exhibited markedly weaker discharges in both MEC and CA3 (Fig. 4D), while in tetCA3 mice, discharges 155 

decreased in CA3 but persisted in MEC (Fig. 4F; Supp. Fig. 8). Quantification confirmed that silencing MEC 156 

synaptic outputs significantly decreased peak LFP amplitude in both MEC and CA3 compared to wt mice, 157 

demonstrating their necessity for discharges in both regions (Fig. 4G–I). While silencing CA3 synaptic outputs 158 

significantly decreased CA3 peak amplitudes, MEC peak amplitudes remained similar to those of wt mice, 159

Next, we investigate the network mechanisms of PTZ-induced destabilization. Our simulations predict 169 

that continuous and discrete networks should exhibit distinct responses to mild perturbations that destabilize 170 

attractor states without causing seizures. Continuous attractors should drift along the low-dimensional attractor 171 

manifold with high network state overlap from one moment to the next, while discrete attractors should hop 172 

among attractor states spread out in high-dimensional state space with low temporal overlap (Fig. 5A). These 173 

expected dynamics are confirmed by simulations (Fig. 5B) and quantified by temporal continuity , defined as the 174 

cosine similarity of the detrended network state between successive time bins (Methods). Continuous attractors 175 

exhibit higher temporal continuity than discrete attractors, which have values closer to zero (Fig. 5C,D). 176 

To experimentally test these predictions, we used the same recording paradigm, except with a low PTZ 177 

dose to perturb the network without entering the seizure regime. Without large epileptiform discharges that 178 

can overshadow action potential waveforms, we could perform spike sorting to yield simultaneously recorded 179 

neurons in MEC and CA3 (Fig. 5E; Supp. Fig. 10; Methods). These regions showed no significant differences 180 

in their overall levels of population activity (Fig. 5F). However, MEC and CA3 network states in wt mice 181 

demonstrated markedly different distributions of temporal continuity that agree with those of continuous and 182 

discrete attractor networks, respectively (Fig. 5G–I; Supp. Fig. 11). Moreover, in tetMEC mice, MEC temporal 183 

continuity decreased towards zero, demonstrating that the overlapping trajectories traversed under PTZ can 184 

only be accessed with MEC synaptic outputs intact. Silencing CA3 synaptic outputs in tetCA3 mice did not 185 

affect MEC temporal continuity. This agreement between experiment and simulation suggests that the MEC 186 

response to PTZ administration reflects continuous features in its network dynamics that are established through 187 

recurrent connectivity. 188 

In summary, our experimental results support an explanatory model in which an ictogenic perturbation, in 189 

the form of PTZ administration, first induces MEC population activity to drift through nearby states while 190 

the CA3 network state fluctuates with less temporal continuity (Fig. 5J). MEC synaptic outputs are required 191 

for its overlapping trajectories, independently of CA3. With a large enough perturbation, MEC becomes 192 

fully destabilized and exhibits large seizure discharges facilitated by its synaptic outputs, which also provide a 193 

necessary contribution to large seizure discharges in CA3. 194 

# Discussion 195 

Our results establish a link between network computation and seizure susceptibility through three complemen- 196 

tary approaches that impose computational demands in distinct ways. The demands of attractor networks are 197 

structurally defined by choosing their synaptic connectivities, those of trained RNNs are functionally defined by 198 

the tasks for which they are optimized, and those of networks in the hippocampal-entorhinal region arise through 199 

biological processes. In all three cases, we observed that the network performing more continuous computations 200 

responds more intensely to ictogenic perturbations than its discrete counterpart. Continuous attractors and 201 

continuous-trained RNNs maintain higher activity levels and demonstrate earlier task degradation across wide 202 

ranges of model configurations. In vivo , MEC synaptic outputs drive the amplification of epileptiform discharges 203 

in both MEC and CA3 with destabilization dynamics characteristic of our attractor simulations. 204 

Our findings interface with previous observations related to seizures. Many reports have also identified 205

Although MEC and CA3 contain heterogeneous neural populations and exhibit complex dynamics that 215 

deviate from idealized attractor models, our comparison between them is best understood as a contrast between 216 

networks skewed towards different computational topologies. Moreover, focal seizures arise within many other 217 

brain regions, and further experimental efforts can determine how broadly our ideas apply to contrasts in 218 

computation throughout the brain. Future theoretical extensions include the implementation of richer neuronal 219 

dynamics and interactions across brain regions 7,42,43 . Still, our networks can clearly illustrate our conceptual 220 

ideas, and they can demonstrate excessive activity and functional impairment, which are defining features of 221 

seizures common to all types 1. 222 

Our work reflects broader connections between biological function and dysfunction. Computational psychi- 223 

atry uses cognitive computations and their alterations to address mental disorders 44–46 ; likewise, our results 224 

suggest that neural network computations may provide insight into neurological disorders. More generally, 225 

the function–dysfunction duality we identify is conceptually related to the performance–robustness trade-off 226 

in control theory 47 , in which more responsive feedback systems are often less stable, and to the fluctuation– 227 

dissipation theorem in statistical physics 48 , in which thermodynamic systems that respond more strongly to 228 

external inputs also exhibit larger internal fluctuations. Here, we have shown how neural systems that can 229 

responsively encode continuous changes, in contrast to settling into rigid, discrete states, inherently suffer from 230 

greater seizure vulnerability. 231

## Attractor networks 233

excitatory and inhibitory populations P ∈ { E, I}. One excitatory neuron and one inhibitory neuron are located 236

potential is reset to 0: sP (r, t ) ← 1 and vP (r, t ) ← 0. Otherwise, sP (r, t ) = 0. We prevented vP from decreasing 239

Simulation protocols 289 

We initialized attractor networks with membrane potentials consistent with an attractor state. For the contin- 290 

uous attractor network, we first identified the steady-state triangular lattice approached by the network over 291 

a long simulation with random initial potentials. We generated a membrane potential profile by placing 2D 292 

Gaussian bumps at each lattice point with radius 0.1 times the lattice spacing. The profile was rescaled to span 293 

membrane potential values from 0.5 to 0.95 and was used to initialize the continuous attractor networks. For the 294 

discrete attractors, we randomly chose one of the assemblies and initialized its neurons with uniformly random 295 

membrane potentials between 0.5 and 0.95; all other neurons were given potentials 0. We finally added a small 296 

amount of Gaussian noise to each neuron in both networks. In the discrete attractor network, the connectivity 297 

matrix was also generated through a stochastic permutation process, so a different realization was used in each 298 

replica. 299 

Unless otherwise specified, we ran each simulation for 20 s and discarded data from the first 10 s. Standard 300 

unperturbed network parameters are provided in Table 1. Each perturbation is a change in these network 301 

parameters and was applied from the start of the simulation. Disinhibitory current is defined as the decrease in 302 

inhibitory current bI from the unperturbed value. E-to-I weight decrease and I-to-E weight increase are defined 303 

to be changes in weights wIE and wEI , respectively, from the unperturbed value. 304

## Trained RNNs 321 

Network architecture and dynamics 322 

Our RNNs consist of a central recurrent layer of spiking neurons with membrane potentials v(t) and spike indi- 323 

cators s(t). Their leaky integrate-and-fire dynamics are implemented based on the snnTorch Python package 51 . 324 

If neuron i’s potential exceeds a threshold of 1 at time step t, it fires a spike and its potential is reset to 0: 325 

si(t) ← 1 and vi(t) ← 0. Otherwise, si(t) = 0. 326 

The recurrent layer neurons receive all-to-all connections from a rate-based input layer x(t) in addition to 327 

their all-to-all recurrent connections: 328 

v(t + 1) = βv(t) + Win x(t) + Ws(t) + b + η(t), (10) where Win are the input-to-recurrent weights, W are the recurrent-to-recurrent weights, b are the recurrent 329 

biases, η(t) represents independent, zero-mean Gaussian noise, and β is the potential decay factor. The recurrent 330 

layer neurons send all-to-all connections to a rate-based output layer y(t): 331 

y(t) = Wout s(t) + bout , (11) where Wout are the recurrent-to-output weights, and bout are the output biases. 332

Training and evaluation protocols 353 

We trained all weights and biases, which were initialized with PyTorch 2.10.0 default settings. During each 354 

training epoch out of 1000 total, we ran the RNN for 100 time steps on a batch of Nio inputs. We promoted 355 

even sampling of input space within each batch by centering one bump at each input neuron and then jittering 356 

bump positions by uniformly random values between −0.5 and 0 .5. After this forward pass, we computed the 357 

total loss Eq. 21 summed over the batch, and we backpropagated errors using the Adam optimizer with learning 358 

rate 10 −3, weight decay 0, β1 = 0 .9, and β2 = 0 .999. To backpropagate through spike discontinuities, we used 359 

the fast sigmoid surrogate gradient function with snnTorch 0.9.4 default settings 51 . 360 

After training, we froze all weights and biases and evaluated these RNNs over 500 time steps with input 361 

bumps presented for the first 20 time steps. We used an evaluation batch of Nio input bumps centered between 362 

neurons at positions 0 .5, 1.5, . . . , N io − 0.5. Outputs between time steps 21 and 100 are used to compute the 363

Recurrent layer sorting 384 

We developed a method to order neurons in the recurrent layer based on the learned recurrent weight matrix 385 

W after training (Algorithm 1). It is related to spectral clustering 52 , which uses eigenvalues to group data by 386 

similarity, and similar ideas have been used for circular ordering as well as neural connectivity data analysis 53,54 . 387 

It aims to uncover a circular neural ordering in the trained recurrent layer that reflects the circular organization 388 

of the input and output layers. We designed the algorithm for the continuous task, since its targets form a 389 

circular manifold in state space, but we found that it also recovers meaningful structure in discrete-trained 390 

RNNs. 391 

We assume that over the course of training, W has learned to maintain a set of stable states that form 392 

a circular manifold in state space. To do so, we expect that it contains the structure of a circulant matrix, 393 

like those of continuous ring attractors, but its circular ordering is scrambled by a permutation over neural 394 

indices and obscured by noise. We seek to uncover this ordering by inverting the permutation. The eigenvectors 395 

of W whose eigenvalues have largest real parts correspond to population activity patterns that dominate the 396 

linearized network dynamics. We thus sort the eigenpairs by the real part of their eigenvalues in decreasing order. 397 

The eigenvectors of any circulant matrix are the Fourier modes, and a permuted circulant matrix simply has 398 

correspondingly permuted Fourier eigenvectors with the same eigenvalues. We expect that the first eigenvector 399 

will be the first Fourier mode with a circular topology; the zeroth Fourier mode corresponds to overall changes 400 

in activity, and higher order modes correspond to multiple windings around the circle. Thus, we compute the 401 

complex argument for each element in the first eigenvector, and their ordering inverts the permutation of the 402 

first Fourier mode. 403

Spike sorting 546 

Extracellular spike waveforms were detected and clustered into putative single-neuron spike trains using the 547 

Kilosort 4.1.1 and SpikeInterface 0.103.2 Python packages 63,64 . Neuropixels recordings from recording start 548 

to 50 min after low-dose PTZ injection were extracted and then preprocessed with phase correction, global 549 

common average referencing, and highpass filtering above 300 Hz. Kilosort4 was run with max peels = 200 , 550 

which increases the number of spike detection iterations as previously suggested for MEC recordings 19 . 551 

The inter-spike interval (ISI) violation ratio computed with the SpikeInterface compute isi violations 552 

function was primarily used to assess the quality of each cluster. It was computed using a permissive ISI 553 

threshold of 1 ms to allow for more rapid spiking dynamics under PTZ perturbations. Clusters with ISI 554 

violation ratio below 0.2 were considered to represent single units and accepted for further analysis. All other 555 

clusters were subjected to an incremental pruning of spikes with outlying waveforms as follows. For each 556 

cluster, the spatiotemporal profile of each spike was projected into a 4-dimensional space consisting of the 557 

top 2 waveform PCs of the 2 channels with largest trough-to-peak template amplitudes. Each dimension was 558 

z-scored, all points with distance greater than 8 from the origin were excluded, and remaining points were 559 

whitened. These manipulations represent a linear transformation of spike waveform shape into a standardized 560 

space. Each cluster was then incrementally pruned by removing, in increasing percentages from 1% to 30%, the 561 

spikes furthest from the origin in this standardized space. At each step, the ISI violation ratio of the remaining 562 

spikes was recomputed, and if it decreased below 0.2, then the pruning process was terminated and the resulting 563 

cluster was accepted for further analysis. Clusters whose ISI violation ratio never fell below 0.2 under pruning 564 

were excluded. 565 

All accepted clusters were manually curated with the phy 2.0 Python package 65 to exclude clusters with 566 

markedly artifactual waveform shapes and to split and merge clusters where clearly appropriate. All clusters 567 

with firing rates below 0.2 Hz were excluded. 568
