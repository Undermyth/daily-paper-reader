# Abstract 4

The rapid and coordinated propagation of neural activity across a network of distributed brain 5

regions underpins complex behavior and cognition. Yet how multiple distributed processes are 6

facilitated in parallel across time scales and behavioral states remains unclear. Using 7

simultaneous fast wide -field voltage and hemodynamic imaging in awake mice, we show that 8

behavior differentially shapes cortical network dynamics across frequencies and signal types .9

Although static functional connectivity preserved canonical networks across frequencies, the 10 

underlying dynamics diverged. Low -frequency and hemodynamic activity were dominated by 11 

behavior -dependent persistence of network states, whereas higher -frequency activity exhibited 12 

stable network structure with modulation through changes in state expression. Despite these 13 

differences, shared network organization was preserved across timescales and signal types. This 14 

indicat es that similar functional connectivity can arise from distinct temporal dynamics. These 15 

findings reveal that cortical networks are governed by frequency -dependent principles through 16 

which behavior shapes the persistence and expression of large -scale brain states. 17 

# Introduction 18 

Behavior arises from rapid interactions among distributed brain regions that flexibly reconfigure. 19 

These coordinated patterns of activity give rise to spatiotemporally structured correlations that 20 

enable the brain to route information and perform different cognitive computations at various 21 

timescales . Converging evidence suggests that behavior and arousal are tightly linked to 22 

transient, recurring whole -brain states in both neural and hemodynamic signals [1, 2] . Yet how 23 

behavior shape s the expression of large -scale network structure across timescales from slow 24 

hemodynamic signals to fast neural activity remains unknown . Here, we test the hypothesis that 25 

large -scale cortical networks are constrained by a shared spatial scaffold but are expressed 26 

through frequency -dependent dynamics that are differentially modulated by behavioral state. 27 

There is growing evidence that spontaneous activity can be decomposed into low -dimensional, 28 

transient states [3, 4] . This principle generalizes across various signal sources [5 -7] , from 29 

hemodynamic measures , which rely on low -frequency oscillations below 0.1 Hz [8, 9] , to30 

electrical activity that fluctuates synchronously across distributed brain regions spanning five 31 

orders of magnitude from approximately 0.05 Hz to 500 Hz [10 -12] . Additionally ,32 

electrophysiology studies to date suggest that a multitude of neural operations can occur in 33 

parallel across these timescales [13] . This multiplexing produces multiple dynami c network 34 

configurations at different timescales, which in combination are often inferred to represent the 35 

slow hemodynamic networks observed [7] . Occurrence of these transient states on the timescale 36

Large -scale hemodynamic network structure may reflect an underlying repertoire of dynamic 40 

brain states , but key questions remain about how this structure relates to underlying neural 41 

dynamics across timescales. It is unclear whether the spatial organization of functional networks 42 

is preserved across frequencies or whether distinct temporal regimes give rise to fundamentally 43 

different architectures. Moreover, although behavioral and arousal -related changes are known 44 

to influence global brain activity, it remains unclear whether they shape network dynamics by 45 

altering the persistence of brain states, their selection, or both, and whether these effects 46 

generalize across timescales. 47 

To address these questions , we used high –frame -rate wide -field voltage imaging with a red 48 

reference channel to simultaneously measure neuronal and hemodynamic activity across the 49 

dorsal cortex of awake mice. Static functional connectivity derived from voltage signals revealed 50 

canonical resting -state networks preserved across frequencies up to gamma, whereas 51 

hemodynamic connectivity reflected a similar structure primarily at low frequencies. Despite this 52 

shared spatial organization, network dynamics diverged across timescales: low -frequency and 53 

hemodynamic activity were dominated by temporally persistent states with reduced cross -54 

session generalizability . Higher -frequency activity exhibited more stable state structure with 55 

distinct patterns of expression compared to slower timescales . Together, these findings 56 

demonstrate that stable network architecture coexists with frequency -dependent dynamical 57 

regimes, through which behavior shapes the temporal organization of large -scale cortical activity. 58

## Functional Connectivity is Preserved Across a Broad Range of Frequencies 203 

The preservation of large -scale network organization across neuronal and hemodynamic signals 204 

raises the question of whether a similar organizational principle extends across neuronal 205 

timescales. If functional connectivity reflects a fundamental property of cortical organization, 206 

canonical network structure should remain evident despite large differences in the temporal 207 

characteristics of the underlying activity. To test this, we broke down the recorded JEDI -1P signal 208 

into canonical frequency bands : infraslow [0.05 -0.1Hz], slow [0.1 -1Hz], delta [1 -4Hz], theta [4 -209 

8Hz], alpha [8 -13Hz], beta [13 -30Hz], and gamma [30 -60]. Using the filtered , band -limited JEDI -210 

1P signal we calculated seed -based FC maps and FC matrices (Fig2A ,B, and C ). This analysis 211 

revealed a conserved network organization across all frequency bands for all four selected seeds: 212 

Barrel, M2, RSP and V1 seeds , with all 4 seeds exhibiting significant correlations and 213 

anticorrelations following multiple comparison corrections (Fig2A) . This preserved connectome 214 

structure was corroborated by the static FC matrices ( Fig2B and Fig2C, bottom left triangle) . In 215 

contrast, the mCherry signal exhibit ed this canonical network structure , which included the 216 

anticorrelation, only in the low frequencies up to the delta band (Supp Fig2 -1 B) . Statistically 217 

significant anti correlations in the static FC analysis did not extend past the slow frequency ( Supp 218 

Fig2 -1 A) . Higher -frequency pixels were broadly correlated with one an other, likely due to a 219 

broad distribution of common non -neuronal sources s uch as respiration and heart rate that are 220 

present in the mCherry signal ( Supp Fig2 -1 A -B). When comparing the band -limited JEDI FC 221 

matrices to the <1Hz mCherry matrix, there is an increasing number of matrix elements that 222 

differ ed between the two as a function of increasing frequency ( Fig2B , top right triangle ). This 223 

was assessed using a t-test comparing z-transformed bandlimited JEDI FC correlation values to 224 

hemodynamic FC correlation values . Plotted are the positive and negative thresholded T values 225 

corrected for multiple comparisons. On average the hemodynamic signal has higher connectivity 226 

for pixels within the somatosensory network compared with the band -limited slow –gamma band 227 

JEDI signal (Fig2B , top right triangle, purple T < 0 ). The JEDI signal across frequencies exhibits 228 

significantly stronger correlations within the DMN and with the DMN and pixels from SoMo and 229 

VIS networks (Fig2B , top right triangle, yellow T > 0 ).230

Together, these findings demonstrate that large -scale cortical functional networks are 251 

preserved across a broad range of neuronal timescales. Although the magnitude of connectivity 252 

progressively decreased with increasing frequenc y, the overall spatial organization of canonical 253 

networks remained remarkably stable. The high reproducibility of this network structure across 254 

sessions, animals, and imaging modalities further suggests that static functional connectivity 255 

captures a robust and conserved feature of cortical organization. However, the preservation of 256 

network topography does not necessarily imply that the underlying dynamics are the same, this 257 

motivated a closer examination of how these networks are expressed over time. 258

## Different Timescales 294 

So far, we have analyzed this resting state data within the context of static FC , which looks at 295 

time -averaged signal correlations across areas. However , resting state activity is known to be 296 

highly dynamic, resulting in several different network configurations that can unfold throughout 297 

the duration of a single resting state session [3, 4] . To capture these resting state dynamics, we 298 

extracted co -activation patterns (CAPs) , which are commonly used in dynamic rsfMRI analysis 299 

[21] . Similar to other work that uses CAPs on preclinical neuroimaging data [22] , we ran this 300 

analysis without any a priori intensity -based restrictions. The CAPs obtained represent recurring, 301 

momentary network -level activity patterns that characterize different brain regions that are 302

concurrently activated or deactivate d. CAPs are al so defined at the temporal resolution of the 303 

data, making them well -suited for this cross -frequency study. 304 

CAPs were identified on a per -session basis for all signals and frequency bands that we also used 305 

for the static analysis. This includes the slow mCherry hemodynamic signal, the broadband JEDI 306 

voltage signal, along with the band -limited JEDI signals (Fig3A ). CAPs were obtained by running 307 

k-means clustering on concatenated z -scored data on a per -session and per -frequency basis for 308 

clusters ranging from 2 -26. This results in K number of CAPs based on the selected K cluster 309 

number. Each CAP is an image obtained from averaging all frames together that were clustered 310 

based on their spatial similarity. The optimal number of CAPs for each signal type was then 311 

obtained by looking at the elbow point in the gain of percent variance explained as a function of 312 

increasing K (Supp Fig3 -1A) . The mean cluster number K, and therefore the mean number of CAPs 313 

did not greatly differ across signal types ( Fig3B, purple dots ) ranging on average between 13 (for 314 

theta) to 20 ( for hemodynamics, delta, gamma and broadband signals) CAPs. Despite similar 315 

number of ideal CAPs, w hat did vary across signal types was the percent of the variance that 316 

these CAPs explained in the data. Time -varying changes in hemodynamic resting state activity 317 

were best explained by CAPs, accounting for an average of 65 % of the explained variance (Fig3B, 318

occupancy patterns exceeded chance levels , these values were compared to a shuffled control 494 

null distribution . The observed occupancy matrix was then z -scored relative to this null 495 

distribution, producing a z -scored occupancy (enrichment) matrix that reflects the degree to 496

for multiple comparisons using FDR . Occupancy reflects the fraction of time that a given CAP 499 

occurs during a given behavioral state . There were certain CAPs that are preferentially expressed 500

the rest periods , while CAP 4 is expressed in the movement states (Fig5 D).502

given state or because it lasts a long time each time it occurs , we also calculated the dwell time 504 

across behavioral states. Dwell time was defined as the duration (in frames) of each CAP event. 505 

For each CAP and behavioral state, we computed the mean of dwell times across all events. This 506 

resulted in a CAP -by -state dwell time matrix for each frequency band (Fig5E , Fig5 -1B ), capturing 507 

how long each CAP persists under different behavioral conditions. In contrast to the occupancy ,508 

dwell time reflects how long a given CAP is maintained once engaged; for theta band CAPs , there 509 

is less of a distinction across behavioral states , but differences are larger across CAPs. Where CAP 510 

5 has the longest dwell time across conditions, while CAP 4 has the lowest (Fig5E ). This suggests 511 

that behavioral modulation in theta is driven more strongly by changes in CAP 512 

occurrence/selection than by prolonged CAP persistence. 513

Behavior Shapes the Temporal Organization of Large -Scale Cortical Activity 624 

A growing body of work suggests that spontaneous brain activity reflects a low -dimensional 625 

dynamical system in which spatially structured fluctuations give rise to coordinated large -scale 626 

networks. These dynamics are closely linked to behavioral and arousal states, particularly 627 

during quiet rest, where strongly fluctuating patterns of activity across distributed regions 628 

contribute substantially to interregional correlations [1] . Recent studies further show that 629 

spontaneous behaviors are accompanied by rapid changes in both the magnitude and 630 

correlational structure of cortical activity, underscoring the tight coupling between behavior 631 

and large -scale brain dynamics [15, 34] .632 

Consistent with this framework, our results demonstrate that behavioral changes shape cortical 633 

network activity in a frequency -dependent manner. At low frequencies for both infraslow 634 

neural and hemodynamic activity , behavioral modulation primarily alter ed the persistence of 635 

network states, leading to prolonged dwell times and sustained periods of coordinated activity 636 

in either prolonged movement or prolonged rest periods . In contrast, higher -frequency activity 637 

was dominated by changes in state occurrence, with stable network motifs being flexibly 638 

recruited over time rather than persistently maintained. These findings indicate that similar 639 

large -scale network configurations can support fundamentally different temporal organizations 640 

depending on frequency and behavioral state. Importantly, despite differences in dynamic 641 

expression across frequencies, reconstructed activity preserved the overall static FC structure, 642 

even when low -frequency CAP basis sets explained less variance across sessions than session -643 

specific CAPs. This suggests that large -scale functional organization is constrained by a stable 644 

spatial scaffold, whereas behavior primarily modulates the temporal expression and sampling 645 

of these network configurations across timescales. 646

particularly relevant for lower -frequency analyses, where longer recordings may be necessary to 664 

fully capture slow dynamics and a broader range of behavioral variability. As such, low -frequency 665 

CAPs may exhibit greater convergence across sessions and explain more variance if longer trials 666 

were used to better sample diverse brain and behavioral states. Second , while the mCherry signal 667 

was used both for hemodynamic correction and as a proxy for hemodynamic signal changes , it 668 

provides a less direct readout compared to approaches that use interleaved diffuse reflectance 669 

imaging of red and green light [20] . Although similar strategies have been successfully employed 670 

in studies, the mCherry signal remains a composite optical measure of hemoglobin absorption 671 

and fluorescence , similar in many ways to the hemodynamic signal obtained from the ratiometric 672 

VSFP -Butterfly sensor. While also not a perfect readout of HbT , HbR or HbO, VSFP -Butterfly has 673 

proven to be useful in many studies relating neural to hemodynamic activity [27, 35, 36] . We 674 

believe mCherry can serve a similar role when performing voltage imaging at much higher frame 675 

rates , where interleaving of additional LEDs for hemodynamic correction are not feasible . Thirdly, 676 

we derived CAPs from band -limited signals, and temporal filtering may introduce dependencies 677 

that transform discrete state transitions into continuous mixtures of underlying patterns. As a 678 

result, the observed variability in low -frequency CAPs reflects not only neural dynamics but also 679 

the interaction between temporal integration and behavioral variability . Clustering approaches 680 

impose a discrete representation onto what is likely a continuous state space, and the number 681 

of CAPs may differentially constrain single -session and group -level analyses, particularly at low 682 

frequencies where greater heterogeneity may require higher model complexity. 683 

One possible interpretation of our findings is that cortical dynamics reflect at least two partially 684 

overlapping modes of state organization operating on different timescales. Slow resting state 685 

fluctuations, which dominate hemodynamic and low -frequency voltage activity, may reflect 686 

gradual global changes in behavioral state or internal state that stabilize network configurations 687 

over extended periods. In contrast, faster neural activity may capture more rapid transitions 688 

associated with moment -to -moment behavioral engagement, enabling flexible selection of 689 

network configurations without prolonged persistence. Under this framework, slow activity 690 

shapes the background state in which cortical networks operate, whereas faster dynamics 691 

support rapid reconfiguration within that state. Rather than reflecting distinct network 692 

architectures, these dyn am ics may represent complementary temporal modes through which 693 

the same large -scale cortical networks are organized and expressed. 694

across pixels to normalize spatial variance. Pairwise spatial similarity between CAPs was 938 

quantified using Pearson correlation, yielding a CAP –CAP correlation matrix. This similarity matrix 939 

was converted to a distance matrix defined as 1-r, where r denotes the spatial correlation. 940 

Hierarchical clustering was performed on this distance matrix using average linkage. Aggregated 941 

CAP groups were then defined by cutting the dendrogram at a fixed distance threshold (cutoff = 942 

0.8), producing a set of spatially coherent CAP clusters shared across sessions. This cutoff was 943 

selected after visual inspection of the optimal leaf order of the resulting dendrogram across 944 

frequencies . The obtained basis set from this method was used to highlight the fact that CAP 945 

patterns are recurring across sessions and mice, but it does not represent a final “true” set of 946 

CAPs. Rather, it just suggests that activity can be represented by a subset of spatial patterns. 947

and each CAP centroid was calculated. CAPs w ere assign ed to the nearest frames , exactly as in 957

compute d a least -squares scalar gain for that frame, effectively fitting the selected CAP to the 959

CAP Occupancy and Dwell Time Across Behavioral States CAP occupancy was defined as the 963 

conditional probability of observing each CAP given a behavioral state. This was calculated by 964 

normalizing CAP counts within each state by the total number of timepoints in that state. To 965 

assess whether observed occupancy patterns exceeded chance levels, we generated a null 966 

distribution by shuffling behavioral state labels within each session (using circular shifts to 967 

preserve temporal structure) across 1000 iterations. For each shuffle, occupancy was 968 

recomputed, yielding a null distribution of values. The observed occupancy matrix was then z -969 

scored relative to this null distribution, producing a z -scored occupancy (enrichment) matrix that 970 

reflects the degree to which each CAP is over - or under -represented in each behavioral state 971 

compared to chance. Statistical significance was assessed using a two -sided permutation test, 972 

where p -values were computed based on the deviation of observed values from the null mean. 973 

These p -values were subsequently corrected for multiple comparisons across CAPs and states 974 

using the Benjamini –Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure, yielding q -values for each 975 

CAP –state pair. CAP temporal dynamics were assessed using an event -based dwell time analysis. 976 

Contiguous sequences of identical CAP labels were identified as discrete events within each 977 

session, and each event was assigned a behavioral state based on the modal state during that 978 

period. Dwell time was defined as the duration (in frames) of each CAP event. This resulted in a 979 

CAP -by -state dwell time matrix for each frequency band, capturing how long each CAP persists 980 

under different behavioral conditions. 981
