# Abstract 

Disorders of consciousness (DoC) are associated with large-scale alterations in brain dynamics, yet the structural factors that constrain these changes remain unclear. Here, we investigate how the topology of the structural connectome shapes the sensitivity of brain dynamics to perturbation using a whole-brain computational model constrained by diffusion MRI-derived connectivity. We show that a node’s integration within the structural connectome, quantified using a spectral integration measure, strongly predicts its impact on global brain dynamics. Lesions to highly integrative hubs drive the system toward low-complexity dynamical regimes resembling those observed in DoC, particularly posterior medial regions such as the precuneus and posterior cingulate cortex. Analogously, increasing excitability in these regions restores healthy-like dynamics in silico. In contrast, perturbations to weakly integrated regions have limited global effects. These results demonstrate that generic features of structural connectivity constrain whole-brain dynamical stability and help explain why damage to specific hubs disproportionately disrupts conscious brain activity. 

# Introduction 1

Disorders of consciousness (DoC) present a significant challenge in clinical neuroscience, 2

characterized by profound alterations in awareness and responsiveness following severe 3

brain injury [1]. DoCs are commonly classified using the Coma Recovery Scale [2] in the 4

Minimally Conscious State (MCS) when there are traces of volitional responses to 5

external stimuli, or Unresponsive Wakefulness Syndrome (UWS, also known as the 6

Vegetative State) when there are none [3]. 7

A key approach to understanding these alterations has been the study of large-scale 8

patterns of spontaneous brain activity, which reflect the coordinated dynamics of 9

distributed neural systems even in the absence of external stimulation. In DoC patients, 10 

such patterns are systematically disrupted, offering a neurophysiological correlate of 11 

impaired consciousness [4]. Functional Connectivity (FC) is defined as the statistical 12 

correlations between brain areas, usually when no explicit task is being performed 13 

(resting state). Although reduced activity in specific cortical regions (e.g., the precuneus 14 

and cingulate cortex) was long recognized as a hallmark of UWS [5], later PET work 15 

showed that UWS is strongly associated with disruptions in FC in large scale networks 16 

of the resting brain [6] [7], especially the Default Mode Network (DMN). The DMN is a 17 

group of brain areas whose FC is intricately linked with processes such as self-referential 18

Although static FC and dynamic FC (dFC) have been used to characterize the role 27

Whole-brain computational models offer a principled framework to go beyond 33

coupling to reproduce their spontaneous dynamics [13, 16, 17], and that virtual lesions 36 

can recapitulate features of pathological brain states [18]. However, whether the 37 

structural properties of individual nodes can systematically predict their dynamical 38 

influence remains unclear 39 

Here we ask how the structural connectivity profile of individual brain areas shapes 40 

their causal influence on whole-brain dynamics, using virtual lesions and local 41 

perturbations in a computational model fitted to empirical dFC measures in CNT and 42 

DoC patients. To this end, we introduce a whole-brain computational model that 43 

simulates virtual brain lesions and quantifies their impact on dFC. Specifically, we link 44 

a graph-theoretical integration measure, computed from the SC matrix, to the causal 45 

influence each node exerts on brain-wide dFC when it is disconnected or locally 46 

stimulated. This approach contributes to a mechanistic understanding of the structural 47 

underpinnings of dFC and their relevance to disorders of consciousness. 48

## dFC stream jump length distribution 68 

We first sought to characterize the temporal dynamics of FC changes. For this we 69 

calculated the instantaneous phases of BOLD signals between regions defined by the 70 

AAL parcellation ( [21]). This allowed us to represent each time point with a FC matrix 71 

of phase connectivity (see Methods), which we call the dynamic FC stream (dFC 72 

stream). We then calculated the euclidean distance between consecutive FC matrices, 73 

obtaining a distribution of jump lengths for each registering session. This is what we 74 

call ”jump lengths” in the dFC stream: larger jump lengths imply bigger changes in the 75 

connectivity profile. We can characterize the dFC stream by considering these jump 76 

lengths as steps lengths of a random walk, in a random direction chosen isotropically, 77 

and length drawn from a given positive distribution. On average, CNT individuals 78 

display shorter jump lengths than MCS and UWS (mean jump length at 24 .591, 26 .829 79

## Occupation of dynamic states of connectivity in CNT and DoC 108 

After characterizing temporal changes in the dFC stream, we studied the main 109 

spatio-temporal patterns governing brain activity. For this, we leveraged on a 110 

cluster-based analysis developed in [4], and reproduced in several other 111 

studies [22] [23] [24]. We used a K-Means clustering algorithm (K=4, see Methods), 112 

trained over all concatenated and flattened time-resolved FC matrices of the dFC 113 

stream. We sorted centroids according to their resemblance to SC, as measured by the 114 

FC-SC correlation coefficient of each centroid (see Fig 3A). 115 

From the algorithm’s labels (indicating which cluster the dFC belongs to at each 116 

time step), we calculated the transition rate between clusters (Fig3 B) for CNT, MCS 117 

and UWS, from which the Markov chain Shannon entropy rate can be estimated, 118 

obtaining 0 .787 , 0.713 , 0.567 (nats) for CNT, MCS and UWS transition matrices, 119 

respectively. This monotonic decrease in entropy rate from CNT to UWS reflects a 120 

progressive reduction in the diversity of transitions between connectivity states, 121 

consistent with the loss of dynamical richness observed in the jump length distributions. 122 

We define occupancy as the count of how many fMRI volumes our K-means 123 

algorithm assigned to each cluster, and Fig3 C shows the across-individuals average 124 

occupation of each cluster. We also analyzed occupancies per individual, placing each 125 

centroid SC-FC correlation value on an x-axis, and considering the occupancy of each 126 

cluster as a function of its FC-SC resemblance. We found that CNT individuals tend to 127 

occupy the three clusters at nearly the same rate (flat dependence, ρ = 0 .176 , p = 0 .54, 128 

non-significant monotone fit), contrary to MCS and UWS, that consistently occupied 129 

clusters with higher FC-SC correlation (occupancy vs SC-FC resemblance, Spearman’s 130 

ρ = 0 .73 , 0.88 MCS and UWS respectively, both with p < 0.001; Fig 3D). 131

# Discussion 234 

In this study, we examined how large-scale structural connectivity constrains the 235 

sensitivity of whole-brain dynamics to perturbations, focusing on transitions between 236 

dFC regimes characteristic of CNT and DoC conditions. First, we quantified dynamical 237 

richness using measures derived from transitions in functional connectivity space. Using 238 

perturbations on a whole-brain model, we found that applying the Hierarchical Modular 239 

Decomposition integration component to the SC matrix revealed nodes that are central 240 

to large-scale brain connectivity, as regions with highest integration component best 241 

explain transitions from CNT-like to DoC-like dynamics when removed from the 242 

network, and transitions from DoC-like to CNT-like dynamics when perturbed toward 243 

higher excitability. 244 

Highly integrated nodes overlap with regions previously identified as belonging to 245 

the rich club, defined as areas whose connectivity profiles make them particularly 246 

important for global information integration [26]. While the precise anatomical identity 247 

of such hubs can depend on methodological choices such as parcellation and binarization 248 

thresholds [26], our results consistently identify the Precuneus and Posterior Cingulate 249 

cortex (core components of the default mode network DMN [7]) as the regions with 250 

both the highest integration and the largest impact on network dynamics. The 251 

relevance of the Precuneus for consciousness-related processes is well established [27], 252 

and its activity is consistently reduced in DoC patients [28], while its connectivity 253 

profile has been shown to predict clinical outcomes following coma [12]. 254 

A methodological advantage of the integration measure used here is that it does not 255 

depend on arbitrary thresholding of the SC matrix. Although the Hierarchical Modular 256 

Decomposition framework was originally developed for functional connectivity [20], 257 

being defined over a spectral decomposition basis makes it applicable to general 258 

matrices, including SC. Although choosing a different parcellation may partially alter 259 

the anatomical identity of which nodes are highly integrated (preserving the 260

SC sets the conditions under which FC emerges [29]. From a dynamical systems 265

structural characteristics best predict the controllability of a network toward a desired 268

neuroscience, this perspective is particularly relevant to understand how targeted 270 

interventions can modulate pathological brain dynamics. Non-invasive stimulation 271 

techniques such as transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) have shown measurable 272 

improvements in clinical scales on DoC patients when applied to regions such as the 273 

dorsolateral prefrontal cortex [30] or posterior parietal areas [31] [32]. Our results 274 

provide a structural rationale for why perturbing integrative hubs may be particularly 275 

effective in reshaping global brain dynamics. 276 

Consistent with this interpretation, increasing local excitation–inhibition balance in 277 

specific cortical regions in our model is sufficient to restore healthy-like dynamics from a 278 

DoC regime. This finding aligns with the view that DoC corresponds to a network 279 

operating in a sub-excitable state [33] [28], in which activity fails to propagate and 280 

integrate across cortical hubs [34]. 281 

Given that our integration metric reflects cortico–cortical spectral dominance, it is 282 

not surprising that the thalamus did not emerge as a highly influential node in our 283 

simulations, despite prior work identifying the thalamus as crucial for sustaining arousal, 284 

using a similar pipeline for estimating dFC [24]. We attribute this discrepancy to 285 

simplifying assumptions in our model, which does not capture key cytoarchitectural 286 

differences between thalamic and cortical regions, subreticular nuclei, subcortical and 287 

sensory inputs, or the full complexity of corticothalamic pathways that underlie thalamic 288 

function beyond what can be inferred from DTI-derived connectivity alone [35]. 289

with higher SC FC similarity, yet lower global coupling values are required to fit DoC 291 

dynamics than CNT dynamics. Similar findings have been reported when fitting 292

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The specific parcellation used may 297 

influence the anatomical identity of highly integrated or high-impact regions, although 298 

the relationship between nodal integration and dynamical impact is expected to depend 299 

primarily on global network structure rather than precise spatial subdivision. In 300 

addition, the whole-brain model relies on simplifying assumptions, including 301 

predominantly excitatory interareal coupling and a fixed hemodynamic response model, 302 

which are expected to affect quantitative aspects of the dynamics rather than the 303 

qualitative relationships reported here. Stimulation was implemented as a simplified 304 

modulation of local E/I balance, providing a controlled and interpretable perturbation 305 

but not capturing the full biophysical complexity of neuromodulatory interventions. A 306 

further limitation is that we relied on a normative structural connectome, which may 307 

not accurately reflect individual or pathology-specific connectivity in DoC patients. 308 

Accordingly, our results are not intended as patient-specific predictions, but rather as a 309 

characterization of general relationships between structural integration and dynamical 310 

sensitivity that can inform future studies using individualized connectomes. 311 

Taken together, our results show that generic features of large-scale structural 312 

connectivity, as measured by diffusion MRI, constrain the stability of whole-brain 313 

dynamical states and their transitions between brain-wide regimes, providing a 314 

principled framework for understanding how structural integration shapes sensitivity to 315 

perturbation in health and disorders of consciousness. 316

## Dynamic FC calculation 350 

We applied phase-based estimation of time-resolved Functional Connectivity. For each 351 

registering session, we use the Hilbert Transform to get the analytical expression of our 352 

signals in the complex plane, whose angle we extract to obtain the instantaneous phase 353 

of all areas’ BOLD signals {ϕi(t) ∈ [−π, π ], i = 1 , 2, ..., 90 }, and measure the phase 354 

coherence matrix at time t between areas i and j as 355 

dF C (t)ij = cos( ϕi(t) − ϕj (t)) which is equal to 1 when the two areas are in the same phase, zero when they are 356 

orthogonal (difference of π/ 2), and -1 if they are in antiphase (difference of π). Given 357 

that cos is a pair function, these matrices are symmetric, so we take the lower triangular 358 

entries (4005 in total) to perform our analyses. 359 

## Clustering algorithm on dFC matrices 360 

A K-means algorithm was applied on the concatenated lower triangulars of all 361 

individuals, using the scikit-learn native implementation of the algorithm. The number 362 

of clusters (K=4) was chosen such that the variability between the inter-cluster 363 

correlations is maximized, subject that the occupancy of each cluster was greater than 364 

zero by all three states. 365 

## Jump length calculation 366 

Given the flattened lower triangular part of the dFC stream matrices, we calculated the 367 

jump length at time t as the euclidean distance between matrices corresponding to 368 

consecutive time points JL (ti) = euc( dF C (ti) − dF C (ti−1)). 369

## Integration and Segregation using Hierarchical Modular Analysis 420 

To identify functional modules in the SC matrix, and quantify their integration and 421 

segregation components, we used the Hierarchical Modular Analysis (HMA) 422 

method [19] [20]. The method applies an SVD decomposition of the SC matrix to find 423 

its eigenvectors and eigenvalues (sorted by eigenvalue magnitude, from largest to 424 

smallest). The regions whose corresponding entries in a given eigenvector have the same 425 

signs are assumed to have joint activity (cooperation) and put in the same module, one 426 

for positive and one for negative values. Given that all entries of the SC matrix are 427 

positive, the first functional level (largest eigenvalue) consists of a sole one module that 428 

encompasses all brain areas, the second level can divide the brain in two modules 429 

according to the signs of the entries of the second eigenvector, the third can divide each 430 

of these in two more modules, and so on. During this partition process, we save the 431 

number of modules in each level Mi, and the size of each module mj . 432 

The single large module on the first level with the largest eigenvalue Λ 1 corresponds 433 

to global network integration. The second level defines two modules that support local 434 

integration within each one and segregation between them, which are weighted by a 435 

lower eigenvalue. In subsequent levels, more modules reflect deeper levels of segregation, 436 

accompanied by smaller eigenvalues Λ. The weighted module number in each level can 437
