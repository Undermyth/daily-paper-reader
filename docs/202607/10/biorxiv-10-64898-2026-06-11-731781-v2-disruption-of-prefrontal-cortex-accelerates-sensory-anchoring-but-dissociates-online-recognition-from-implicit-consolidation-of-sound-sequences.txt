Abstract 27 

The auditory brain excels at extracting predictive regularities from sound, but the neural and 28 

temporal mechanisms mediating long -term learning of these regularities remain poorly 29 

understood. Here, using a perceptual anchoring paradigm, we investigate whether the 30 

dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC ) is causally required to transform transient regularities 31 

into stable internal templates (anchors). We characterized the behavioural dynamics of 32 

anchoring to predicable acoustic events using sensitivity ( d' ) and decision bias ( c) metrics, 33 

alongside growth curve analysis (GCA) to model trial -by -trial learning. Listeners exhibited a 34 

robust anchoring effect, showing significantly higher sensitivity in decision strategy for 35 

recurring acoustic regularities compared to novel ones. This implicit anchoring was driven 36 

selectively by specific features in regular sequences —the local repetition of an acoustic 37 

motif —whereas explicit memory indiscriminately encoded the entire acoustic episode. To test 38 

for causality, we applied inhibitory continuous theta -burst stimulation ( cTBS) to the dlPFC. 39 

Crucially, not only did this not impair anchoring, but significantly accelerated the rate of 40 

anchoring for predictable patterns, suggesting the dlPFC may act as a source of top -down 41 

interference in implicit learning. In contrast to predictable sequences, inhibition of dlPFC 42 

disrupted the initial precision of online comparisons for novel sequences, though insulating 43 

against the rapid, interference -driven reduction in performance or novel sequences in observed 44 

in listeners subjected to a sham cTBS procedure. These findings reveal a functional 45 

dissociation. Whilst dlPFC provides an essential buffer for novel sensory comparisons, long -46 

term consolidation of regularities in acoustic sequences relies on a robust implicit pathway 47 

independent of prefrontal control. 48

Introduction 50 

Learning is critical to survival. We are constantly exposed to multiple streams of sensory 51 

information and must learn and act on those most relevant in a wide range of environments, 52 

situations, and contexts. Given metabolic and other constraints, it is likely that commonly and 53 

rarely occurring events are provided different perceptual weights, a phenomenon reflecting the 54 

ability of the nervous system to adapt to sensory information over multiple timescales. One 55 

means by which this is achieved is through statistical learning, an unconscious, automatic 56 

process by which regular sensory patterns are identified by virtue of the predictable nature of 57 

their appearance over time (Chun & Jiang, 1998; Frost et al., 2015; Kirkham et al., 2002; 58 

Saffran et al., 1996; Turk -Browne et al., 2009) . Statistical learning (SL) appears to be a 59 

fundamental, domain -general principle of sensory organisation, enabling efficient processing 60 

across sensory modalities; vision (Fiser & Aslin, 2002; Turk -Browne et al., 2010) , tactile 61 

sensation (Conway & Christiansen, 2005) , and olfaction (Marcout et al., 2025; Tong et al., 62 

2014) , and in the sense of hearing (Agus et al., 2010; Saffran et al., 1996, 1999) where it is 63 

supports language acquisition (Aslin et al., 1998) , the representation of background sounds 64 

‘textures’ in terms of their summary statistics (Bastug et al., 2026; McDermott & Simoncelli, 65 

2011; McWalter & McDermott, 2018) , and in the parsing of complex room acoustics to better 66 

understand speech in background noise (Brandewie & Zahorik, 2010, 2013; Hernández -Pérez 67 

et al., 2025) .68 

Whilst dedicated neural pathways to the level of auditory cortex are essential for processing 69 

the dynamic spectro -temporal features of sound (Banai et al., 2009; Bizley & Cohen, 2013; 70 

Souffi et al., 2021) , higher -order statistical inference likely requires a distributed network 71 

involving corticofugal efferent pathways (Bajo et al., 2019; Souffi et al., 2021; Wang et al., 72 

2023) as well as bidirectional communication with prefrontal brain regions that support the 73 

formation and maintenance of working memory, cognitive control, and multi -sensory 74 

integration (Ahveninen et al., 2023; Ambrus et al., 2020; Bonetti et al., 2024; Demarchi et al., 75 

2019; Jang & Choi, 2022; Kumar et al., 2016; Plakke & Romanski, 2014; Rolls et al., 2023; 76 

Schiavo & Froemke, 2019; Winer & Lee, 2007) . Nevertheless, despite clear anatomical 77 

evidence of bidirectional innervation of auditory and prefrontal cortical areas (Bidelman & 78 

Myers, 2020; Hockley & Malmierca, 2024; Petrides & Pandya, 2002; Zikopoulos & Barbas, 79 

2006) such as dorsal -lateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC), any causal role for these pathways in 80 

encoding long -term auditory regularities has not been established. Recently, we explored how 81 

the process of rapid learning of room acoustics improves understanding of speech in noise 82

of the abstract feature being learned —the reverberant characteristics of rooms —but were 86

was characterised from the room impulse response (RIR), specifically the RT 60 , the time it 88

which a 'bait and switch' paradigm —speech understanding, rather than discrimination of the 93 

abstract statistical features being implicitly learned —demonstrates real -world benefit of 94 

implicitly learning a background feature defined by its statistics (reverberation time). 95 

Here, we explore the contribution of dlPFC to statistical learning of abstract acoustic patterns 96 

in the framework of perceptual anchoring (Ahissar, 2007; Ahissar et al., 2006) . Perceptual 97 

anchoring models learning as a hierarchical process in which short -term brain mechanisms 98 

track the statistics of unfolding sensory input (i.e., in the order of tens of milliseconds) to 99 

facilitate real -time identification, with longer -term mechanisms integrating this statistical 100 

information to generate a stable internal representation (perceptual anchor). In the context of 101 

listening, anchoring provides for more efficient recognition of sounds at a later time (Ahissar, 102 

2007; Ahissar et al., 2006; Daikhin et al., 2017; Jaffe -Dax et al., 2018; Lieder et al., 2019) . To 103 

do so, we employed a sound paradigm designed to minimise listeners' abilities to encode 104 

explicitly regular features in an unfolding acoustic stimulus. The stimulus consists of rapid 105 

sequences of 50 -ms pure tones —drawn from a limited pool of 20 frequencies —that transition 106 

from random (RAND) to regular (REG) segments at a fixed time point (Barascud et al., 2016) .107 

Whilst each RAND segment samples this pool uniformly, REG segments do so 108 

deterministically, in that they comprise a concatenated, local repeat (see Fig 1A). Transitions 109 

from RAND to REG segments provide a means of indexing the brain's ability to process 110 

structural order at multiple temporal scales. 111 

Confirming that detection of transitions improves with familiarity, i.e., improved reaction times 112 

with increased exposure to the transition from RAND to REG ( Barascud et al., 2016; Bianco 113 

et al., 2020) , we found that REG, but not RAND, segments drove a sensory anchoring process. 114 

Listeners were increasingly able to discriminate whether repeated RAND+REG sequences in 115

Finally, hypothesising that integrating tone sequences into an anchoring framework is mediated 127 

by prefrontal cortical circuits, we applied continuous theta -burst stimulation (cTBS) —a rapid 128 

form of transcranial magnetic stimulation —to inhibit activity in dlPFC and determine any 129 

causal contribution of this brain region to perceptual anchoring. We found that disrupting 130 

dlPFC with cTBS speeds up the rate at which listeners accumulate knowledge of predictable 131 

auditory sequences to generate perceptual anchors but renders them less proficient at 132 

processing unpredictable sequences. Together, our data suggest that committing acoustic 133 

features to memory (anchoring) requires some form of regularity in their structure —local 134 

repetitions (REG segments) in acoustic structure that recur repeatedly —and that prefrontal 135 

cortical circuits modulate the rate and extent to which predictable and unpredictable sound 136 

sequences are committed to implicit memory. 137 

Methods 138 

Equipment 139 

Perceptual anchoring tasks were programmed in MATLAB (ver. R2018b) using the 140 

Psychophysics Toolbox (Brainard, 1997; Kleiner et al., 2007; Pelli, 1997) and completed on a 141 

15’ Dell laptop. Participants were seated comfortably in front of the laptop and headphones 142 

were inserted by the researcher. Sounds were presented over insert headphones (ER -2 insert 143 

phones, Etymotic) to both ears simultaneously. Stimuli were played through an RME Fireface 144 

UCX digital -to -analog converted with 16 -bit resolution at a 44.1 kHz sample rate. All 145 

experiments were completed in an acoustically treated booth. The task was completed via key 146 

presses on the laptop keyboard. 147

Acoustic Stimuli 148 

Pure tone sequences were created as described by Barascud et al. (2016) and were generated 149 

using MATLAB (Mathworks ver. R2018b). Sequences were generated as segments of 50 ms 150 

tone -pips with frequencies drawn from a pool of 20 values equally spaced on a logarithmic 151 

scale between 222 and 2000 Hz. Each sequence consisted of two segments, a random (RAND) 152 

and regular (REG) segment. RAND segments consisted of 50 randomly generated tone -pips 153 

(2.5 s total duration). REG segments consisted of two recurring segments (20 tone pips each; 154 

40 in total; 2 s total duration). The two recurring segments were identical in structure and 155 

content. There was no intervening silence between tone -pips and segments, making the total 156 

duration of sequences 4.5 s each. The experiment consisted of two conditions, a random and 157 

repeating condition. The random condition consisted of two NonREP novel sequences that 158 

consisted of a novel RAND and REG segment in each sequence. This condition was 159 

nonidentical and non -recurring across the task. The repeating condition consisted of identical 160 

REP novel or REP fixed sequences. Both repeating conditions consisted of two sequences, however, 161 

REP fixed sequences repeated across trials such that participants would hear the same sequence 162 

pair multiple times. 163 

Continuous Theta -Burst Stimulation 164 

Participants were screened for contraindications to repetitive Transcranial Magnetic 165 

Stimulation (rTMS) prior to testing. Two continuous Theta Burst Stimulation (cTBS) 166 

conditions (“Active” and “Sham” TMS) were counterbalanced across participants, who were 167 

blinded to the type of manipulation they might receive. 168 

CTBS was administer using a Magstim Rapid2 system (Magstim, Whitland, United 169 

Kingdom) and a 70 -mm figure -of -eight coil oriented at 45 ° to the scalp with current flowing 170 

posterior -anterior across the primary motor cortex. cTBS intensity was individuals 171 

determined for each participant as a function of corticospinal excitability, assessed via single -172 

pulse TMS motor thresholds. For threshold determination, we used visual observation of first 173 

dorsal interosseus (FDI) muscle twitches (Varnava et al., 2011) . The coil position and angle 174 

were adjusted until the optimal site for consistent and robust FDI muscle twitch elicitation 175 

was identified. Participants were instructed to relax their arm on their lap, palm facing 176 

upwards, whilst gently squeezing their thumb and forefinger together. The minimum 177 

stimulator output producing a visible twitch in either the left or right hand was then 178 

determined, with a criterion of 3 out of 5 successful twitches in response to single -pulse 179

Procedure 195 

The experiment consisted of a 2 -alternative -forced -choice (2AFC) task and contained two 196 

phases: a training phase followed by a test phase. Training was completed to allow participants 197 

to familiarise themselves with the task and to be better able to distinguish NonREP and REP 198 

conditions. For each trial within a phase, participants were exposed to two stimuli separated by 199 

an interstimulus interval (ISI; 2.0 s). Participants were asked “were the sounds the same?” (i.e., 200 

were the two sequences identical; ‘Yes’ for they were the same, and ‘No’ for they were not the 201 

same). Responding with a key press (“f” for Yes, “j” for No) triggered the next trial until all 202 

trials were completed. Participants were provided a single break which was predetermined (i.e., 203 

occurred half -way through test phase) and self -directed (i.e., lasted as long as the participant 204 

desired). Training phases included only NonREP novel and REP novel conditions. The test phase 205 

followed an identical procedure to the training phase but included the additional REP fixed 206 

condition. Significantly, participants were not instructed that the REP condition contained a 207 

reoccurring stimulus pair (REP fixed ), only that they should report when they perceived the two 208 

pure -tone sequences as the same. 209

Results 248 

Successful sensory anchoring reflects a combined process of adaptation by a perceptual system 249 

to stimulus -relevant information and recruitment of attentional mechanisms to select, separate, 250 

and store that information for later access (Ahissar, 2007; Ahissar et al., 2006; Lieder et al., 251 

2019) . To assess the capacity of listeners to form perceptual anchors we employed sequences 252 

of pure tones comprised of distinct random (RAND) and regular (REG) segments. RAND 253 

segments were constructed from 50 randomly generated tone -pips (2.5 -s total duration), and 254 

REG segments from two consecutive and identical motifs of 20 tone -pips (40 in total; 2 -s total 255 

duration) (Figure 1A). No intervening silence separated RAND and REG segments in a 256 

sequence, or the motifs in the REG segment, providing for a total sequence duration of 4.5 s. 257 

Each trial consisted of two complete sequences, separated by an interstimulus interval (ISI) of 258 

2.0 s. In a REP fixed condition, pairs of sequences in a trial were identical (including RAND and 259 

REG segments) and appeared multiple times (i.e., were 'fixed') across the course of the 260 

experiment. In a REP novel condition, pairs of sequences in a trial were also identical (i.e., 261 

repeated) but appeared only once during the experiment ('novel'). In a third condition, 262 

NonREP novel , motifs within each REG segment of a sequence were identical, but both the 263 

RAND and the REG segments differed between the two sequences presented in a trial, and 264 

these sequences never reappeared during the experiment. 265 

Listeners anchor to repeated, fixed sequences of pure tones 266 

We first established whether listeners could utilise the recurrence of pure -tone sequences to 267 

improve their recognition of these sequences, even in the absence of explicit instruction to do 268 

so. In a two -alternative forced choice (2AFC) task, 16 normal -hearing (M age , = 33.1 SD = 269 

5.16) participants were exposed to an experimental block of 80 trials. For REP fixed and 270 

REP novel conditions the correct response was 'yes' (the sequences were the same), and for 271 

NonREP novel the correct answer was 'no' (the sequences were different). Prior to testing, all 272 

participants completed a brief training session to familiarise them with the task . Importantly, 273 

REP fixed stimuli were not presented to listeners during the training phase (data obtained 274 

during the training phase are included in the Supplemental Information). 275

Performance in the test phase of the task was first analysed in terms of global sensitivity ( d’ ;276 

d-prime) and decision bias ( c; criterion), focusing on the magnitude of the anchoring effect 277 

between REP fixed and REP novel conditions. Generalised linear mixed -effects modelling 278 

(GLMM) was used to quantify these fixed effects of condition whilst accounting for random 279 

effects associated with individual listeners (Bates et al., 2015; Kuznetsova et al., 2017). This 280 

approach allowed us to estimate the variability participants contributed to overall 281 

performance, with Cohen’s d values reported to specify effect sizes (Cohen, 1988, 2013) .282 

Because global false -alarm (FA) rates (derived from the NonREP novel condition) served as a 283 

shared baseline for REP fixed and REP novel conditions, variance in both sensitivity and decision 284 

bias was driven entirely by fluctuations in hit rates of those specific conditions .285 

Consequently, shifts in decision bias are mathematically proportional to shifts in sensitivity 286 

within our models. 287 

Our data confirmed a robust anchoring effect (Figure 1C): listeners were significantly better 288 

at detecting whether the two sequences comprising a trial were identical when these paired 289 

sequences appeared consistently across an experimental block (REP fixed ; d’ = 3.35) compared 290 

to when an identical pair of sequences appeared only once (REP novel ; d’ = 1.87). This 291 

improvement in performance was substantial ( p < .0 01), with a large effect size ( d = 1.02), 292 

confirming that listeners were able to exploit long -term regularities in sequences of pure 293 

tones to identify paired sequences as being identical (see Tables S1 –S3, Supplemental 294 

Information). 295 

Improvements in recognising repeated spectro -temporal features of pure -tone sequences with 296 

increasing exposure might signify a change in perceptual acuity but they can also represent a 297 

shift in decision strategy —the internal threshold, or criterion ( c), used to categorise a 298 

stimulus. We therefore analysed decision bias ( c) to determine how listeners’ willingness to 299 

report a repeated sequence as actually being repeated evolved as the anchor was formed 300 

(Figure 1D). For the REP novel condition —in which tone sequences were never experienced 301 

previously —listeners remained essentially unbiased ( c = 0.00), suggesting an equal 302 

weighting of decisions for each unique sequence. However, in the REP fixed condition, the 303 

formation of an auditory anchor fundamentally altered their approach to the task, with 304 

listeners adopting a significantly more liberal criterion ( c = -0.82, p < .001), indicating a shift 305 

in confidence where they became primed to say 'yes' to the learned pattern. 306 

To capture the evolution of performance in the task as a function of time (i.e., the rate of 307 

learning), we employed a logistic growth curve analysis (GCA) to model trial -by -trial error 308 

probabilities. We focused on error rates rather than aggregate d’ to isolate the temporal 309

Perceptual anchoring is driven by locally repeated motifs within fixed sequences 325 

Listeners appear rapidly to 'lock in' to auditory patterns that re -appear over time, rendering 326 

features within these patterns more easily discriminable. Specifically, spectro -temporal 327 

sequences are more likely to be judged as doing so if they appear over the course of an 328 

experiment —i.e., they become anchored. However, it remains unclear which segments, or 329 

features of those segments, within a sequence supports the anchoring process. Thus far, our 330 

sequences comprise two distinct segments: RAND, consisting of a non -recurring pattern of 331 

tones, and REG, containing a locally recurring pattern of tones —i.e. two concatenated 332 

instances of a 'motif' (Figure 1A). REG segments —which always comprise the second half of 333 

a sequence —are distinguishable from RAND segments by this locally repeating motif. 334 

Nevertheless, RAND segments in REP fixed sequences are identical within and across trials and 335 

could provide for perceptual anchoring if listeners were able to judge this to be so and, 336 

importantly, exploit this knowledge to form a perceptual anchor. To determine whether 337

Discussion 531 

We investigated the perceptual, cognitive, and neural architectures underlying the rapid 532 

perceptual anchoring to structured auditory sequences. We sought to determine if the auditory 533 

brain can extract complex regularities from sequences of acoustic patterns, the structural 534 

features contributing to this stabilisation, and whether activation of dorsolateral prefrontal 535 

cortex (dlPFC) is causally required for this process to occur. 536 

We confirmed that listeners rapidly extract and 'lock in' to specific structural features, storing 537 

information about these features for future retrieval. Listeners increasingly judged paired 538 

sequences of pure tones as being identical if these sequences appeared multiple times across 539

identical but appeared only once ('novel') over the experiment. Isolating the specific driver of 541 

this effect, we revealed a striking behavioural dissociation: anchoring is not driven by global 542 

sensitivity to the entire acoustic sequence but, rather, by the perceptual dominance of the 543 

locally (in time) repeated motif of tones in the REG segment of REP fixed sequences. Such was 544 

the influence of this perceptual cue that listeners frequently failed to notice trial -unique changes 545 

in the immediately preceding pattern (the RAND segment of the REP REG -fixed sequence). 546 

Though specificity for the REG segment, but not the RAND segment, appeared to be implicit, 547 

a conscious recognition task revealed that both the REG and the RAND segments were encoded 548 

in explicit memory. Together, these data demonstrate an efficient and implicit mechanism that 549 

prioritises specific stimulus features to facilitate real -time perception. Listeners anchor to 550 

locally repeated motifs of tone sequences (REG segments) when these specific motifs appear 551 

across the course of the experiment and do not anchor to RAND segments regardless of their 552 

repeated appearance (i.e., in the REP fixed and REP RAND -fixed conditions). 553 

A critical question, then, concerns the type of 'learning' this anchoring represents and why the 554 

locally repeated motif —the REG segment —in the REP fixed condition drives this anchoring. 555 

Does the formation of a stable auditory anchor reflect the slow, cortical consolidation 556 

characteristic of long -term statistical learning, or a process of short -term spectro -temporal 557

aggregate sensitivity ( d’ ) was comparable between groups, cTBS facilitated a more rapid 565 

transition to a near -zero floor in errors (FAs). This suggests that dlPFC may modulate implicit 566 

learning by inhibiting a prefrontal 'monitor', and the brain may more efficiently offload fixed 567 

auditory information to long -term memory pathways. 568

Perceptual mechanisms supporting auditory integration 579 

Recent models of complex sound perception suggest the auditory system flexibly adjusts its 580 

integration time to encode statistically coherent patterns (McDermott & Simoncelli, 2011; 581 

McWalter & McDermott, 2018; Teng et al., 2016) . Our data suggest that the REG segments in 582 

our sequences perform this role of a highly salient, recurring feature set. When a sequence is 583 

completely novel (as in the REP novel condition), the auditory system must sample, maintain, 584 

and compare these transient local features within working memory, a cognitive process to 585 

which dlPFC has been tied (Petrides, 2000; Schneegans et al., 2020) .586 

Nevertheless, our data are also explicable in terms of dlPFC providing the precision required 587 

for initial comparison of novel events, but that activity originating in this pathway competes 588 

with faster, more efficient implicit pathways during the acquisition of stable anchors. When 589 

the REG segment in our paradigm reliably repeats across trials, computational reliance on 590 

dlPFC is reduced, and the sequence is consolidated into memory via broader cortical and 591 

subcortical networks —likely involving secondary auditory areas, corticofugal efferent 592 

pathways, and thalamus —that successfully bypass prefrontal control (Antunes & Malmierca, 593 

2021; Bajo et al., 2019; Barascud et al., 2016) . This flexible, dlPFC -dependent weighting 594 

mechanism may be particularly critical in naturalistic environments with constantly changing 595 

sensory input. Without a reliable cross -trial anchor to guide implicit integration, the brain must 596 

continuously deploy prefrontal resources to hold and compare fleeting sensory evidence in real 597 

time. 598

pure -tone sequences potentially limits interpreting these findings as a direct reflection of long -601 

term statistical learning. In the REP fixed condition, sequence structure and statistical probability 602 

are perfectly correlated because the sequence is identical across trials, the conditional 603 

probability of the next tone is always 1.0. Importantly, though, this holds for both the RAND 604

Anchoring in our paradigm seems to require segments not simply to be fixed (multiple 610

mechanism of statistical learning —if such a separation exists —future anchoring paradigms 615

in which, for example, continuous exposure to white -noise sounds can lead to listeners 631

2015) .633
