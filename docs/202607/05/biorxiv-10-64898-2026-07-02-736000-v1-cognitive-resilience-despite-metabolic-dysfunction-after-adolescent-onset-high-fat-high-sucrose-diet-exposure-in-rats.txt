25 Adolescence is a sensitive period during which unhealthy diets may shape metabolic health 26 and cognition. Diets high in fat and sugar have been linked to obesity, impaired glucose 27 regulation and hippocampus-dependent deficits, but the exposure duration required to affect 28 cognition remains unclear. This study examined whether adolescent-onset exposure to a 29 high-fat high-sucrose (HFHS) diet induces metabolic dysfunction and impairs object-based 30 memory, spatial working memory and spatial pattern separation in male Long Evans rats. 31 Rats were assigned to a control or HFHS diet at four weeks of age and remained on this diet 32 into adulthood. Basal blood glucose was assessed monthly and home-cage behaviour using 33 48-hour LABORAS recordings. Cognitive testing started after 10 weeks of diet exposure, 34 when basal glucose was elevated in HFHS-fed rats. Object displacement and novel object 35 recognition were used in short open-field test settings, whereas touchscreen-based trial-36 unique nonmatching-to-location testing (TUNL) assessed spatial working memory and 37 pattern separation across repeated operant sessions. Finally, glucose (in)tolerance and 38 tissue weights were measured. 39 HFHS diet exposure produced a metabolic phenotype, including increased body weight, 40 elevated basal glucose, impaired glucose tolerance and increased liver and gonadal white 41 adipose tissue weights. The diet also altered the general behavioural repertoire, with 42 increased immobility and grooming and reduced rearing. HFHS-fed rats did not differ from 43 controls in object displacement or novel object recognition performance. In the touchscreen 44 task, both groups acquired the task at a comparable rate. Long-delay and spatial separation 45 challenges reduced performance as expected, confirming task sensitivity, but did not reveal 46 diet-related impairments. 47 These findings show that adolescent-onset HFHS diet exposure induces metabolic 48 dysfunction but does not necessarily produce detectable cognitive impairment when 49 behavioural testing starts after 10 weeks of exposure. Longer exposure or advanced diet-50 induced inflammatory or neurobiological alterations may be required to reveal cognitive 51 consequences.

These cognitive processes may be vulnerable 77 to diet-induced metabolic, inflammatory and neurobiological changes, although the 78 magnitude and timing of such effects vary across studies [4–6]. 79 Adolescence may represent a particularly sensitive developmental window for diet-induced 80 effects on cognition. During this period, brain regions involved in memory, reward processing 81 and behavioural regulation continue to mature, while dietary habits and metabolic trajectories 82 are also being shaped [6–8]. A systematic review of animal studies concluded that high-fat 83 and high-sugar diet exposure during adolescence often produces stronger or more persistent 84 memory impairments than similar exposure during adulthood [6]. Experimental studies have 85 reported that juvenile or adolescent exposure to high-fat or high-sugar diets can impair 86 hippocampus-dependent memory, alter neurogenesis or synaptic plasticity, and increase 87 inflammatory signalling in the hippocampus [9–13]. More recent work further suggests that 88 obesogenic diet exposure during adolescence can induce circuit-specific alterations in 89 object-based memory through ventral hippocampal projections [14]. 90 However, the relationship between obesogenic diet exposure and cognition is not uniform. 91 Some studies have reported clear impairments in spatial or object-location memory after 92 high-fat or high-sugar diet exposure, whereas others found metabolic, microbiome or 93 neurobiological changes without measurable learning or memory deficits [14–16]. 94 Differences in diet composition, sucrose content, exposure duration, age at exposure, 95 species, sex, strain and behavioural task may all influence whether cognitive effects are 96 detected. Thus, negative or null behavioural findings are informative when the dietary 97 manipulation is effective and when task sensitivity can be demonstrated. 98 Object displacement and novel object recognition are widely used short behavioural tests to 99 assess object-location and recognition memory in rodents [17,18]. 448 449 450 451 452 453 454 455 456 457 458 459 460 Discussion 

461 In this study, adolescent-onset exposure to a HFHS diet induced a clear metabolic 462 phenotype in male Long Evans rats, but did not lead to detectable impairments in the 463 cognitive tasks used here. Compared with control-fed rats, HFHS-fed rats gained more body 464 weight, developed higher basal glucose levels, showed impaired glucose tolerance and had 465 increased liver and gonadal white adipose tissue weights. The diet also altered aspects of 466 the general behavioural repertoire, with increased immobility and grooming and reduced 467 rearing. However, these metabolic and behavioural changes were not accompanied by 468 impairments in object displacement, novel object recognition, TUNL acquisition, delayed 469 spatial working memory or spatial pattern separation. Importantly, the TUNL challenge 470 sessions reduced performance as expected, indicating that the task manipulations increased 471 cognitive demand.

472 Absence of detectable cognitive impairment despite metabolic dysfunction 473 The central finding of this study is that metabolic dysfunction did not translate into detectable 474 cognitive impairment under the present testing conditions. This contrasts with several studies 475 showing that high-fat or high-sugar diet exposure can impair hippocampus-dependent 476 memory, particularly when exposure occurs during adolescence [6,9–14]. In particular, 477 juvenile or adolescent high-fat diet exposure has been associated with impaired relational 478 memory, object-location memory, reduced hippocampal neurogenesis, altered synaptic 479 plasticity and hippocampal inflammation [9–12]. More recent work has shown that adolescent 480 obesogenic diet exposure can induce circuit-specific object-based memory deficits through 481 ventral hippocampal pathways [14]. 482 At the same time, the present results are consistent with studies showing that diet-induced 483 metabolic or biological changes do not always coincide with measurable impairments in 484 learning or memory. Deshpande et al. reported that high-fat diet exposure altered gut 485 microbiota, but did not affect spatial working memory or microgliosis in early middle-aged 486 Sprague Dawley rats [15]. Similarly, Leyh et al. found that long-term diet-induced obesity in 487 mice did not necessarily lead to learning or spatial memory deficits, despite general health 488 effects and hypothalamic microglial changes [16]. In our previous work in Zucker Diabetic 489 Fatty rats, early diabetic and inflammatory features were also not accompanied by cognitive 490 decline during adolescence [32]. Together, these findings support the view that the 491 relationship between obesogenic diet exposure and cognition depends on diet composition, 492 exposure duration, age at exposure, species or strain, sex, task demands and the 493 neurobiological state at the time of testing. 494 Timing of behavioural testing and possible delayed neurobiological consequences 495 One possible explanation for the absence of detectable cognitive impairment is the timing of 496 behavioural testing relative to the development of diet-induced neurobiological alterations. In 497 the present study, cognitive testing started after 10 weeks of diet exposure, when basal

It remains possible that stronger cognitive 511 effects would have emerged if all cognitive testing had started only after more prolonged diet 512 exposure, or if behavioural testing had been combined with direct measures of hippocampal 513 inflammation, synaptic plasticity, insulin signalling or neurogenesis. Studies reporting diet-514 induced cognitive impairments often implicate hippocampal plasticity, glucocorticoid 515 signalling, neuroinflammation or blood-brain barrier changes as potential mechanisms 516 [10,12,35]. 517 Interpretation of the TUNL findings 518 The TUNL task was included to assess spatial working memory and pattern separation 519 across repeated operant sessions.
