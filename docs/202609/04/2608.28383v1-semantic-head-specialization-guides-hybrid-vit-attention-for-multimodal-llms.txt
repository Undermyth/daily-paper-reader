Abstract 

Hybrid attention dominates frontier LLMs, yet Vision Transformers (ViTs) in multimodal LLMs lack a satisfactory hybrid design, with no consensus on why certain attention patterns work better. To fill this gap, we study ViT at-tention heads and find they differentiate into object- and background-specialist roles, a pat-tern most pronounced under full attention; we call this Semantic Head Specialization (SHS). We propose SHS-Index to quantify this special-ization, show that it distinguishes full-attention from chunk-window ViTs, and find that it cor-relates with downstream benchmark perfor-mance within our controlled design space. We then identify three structural factors that shape SHS—window interaction, token serialization, and local softmax allocation—and use them as design principles for hybrid attention. Guided by these factors, we design Ariadne Attention, a hybrid within about 0.5 points of full atten-tion on the 20-image benchmark at 6.5× lower attention FLOPs; its end-to-end ViT time is 13.5% lower at 896 2. Our findings establish head specialization as a measurable property for diagnosing and designing principled hybrid ViT attention at the multimodal-LLM scale.

Hybrid atten-tion is a natural candidate: frontier LLMs such as GPT-OSS (OpenAI, 2025) and Gemma 3 (Gemma Team, 2025) reduce cost by interleaving local and full-attention layers. In ViTs, however, ex-isting hybrids have not yet matched the quality of full attention. 2 Semantic Head Specialization in ViT Attention 

To compare how full-attention and chunk-window ViTs differ in attention behavior and downstream quality, we first study their per-head attention pat-terns under a controlled full-vs.-chunk comparison (§2.1). We quantify this phenomenon with SHS-Index (§2.2) and analyze whether the specializa-tion gap is an architecture-level property rather than merely a side effect of LLM scale.

SHS is also not a single-image artifact. The same head maintains a consistent foreground-or-background preference across all analysis images (per-image heatmaps in Appendix D; iteration-aggregated object dominance in Appendix E), indi-cating that SHS is a stable property of the trained model rather than a stimulus-dependent visualiza-tion effect. The pattern further generalizes across diverse visual scenes—including single objects, multiple objects, complex backgrounds, and scenes with overlapping or cluttered foreground entities— and consistently maintains object/background head specialization (Appendix D). 

2.2 Quantifying Head Specialization 

The heatmaps above suggest that a specialized head should consistently prefer one semantic side of the image: either object tokens or back-ground tokens. We therefore turn SHS into a foreground–background separation problem. For this quantitative analysis, we use COCO segmen-tation masks to assign each visual token to fore-ground or background according to the dominant region in its image patch (details in Appendix C). Given the received-attention score rh(j) (Eq. 2), a foreground-specialist head should give larger scores to foreground tokens than to background tokens, while a background-specialist head should consistently do the opposite. For each head h and image, we compute the AUROC of received attention rh(j) for ranking foreground tokens above background tokens: AUROC h = Pr( rh(j+) > r h(j−)) + 12 Pr( rh(j+) = rh(j−)) , (3) where j+ and j− denote foreground and back-ground tokens. AUROC values near 1 or 0 indicate foreground- or background-oriented heads, while values near 0.5 indicate no separation. Since ei-ther direction (FG/BG) indicates specialization, we define the head-level score as SHS h = max( AUROC h, 1 − AUROC h). (4) which ranges from 0.5 (no separation) to 1 (strong specialization). The final SHS-Index averages this score over images, layers, and heads: SHS-Index = Eimg ,ℓ,h [SHS h]. (5) On our controlled full-vs.-chunk pair, SHS-Index is .606 for Full Attention and .577 for Chunk Window, quantitatively matching the specialization gap observed in the heatmaps. 

SHS-Index separates attention families in open-source models. The controlled pair above shows that SHS-Index captures the specialization gap between full and chunk-window attention under matched training conditions. We next ask whether the same signal appears in existing VLMs rather than only in our controlled models. Across 16 open-source visual encoders and VLMs, SHS-Index cleanly separates the 11 full-attention en-coders (mean SHS ≈ 0.631) from the 5 chunk-window ViTs (mean SHS = 0.585), with no ob-served overlap (Figure 1; full data in Appendix E). Moreover, the identical Qwen2.5-VL ViT changes by only < 0.002 when paired with 3B–72B LLM backbones, showing that SHS-Index is determined by the ViT attention design rather than by the language-model scale. Thus, the open-source anal-ysis supports SHS as an architecture-level diagnos-tic. We do not use these heterogeneous models for benchmark correlation because their data, training recipes, and language backbones differ. 

3 Structural Sources of the SHS Gap 

The evidence above points to a clear SHS gap: full attention specializes heads much more strongly than chunk-window attention. We now ask which structural choices create this gap, with the goal of turning them into design principles for a better hybrid attention. We study three factors in turn— window interaction, token serialization, and lo-cal softmax allocation—using controlled attention-only comparisons. As in §2.1, all comparisons keep the 32-layer ViT, data, training recipe, and full-attention anchor layers fixed; only the atten-tion structure in the remaining 28 layers changes. 

Factor 1: Window isolation restricts spatial scope. Chunk-window attention differs from full attention most directly in spatial reach. In full attention, every token can collect evidence from the whole image. In chunk-window attention, im-age tokens are partitioned into non-overlapping chunks, so a local layer can only mix information inside each chunk. If SHS requires heads to com-pare object and background evidence across re-gions, then the first question is whether relaxing this chunk-level isolation improves SHS. To an-swer this question, we keep Qwen2.5-VL’s orig-inal 2D block layout, where image patches are grouped into local 2×2 blocks and then ordered as chunk windows (Figure 3b), and change only the attention operator from chunk-window attention to sliding-window attention (SWA) (Jiang et al., 2023). Here, SWA denotes a token-wise local at-tention pattern: with window (l, r ), query i attends to NSWA (i) = I(max(1 , i −l), min( N, i +r)) , i.e., a window of preceding and following tokens rather than a fixed chunk. Unlike fixed chunks, this win-dow overlaps across chunk boundaries, so tokens are no longer confined to their original chunk; across layers, information can propagate through successive windows to much farther spatial regions. Under this controlled change, SHS-Index increases from .577 (chunk-2D) to .588 (SWA-2D). Attention distance provides a direct physical check: using the attention distance of Dosovitskiy et al. 4 SHS-Guided Design of Ariadne Attention 

The previous section identifies structural factors that shape SHS and shows that SHS-Index corre-lates with downstream benchmark performance in the controlled comparison. We now use these find-ings to design a hybrid attention pattern. Guided by the validated components—sliding-window at-tention, 1D serialization, and sink bias—we further increase token interaction across image dimensions by alternating row- and column-major serialization. The resulting hybrid, Ariadne Attention , is a vali-dation of the SHS framework rather than a claim of a new primitive attention operator. 

4.1 Ariadne Attention 

We now instantiate the design suggested by the SHS analysis. Ariadne starts from the three com-ponents that improved or stabilized SHS in the controlled study: sliding-window attention (SWA) to relax chunk isolation, spatially coherent 1D se-rialization to make local windows meaningful on images, and a per-head sink bias to absorb sur-plus softmax mass. SWA replaces non-overlapping chunk windows with overlapping token-wise neigh-borhoods, so adjacent windows can exchange in-formation without returning to full attention. 4.2 Main Results

Ariadne scores 40.40 on the 20-task image benchmark versus 40.92 for full attention, a 0.52-point gap, and reaches 24.3 on the two video tasks (Table 1; Figure 8). Relative to chunk-window attention, Ariadne improves the corrected 20-image average by +3 .05 , showing that the hybrid recovers most of full attention’s aggregate image quality while improving over the chunk-window baseline. The largest gains occur on tasks where chunk-window isolation is most limiting: tasks that re-quire finding evidence across distant regions or combining fine local details with broader context. On perception benchmarks, Ariadne improves over chunk-window attention by +6 .1 on CV-Bench and +7 .2 on VLMs-are-Blind. 4.3 Efficiency 

Finally, we test actual kernel timings, not just asymptotic attention complexity. Sink bias adds zero computational overhead: FlashAttention-3 im-plements s_aux as a single scalar addition to the softmax denominator (Shah et al., 2024). 5 Related Work 

Attention head behavior. Prior work identifies specialized attention heads for syntactic and po-sitional roles in NLP (Voita et al., 2019; Michel et al., 2019). In vision, self-supervised ViTs develop object-segmenting CLS-attention maps (Caron et al., 2021), while CLIP and generative multimodal transformers show semantically in-terpretable head roles (Gandelsman et al., 2023; Basile et al., 2025). We extend this line to su-pervised multimodal ViTs with SHS-Index, a con-tinuous, direction-agnostic foreground/background measure. Our result is a cross-architecture correla-tion at a fixed, sufficiently trained checkpoint, not a claim that specialization monotonically predicts every task. 

Hybrid attention in Vision Transformers. Effi-cient ViTs span shifted windows (Liu et al., 2021), axial/stripe designs (Ho et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2019; Dong et al., 2021), and local/hybrid backbones such as Twins, Focal, NAT, and MaxViT (Chu et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2021; Hassani et al., 2022; Tu et al., 2022). Vision state-space models further show that 2D-to-1D scan order matters (Liu et al., 2024b; Zhu et al., 2024). InternVL 2.5 and Qwen3-VL use full atten-tion, whereas Qwen2.5-VL uses chunk windows (Chen et al., 2024; Bai et al., 2025a,b). We study this trade-off inside a controlled multimodal-LLM pipeline. Ariadne is functionally related to ax-ial/stripe attention, but uses overlapping windows and a block-wise row/column schedule for fused-kernel efficiency; the central contribution is SHS, not a new primitive. Its sink bias also relates to ViT registers and LLM attention sinks (Darcet et al., 2024; Xiao et al., 2024). 

6 Conclusion 

This paper studies why hybrid ViT attention lags behind full attention in multimodal LLMs. We discover Semantic Head Specialization (SHS): full-attention heads separate into object- and background-specialist roles, while chunk-window heads show weaker separation. SHS-Index quanti-fies this behavior, separates full and chunk families across 16 open-source visual encoders and VLMs, and exposes three structural factors: window in-teraction, token serialization, and local softmax allocation. At 8k, it correlates with the corrected 22-task average ( r = .858 ), with an intermediate rather than monotonic optimum. Guided by these factors, Ariadne reaches 40.40 versus 40.92 for full attention on 20 image tasks, at 6.5× lower attention FLOPs and 13.5% lower end-to-end ViT time at 

896 2. SHS-Index is therefore a compact diagnostic for comparing and designing hybrid ViT attention within this scope. 

Limitations 

Scale of the controlled study. All nine archi-tectures share the same training configuration (random-init ViT, Qwen2-0.5B, 10k steps), use one random seed, and are evaluated at a single primary checkpoint. The corrected MMBench-EN evaluation is available for the 8k primary check-point; the 10k sensitivity rows retain the original evaluation and are labeled accordingly. The con-trolled design isolates attention operators, but the quantitative correlation has not been tested with a larger language backbone, multiple seeds, or from-scratch comparisons to every axial/stripe baseline. The open-source analysis now spans roughly five independent full-attention ViT lineages and sup-ports the structural separation at scale, but it does not establish benchmark correlation across those heterogeneous models.

A.1 ViT Architecture 

All nine models use a 32-layer ViT encoder fol-lowing the Qwen2.5-VL (Bai et al., 2025b) design. Of the 32 layers, 4 (at 0-indexed positions 7, 15, 23, 31) use full self-attention; the remaining 28 use the local attention operator under study. The ViT extracts non-overlapping 14 × 14 patches and groups every 2 × 2 spatially adjacent patches into an atomic block of 4 sub-tokens, the basic unit of both chunk-window grouping and 1D serialization. A.3 Attention Design Space Chunk window attention. Blocks are grouped into 4 × 4-block windows ( W =64 tokens per win-dow), similar to the shifted-window mechanism of Swin Transformer (Liu et al., 2021). Self-attention is computed independently within each window. Tokens in different windows can only exchange information through the 4 full-attention layers. 

Sliding window attention (SWA). Each token attends to a window of (wL, w R) tokens to its left and right in the 1D sequence, as popularized by Mistral (Jiang et al., 2023). We use wL=wR=64 

(128 tokens total context). Unlike chunk windows, SWA windows overlap, enabling cross-window in-formation flow. Implemented via FlashAttention-3 (Shah et al., 2024) varlen interface. 

Sink bias. A single learnable scalar s(h) 

> aux

per head is added to the softmax denominator, acting as a vir-tual sink token that absorbs surplus attention mass (Xiao et al., 2024; Darcet et al., 2024). Natively supported by FlashAttention-3’s s_aux parameter at zero additional cost. 

Token serialization. For 1D-layout models, blocks are serialized into a flat sequence. Row-major: left-to-right, top-to-bottom. Column-major: top-to-bottom, left-to-right. The Ariadne configu-ration alternates 4 row-major and 3 column-major layers within each block of 7 local layers (4-row-3-col pattern). See Figure 3 for an illustration. F.3 2D Incompatibility Hypothesis 

Under 2D block layout, the sliding window’s left and right neighbors in 1D sequence map to spa-tially disjoint regions of the image at every window boundary. This forces each head to attend to a mix-ture of spatially near and spatially distant tokens within the same window, diluting the spatial coher-ence that enables meaningful object/background discrimination. Evidence (Table 18): the 2D →1D transition yields the largest single-step benchmark improve-ment (+1.94 without sink, +1.22 with sink), yet Entropy and Distance are essentially unchanged (∆Entropy = +0 .10 , ∆Distance = −0.09 ). Only SHS increases (+0.013), suggesting that the im-provement comes from spatial coherence enabling more meaningful head specialization, not from changes in attention sharpness or receptive field size.

A learnable sink bias s(h)aux is a fixed, input-independent scalar; it competes with the pre-existing input-dependent mechanism rather than complementing it. 

SWA lacks this implicit mechanism. With only 128 tokens per window, SWA models cannot de-velop effective implicit sinks; the local context is too limited. The softmax constraint forces each query to distribute its full attention budget within the window, wasting mass on uninformative back-ground tokens. Here, an explicit sink bias fills a genuine gap: it provides the no-op channel that the architecture cannot learn on its own. 

Over-specialization reduces diversity. 

Full+Sink’s obj-dominant head fraction is 82.3% (the highest among all 9 models), meaning sink bias pushes too many heads toward fore-ground. This reduces the collective role diversity that §H identifies as indispensable, explaining why SHS-Index rises (heads are individually more specialized) while benchmark falls (the ensemble loses complementary background expertise). This analysis is consistent with Gu et al. (2025), who show that attention sinks arise from the soft-max normalization constraint and function as key biases rather than informative signals; adding a sec-ond explicit bias atop an already-effective implicit one yields diminishing or negative returns.
