## ABSTRACT 

Learning robust and scalable finite-state tracking is fundamental to sequence pro-cessing. While linear recurrent neural networks (RNNs), linear attention, and state space models enable scalable parallel training through affine recurrences, their theoretical expressivity guarantees assume idealized arithmetic and do not extend to finite precision, where the parallel scan that makes them fast is itself a source of perturbation. We formalize finite-state tracking at finite precision and characterize 

length independence : tracking that stays correct at every sequence length, at a pre-cision cost that does not grow with the length. We show that length-independent state tracking requires two competing dynamics within a single map: contraction 

to suppress numerical perturbations and separation to keep distinct states apart. We prove that affine recurrences, which offer a single rate at each step to serve both roles, realize at most definite automata at finite precision. Instead of treat-ing scan compatibility as a restriction on the update map, we reinterpret it as a computational budget and introduce the Neural Finite-State Machine (NFSM): a nonaffine, scan-compatible RNN built for length-independent finite-state tracking. On synthetic benchmarks spanning abelian and nonabelian groups, noninvertible monoids, and textual state-tracking tasks, affine baselines fail on every nondefi-nite task, most of them within a few hundred steps. A single NFSM layer instead learns the exact transition tables of every algebraic task, which certifies correct-ness beyond the tested lengths, and a stack of NFSMs keeps perfect accuracy on the textual tasks at every tested length. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

State tracking is the maintenance of an internal memory variable that is sequentially updated as inputs arrive, capturing the cumulative effect of the past inputs. It enables models to resolve pro-nouns across texts (Kim & Schuster, 2023), trace variable states during code execution (Zaremba & Sutskever, 2015), and maintain beliefs about partially observable environments (Kaelbling et al., 1998). Synthetic tasks such as flip-flop retrieval, permutation composition, and modular counting isolate this capability directly (Liu et al., 2023a;b; Delétang et al., 2023). Learning state tracking from data is a central challenge in sequence processing. Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) naturally possess the structure required for state tracking (Casey, 1996; Omlin & Giles, 1996a), but traditional training requires unrolling through time, leading to 

O(L) sequential depth for sequence length L. To address this bottleneck, recent architectures lever-age affine recurrences, which can be parallelized via scan algorithms to achieve O(log L) depth (Blelloch, 1990; Martin & Cundy, 2018). This approach, which underlies modern State Space Mod-els (SSMs) (Smith et al., 2023; Gu & Dao, 2024), gated linear RNNs (Martin & Cundy, 2018; Qin et al., 2023; Feng et al., 2024), and linear attention (Katharopoulos et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2024b), introduces a fundamental tradeoff between computational efficiency and the expressivity of the state dynamics. An extensive body of work has analyzed this tradeoff in exact or log-precision arithmetic (Merrill et al., 2024; Sarrof et al., 2024; Grazzi et al., 2025; Siems et al., 2025), yielding architectures that theoretically attain full finite-state tracking capabilities (Terzi´ c et al., 2025). 1arXiv:2609.32447v1 [cs.LG] 26 Sep 2026 Preprint. Under review. 2

However, these expressivity guarantees rely on idealized arithmetic and often do not hold for trained models. Practical models operate at finite precision, learn parameters that only approximate opti-mal target solutions, and are trained on finite context windows. In practice, state tracking succeeds up to the training horizon but collapses toward chance not far beyond it (Shakerinava et al., 2026; Karuvally et al., 2025; Grazzi et al., 2025; Terzi´ c et al., 2025). This collapse shows that theoretical expressivity is necessary but not sufficient: it does not survive the gap between idealized construc-tions and finite-precision, gradient-trained models. Most of this gap is a learning problem, but not all of it (Appendix E). We address the part that no amount of training removes, finite precision, and focus on finite-state tracking, where the state takes values in a finite set and evolves as in a finite automaton. We ask three questions: (i) What conditions must an RNN satisfy to achieve state tracking at finite precision over arbitrary sequence lengths? (ii) Can affine recurrences satisfy these conditions? (iii) If not, can we design a scan-compatible RNN that guarantees robust state tracking for arbitrarily long sequences at finite precision? We call a realization length-independent when it tracks the state correctly at every sequence length at a fixed precision. Unlike empirical extrapolation, length independence requires an RNN to execute exact target transitions at every step at a precision cost that does not grow with L. Correctness for any sequence length then follows by composition rather than empirical evaluation. We model finite-precision arithmetic as a perturbation of the hidden state bounded by η > 0 at every step. The bound η is set by the arithmetic resolution and the conditioning of the recurrence, and it includes the perturbation injected by the scan itself. Our analysis answers questions (i)–(iii) in turn. (i) Length-independent state tracking at finite precision reduces to three conditions on a single step, which together demand two competing dynamics within one map: contraction to sup-press numerical errors, and separation to maintain distinguishable states (Section 3). (ii) Affine recurrences offer a single rate, which over repeated steps cannot serve both roles; con-sequently, at any precision η > 0, they realize at most definite automata, whose state depends only on a bounded suffix of the input (Section 4). (iii) Scan compatibility is a budget on the representation of the recurrence update maps, not a restriction to affine maps (Section 5). Within this budget, the Neural Finite-State Machine 

(NFSM), a nonaffine recurrence, realizes every finite automaton with length independence and runs under a parallel scan (Section 6). To validate our theoretical results, we evaluate NFSM (Section 7) on synthetic state-tracking tasks spanning abelian and nonabelian groups, noninvertible monoids, and textual Tracking Shuffled Ob-jects tasks (Srivastava et al., 2023). Affine baselines fail beyond a finite length on every nondefinite task, whereas a single NFSM layer keeps perfect sequence accuracy at every tested length on the algebraic tasks, and a stack of two layers keeps perfect question accuracy on the textual tasks. On the algebraic tasks, the transition tables extracted from the trained models match the target exactly, which certifies correctness beyond the tested lengths instead of extrapolating it. Section 8 draws out the implications. Proofs and additional mathematical derivations are available in Appendix F, and Appendix C discusses related work. All source code is available at https:// julienbrandoit.github.io/length-independent-state-tracking/ , and read-ers are invited to follow along with our interactive illustration as they progress through the paper. 

## 2 FORMALIZATION 

This section formalizes state tracking and connects finite-state automata to RNNs. We describe the transition structure of each system, show how an RNN can realize the dynamics of an automaton, and account for finite-precision perturbations. 

State tracking and automata. We formalize state tracking using a semiautomaton (automaton for short), defined as a 3-tuple (Q, Σ, δ ), where Q is a set of states, Σ is an input alphabet, and 

δ : Q × Σ → Q is the transition function specifying how inputs change the state. In this work, we focus on state tracking with finite Q of size n. A single input x ∈ Σ, called a symbol, acts on the state space through δx := δ(·, x ) : Q → Q. We call a finite sequence of symbols a word, and denote the set of all words by Σ∗. For a word w = x1 · · · x|w| ∈ Σ∗, single-symbol transitions chain to form the map δw : Q → Q, defined by δw := δx|w| ◦ · · · ◦ δx1 , with δε := id Q for 2Preprint. Under review. 3

the empty word ε. Thus, δw(q) is the state reached from q after reading the word w. The set of all such maps, Mδ = {δw | w ∈ Σ∗}, is the transition monoid of the automaton, a submonoid of the full transformation monoid Tn := {f : Q → Q}, which contains all nn possible state mappings. Since every n-state task induces a submonoid of Tn, an architecture that can implement every element of Tn as a transition covers all n-state tasks; the NFSM of Section 6 is such an architecture (Proposition G.1). 

Recurrent neural network. An RNN carries a hidden state ht in a space H := Rd, where d is the hidden state dimension, and updates it through an input-driven update function Φ : H × Σ → H :

ht = Φ( ht−1, x t), t = 1 , . . . , L, (1) from an initial hidden state h0; the hidden state ht is the memory the RNN keeps of past inputs. Automaton realization. The automaton evolves in a discrete state space and the RNN in a continu-ous one, so realizing an automaton by an RNN requires an encoding ι : Q → H of automaton states as representations and a partial readout π : H ⇀ Q decoding them back, such that encoding a state, running the word through the RNN and reading out gives the same state as running the word through the automaton:

MΦ. We say that an RNN realizes the automaton (Q, Σ, δ ) if such a triple (ι, Φ, π ) exists. In this paper, we further ask which state-tracking properties survive computation at finite precision.

## 3 LENGTH INDEPENDENCE REQUIRES THREE CONDITIONS ON ONE STEP 

In this section, we first translate the trajectory-level conditions of Definition 2.2 into equivalent step-level conditions on Φ. We then show what these step-level conditions impose on the geometry of the RNN dynamics. First, (T1) with the empty word requires π ◦ ι = id Q (Lemma F.2). Second, (T1) requires the perturbed RNN update ˜Φx to map hidden states representing q to hidden states representing the correct automaton successor δx(q) at every step on the η-reachable set U ⋆ (Lemma F.4). Finally, (T2), the boundedness of U ⋆, keeps the representation within a fixed region of the hidden state space, so that a fixed, finite number of bits suffices at every sequence length (Proposition F.1). ## 4 LENGTH INDEPENDENCE REQUIRES A NONAFFINE RECURRENCE 

Length-independent realization imposes a dual constraint on Φ along every word: the image of each cell must be 2η narrower than the cell it lands in (Theorem 3.3(i)), while the images of cells heading to different states must stay 2η apart (Theorem 3.3(ii)), with margins that do not shrink as the word grows. This section shows that affine recurrences cannot satisfy this dual constraint along the words of a nondefinite target, and identifies what a recurrence requires to satisfy it. 4.1 AFFINE RECURRENCES SUPPLY A SINGLE RATE 

In affine recurrences, the displacement between two trajectories depends only on their initial dis-placement: 

Φw(h) − Φw(h′) = Aw(h − h′), Aw := Ax|w| · · · Ax1 . (5) An affine recurrence supplies a single rate Aw over the whole hidden state space, hence the same operator determines the dynamics of both the signal and the perturbation at each time step (Def-inition F.7, Proposition F.8). Along repeated words wk (w written k times, so Awk = Akw), the operator Akw that must damp the perturbation also shrinks the gap between the codes of two states that the target keeps apart, until that gap falls below 2η, which Theorem 3.3(ii) forbids. Selective (input-dependent) matrices modulate that single rate through time , not across space, so they do not escape this. Specifically, an affine recurrence loses either (R2) or (R3) depending on the spectral radius ρ(Aw):either ρ(Aw) ≥ 1 for some word, so perturbations accumulate or amplify, or ρ(Aw) < 1 for every word, so older inputs are forgotten along with the perturbation. Forgetting old inputs is harmless only when the target never needs them, that is, when it is a definite automaton, whose state de-pends only on the last k inputs, for some fixed k (Perles et al., 1963). The first regime precludes length independence for every target, the second for every nondefinite one. Both still allow length generalization up to a horizon Lmax .5Preprint. Under review. 6

Most scan-compatible architectures fall on one of the two branches of Theorem 4.1 (see Table 3 in Appendix C.2), and Section 7 shows both failure modes empirically. Concurrent work reaches the same limits of affine recurrences on error propagation by a different route (Chung et al., 2026). 4.2 TWO RATES REQUIRE MULTISTABILITY 

Theorem 4.1 shifts the question from the spectrum of a transition matrix to the geometry of the update map. Specifically, realization demands a cell partition (Theorem 3.2) that absorbs local per-turbations while mapping each region cleanly into its prescribed successor, a dynamic reminiscent of the restoring organ of Section 3. The canonical neural system with this error-correcting behavior is the Hopfield network (Hopfield, 1982), but its restoration is static : iterating Φ to convergence returns a corrupted state to the same 

pattern it started near. A state tracker must execute a dynamic restoration : a single step of Φx must simultaneously remove the perturbation and move the hidden state to the correct successor cell. Satisfying equation 4 thus requires a single update map to maintain multiple distinct cells, one per automaton state. When the target is not definite, some word w acts on Q as an idempotent δw that is not constant (Lemma F.11), so it fixes at least two states and, by Theorem 3.3, Φw shrinks each of their cells into itself while keeping them 2η apart. No affine map does this (Proposition F.22): the recurrence must be multistable , which requires a nonaffine operation on the previous hidden state 

ht−1 (Lemma F.17). Its robust capacity , the number of bits one map can hold under perturbation (Definition F.20), is then log 2 n, n being the number of cells, with one attractor per cell and one cell per automaton state. A definite target needs neither multistability nor nonaffinity (Proposition F.13). This capacity is set by the number of cells rather than by the dimension: extra width allows for wider margins rather than more states, and extra depth leaves the temporal recurrence within each layer affine, so the robust capacity of an affine recurrence beyond a bounded suffix of the input is zero at any width (Proposition F.22) and any depth, whatever the nonlinear maps between layers (Theorem F.25). 

## 5 LENGTH INDEPENDENCE IS COMPATIBLE WITH A PARALLEL SCAN 

To meet modern standards of scalability, a recurrence has to be compatible with the parallel scan .The scan operates on state-update maps Φx and their compositions Φw. Because function composi-tion is associative, these maps form a monoid; the core requirement for a scan-compatible recurrence is therefore not associativity, but bounded representation . The parallel scan combines these bounded composites pairwise across sequence steps, with costs governed by storage per composite ( b bits) and computation per composition ( c operations). 

Definition 5.1 (Scan-representable recurrence) . The recurrence ht = Φ( ht−1, x t) is scan-representable with budget (b, c ) if there exists an injective representation ζ : MΦ → { 0, 1}b of the maps of MΦ, the identity Φε included, and an algorithm computing ζ(Φ v ◦Φu) from (ζ(Φ v ), ζ (Φ u)) 

in c operations, for all Φu, Φv ∈ MΦ.

Given per-symbol representations ζ(Φ x1 ), . . . , ζ (Φ xL ), and assuming that building ζ(Φ x) from a symbol and evaluating a composite at a hidden state also cost O(c), a parallel scan computes 

h1, . . . , h L in depth O(log L), work O(Lc ), and memory O(Lb ) (Blelloch, 1990). The depth, the number of successive rounds of compositions, sets the running time with enough processors, whereas a sequential recurrence needs L rounds. 

Proposition 5.2 (The budget forces a finite reachable set) . If Φ is scan-representable with budget 

(b, c ), then MΦ has at most 2b elements and, for every initial hidden state h0, so does the set

The scan is itself a perturbation source. Affine maps form a monoid only in exact arithmetic: floating-point operations are not associative, so the composite a scan returns differs from the se-quential one, and an affine recurrence, lacking restoration, lets this error accumulate (Appendix D). The perturbation floor η is thus as much a product of parallelizing an affine recurrence as an assump-tion imposed on it. Multistable recurrences can resolve this mismatch by replacing continuous drift with discrete basins. Consequently, the hidden states reachable from an attractor are attractors, and each symbol acts as a transition table σx on the K attractor indices, which meets the budget of Definition 5.1: a table costs 

b = K⌈log 2 K⌉ bits, and composing two of them is a lookup per index, c = O(K) operations. This table-composition view explains architectures that achieve scan-compatible multistable dy-namics. Memory Recurrent Units (De Geeter et al., 2026; Brandoit et al., 2026), which assume that the hidden state converges onto an attractor between two steps, implicitly build such tables (Appendix C.3). Scan compatibility and length independence can coexist. Mapping attractor indices onto automaton states, with K = n, aq = ι(q) and cells Uq := aq ⊕ ¯Bη that fit inside the basins Vq , turns exact restoration into the cell inclusions of equation 4 as soon as every table is the target transition, σx =

δx: the recurrence then realizes the target with length independence (Theorem 3.2) and fits the budget at b = n⌈log 2 n⌉ and c = O(n). Since composites of index maps are index maps, the merge is exact whatever the shape of the tree and the scanned trajectory is bit-identical to the sequential one (Proposition G.1). ## 7 EXPERIMENTS 

We compare NFSM with three affine recurrences that cover both branches of Theorem 4.1: Mamba −, Mamba with negative eigenvalues (Grazzi et al., 2025), with ρ < 1; AUSSM (Karu-vally et al., 2025), with ρ = 1 ; and PD-SSM (Terzi´ c et al., 2025), with ρ < 1 and a hardmax that selects the transition matrix but does not act on the hidden state. The algebraic tasks present a sequence of generators of a target monoid, and the target at each posi-tion is the composite of the generators seen so far. They cover abelian groups ( Z2, Z16 ), nonabelian groups ( S3, S4, and the nonsolvable A5 and M11 , the first Mathieu group), and the noninvertible flip-flop monoid, drawn in two ways: DFF 5 never draws more than four consecutive identities, so that the state is a function of the last five inputs, whereas FF draws identity runs of unbounded length. The textual task TSO N (Srivastava et al., 2023) gives the same kind of algebra through text: swaps between N ∈ { 3, 4, 5} people holding N items are described in natural language, for example “ alice has the key. alice swaps with bob. . . . who has the key? ”, and the answer must be read off the tracked state. All models share one backbone (Appendix H.3): an encoder embeds the input tokens into a latent space, the recurrent layers act within this latent space, and a decoder maps it to the predicted states. Only the recurrent layers differ. The baselines use four layers of width 256 . The NFSM uses a single layer on the algebraic tasks, with one head on Z2, Z16 and the flip-flops, and several heads on the permutation groups, for instance 4 heads of 11 attractor indices on M11 , each tracking where one point is sent. On TSO N it stacks two multi-head layers. Models are trained on sequences of a fixed length, with the state supervised at every position, and tested on longer ones. Affine recurrences fail as Theorem 4.1 predicts. The flip-flop pair isolates the definite boundary: 

DFF 5 draws only definite words, which a contracting recurrence can hold, whereas FF does not. The two contracting baselines, Mamba − and PD-SSM, hold DFF 5 up to the longest tested length but fail on FF, while AUSSM, which does not contract, fails on both. The learned trajectories show the two branches (Appendix H.5). Where the baselines learn, the horizon they reach can vary widely across seeds (Appendix H.4). A horizon measured on one trained model therefore does not transfer to the next, which is why we ask for length independence rather than a long horizon. 

Extracted tables certify length independence. Each NFSM step commits to a table entry, so the tables a trained model implements can be read off its logits (Appendix H.6). For every algebraic task and every seed, they match the target exactly. By Propositions G.2 and G.4, this implies correct tracking at every length, provided the logit margin of Proposition G.3 exceeds the error of the logit computation. ## 8 DISCUSSION 

Scan compatibility, expressivity, and robustness to finite precision are often viewed as competing demands; we have shown that a single mechanism meets all three. A bounded representation budget forces the recurrence onto a finite set of reachable hidden states, and a well-separated set of them lets one map both erase the step-wise error and execute the next transition. An affine recurrence has a single rate, so at any finite precision it either lets perturbations grow or realizes at most a definite automaton, at any width and depth. For models built on contracting recurrences, this is an 

undeclared context window : older inputs reach the output only through a term that decays geomet-rically, so beyond a length set by learned gates and the arithmetic floor they are indistinguishable from noise, and that length is exceeded without any signal. The NFSM removes this barrier and leaves learning, the main practical obstacle, checkable rather than solved: the automaton a trained model implements can be read off its weights, so long-horizon correctness is verified rather than ex-trapolated (Appendix E). ## B EXTENDED DISCUSSION 

Robust state tracking asks one map to both erase the perturbation the previous step left behind, and move the state to its successor. An affine recurrence has a single rate, so it buys one at the expense of the other and its horizon is finite at any precision. NFSM does both, because a step is a rounding followed by a table lookup. 

The horizon is not an artifact of precision. Our argument turns on the existence of a perturbation, not its amplitude. For any η > 0 a contracting affine recurrence has a finite horizon, and η only sets where that horizon falls; more precise formats moves it, but does not remove it. Nor is it an artifact of the hardware: the scan that makes an affine recurrence fast reassociates its products and so supplies η by itself, whatever the format. NFSM escapes the horizon not by being more precise but by returning the state to a code point at every step, so that perturbations are annihilated instead of accumulated and, under a scan, are not created at all. 

An undeclared context window. For language models built on contracting recurrences the conse-quence is practical. The realized automaton is definite up to precision, so inputs older than k steps reach the output only through a term of size O(κk): the model has a context window, but an unde-clared one. Its length is set by learned gates and the arithmetic floor rather than by specification, it varies with the input as selectivity varies the decay rate, and, unlike overflow in attention, it is exceeded without any signal. 

Forgetting as computation, not decay. Krohn–Rhodes decomposes every finite-state behavior into simple groups, which remember reversibly, and flip-flops, which remember until reset (Krohn & Rhodes, 1965). Realizing both makes forgetting a chosen property of the transitions rather than an unwanted residual of the dynamics: what is discarded is chosen by the arriving symbol, a reset erases exactly what it collapses, and what no reset targets survives indefinitely. A contracting recurrence offers erasure through contraction, which is graded and unconditional. Persistence is therefore not the price of forgetting; it is what remains once forgetting is expressed as computation. 

Beyond gradient descent. Because the executive organ followed by the restoring organ is a table read off the logits by an arg max , the automaton a trained NFSM implements can be extracted, in-spected, and compared to a target. The same object can in principle be inferred in context rather than learned by gradient descent: a table is small enough to be written from a handful of demonstra-tions, which makes automaton induction at inference time, rather than in the weights, a natural next question. 

Limitations and outlook. Our guarantees cover finite-state tracking. Behaviors requiring un-bounded memory, such as counting without a modulus or stack-like nesting, fall outside the transformation-monoid setting and need a state set that grows with the input; how to keep restoration meaningful in that regime is open. The number of states is also a design choice, and while heads and their sizes can be over-provisioned, the trade-off between capacity and optimization is not yet characterized. Finally, durable state is exactly what partially observable control requires, so NFSM is a natural candidate for reinforcement learning agents whose belief must survive long episodes, which we leave to future work. 

## C RELATED WORK 

This appendix situates our results among five lines of work: parallel scans and the affine recurrences built on them, the expressivity of these recurrences, their length generalization and finite-precision limits, scan-compatible recurrences that are not affine, and recurrent networks as automata. Three subsections then go further: the classical roots of the restoration requirement (Appendix C.1), a survey placing scan-compatible affine architectures on the branches of Theorem 4.1 (Appendix C.2), and Memory Recurrent Units read as transition tables (Appendix C.3). 

Parallel scans and affine recurrences. A parallel scan computes all prefixes of a sequence under an associative operation in logarithmic depth (Blelloch, 1990). Martin & Cundy (2018) used it to train linear recurrences over the sequence length. Linearity in the hidden state has since become the design principle of scalable recurrent models: structured and diagonal state space models (Gu et al., 2022b;a; Gupta et al., 2022; Smith et al., 2023), selective ones (Gu & Dao, 2024; Dao & Gu, 2024; Lahoti et al., 2026), gated linear RNNs (Orvieto et al., 2023; Qin et al., 2023; 2024; De et al., 17 Preprint. Under review. 18 

2024; Feng et al., 2024), and linear attention with its gated and delta-rule variants (Katharopoulos et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2024a;b; 2025a). They parallelize over the sequence in different ways, through a parallel scan (Smith et al., 2023; Gu & Dao, 2024; Orvieto et al., 2023; Feng et al., 2024), a convolution for the time-invariant ones (Gu et al., 2022b;a; Gupta et al., 2022), or a chunkwise algorithm (Yang et al., 2024a;b; Dao & Gu, 2024), but all of them rely on the update being affine in the hidden state, so that the composite of two steps has a closed form. Appendix C.2 surveys them from this angle. We take the opposite reading of the same constraint: what a scan requires is a bounded representation of the composites (Definition 5.1), not an affine update, and Proposition 5.2 shows what this budget implies. 

Expressivity of affine recurrences. A large body of work asks which automata these recurrences can express. At logarithmic precision and under standard complexity assumptions, state space mod-els such as S4 and Mamba cannot track nonsolvable groups such as S5 (Merrill et al., 2024). At finite precision, state space models with nonnegative gates model exactly the star-free regular lan-guages (Sarrof et al., 2024), and a single layer of input-dependent complex diagonal transitions tracks no nonabelian group, while k such layers track exactly the groups with a subnormal series of length k with abelian factors (Shakerinava et al., 2026). A line of architectures then enlarges the reachable transition monoids: negative eigenvalues bring parity, and products of generalized Householder matrices bring every regular language (Grazzi et al., 2025), with the number of factors trading expressivity for cost (Siems et al., 2025), and an NC 1-complete problem within attention (Yang et al., 2025b); adaptive unitary transitions (Karuvally et al., 2025) and generalized delta rules (Peng et al., 2025) target state tracking directly, and the column one-hot transitions of PD-SSM reach every finite automaton with as many hidden dimensions as states (Terzi´ c et al., 2025). These results hold at logarithmic precision (Merrill et al., 2024), at a precision that grows logarithmically with the length (Karuvally et al., 2025), or at finite precision (Sarrof et al., 2024; Grazzi et al., 2025; Siems et al., 2025; Shakerinava et al., 2026), but in none of them is the computation perturbed. In the finite-precision model of Sarrof et al. (2024), numbers carry a fixed number of fractional bits and an unbounded number of integer bits, and arithmetic on them is exact: a gate equal to one then holds an exactly representable state forever, which is how a state space model with nonnegative gates models the flip-flop and, through the Krohn–Rhodes decomposition, every star-free language. Our model instead adds a perturbation of size up to η at every step and asks for correctness under every such perturbation (Definition 2.1). A gate equal to one then accumulates the perturbations, which is branch (i) of Theorem 4.1, and a nondefinite target such as the flip-flop FF is out of reach of every affine recurrence. The two results are consistent: theirs describe what an affine recurrence expresses when its arithmetic does not drift, ours what it retains when it does, as under a floating-point scan (Section 5 and Appendix D). A second difference is the target: they ask for the recognition of a language, we ask for the realization of the automaton at every step. Expressivity and robustness to finite precision are separate axes. 

Length generalization and finite precision. Empirically, state tracking learned by affine recur-rences holds up to around the training length and collapses not far beyond it (Shakerinava et al., 2026; Karuvally et al., 2025; Grazzi et al., 2025; Terzi´ c et al., 2025), and synthetic benchmarks isolate the failure: automata and group word problems (Liu et al., 2023a; Delétang et al., 2023), flip-flops with long gaps (Liu et al., 2023b), and tracking shuffled objects (Srivastava et al., 2023). The η-trajectory of Definition 2.1 is the pseudo-orbit used by Casey (1996) to analyze recurrent networks under bounded noise, while Maass & Orponen (1998) show that arbitrarily small stochas-tic analog noise reduces discrete-time analog computation to the power of finite automata; and that rounding changes the dynamics of a recursion, rather than only blurring them, has long been known from the limit cycles of recursive digital filters (Parker & Hess, 1971; Kaneko, 1973); under a scan, the nonassociativity of floating-point arithmetic makes the scan itself a perturbation source (Sec-tion 5). Concurrent work reaches the same limits of affine recurrences through the dynamics of error propagation (Chung et al., 2026). Our contributions are the step-level characterization of The-orem 3.1, the two-branch classification of Theorem 4.1, its extension to any depth (Theorem F.25), and a scan-compatible recurrence that escapes it. 

Scan-compatible recurrences that are not affine. Two routes lead beyond affine updates without giving up parallel training. The first keeps an arbitrary nonlinear recurrence and solves for its whole trajectory at once: DEER (Lim et al., 2024) and ParaRNN (Danieli et al., 2026) cast the sequence of updates as a system of equations and solve it by Newton iterations, each of which scans the affine lin-earization of the recurrence. They make nonlinear recurrences such as GRUs and LSTMs trainable 18 Preprint. Under review. 19 

at scale, but they return the sequential trajectory only up to the tolerance of the solver, which adds to η instead of removing it, and they place no constraint on the dynamics of the recurrence itself. The second route, which we take, chooses a nonlinear recurrence whose composites have a bounded exact representation. Memory Recurrent Units (De Geeter et al., 2026; Brandoit et al., 2026) are the closest prior instance: they assume that the hidden state converges onto an attractor between two steps, and on the attractors each symbol acts as a transition table (Appendix C.3). The NFSM makes this table explicit and learns it for every element of Td (Proposition G.1). PD-SSM (Terzi´ c et al., 2025) shares its column one-hot structure but applies its hardmax to the input, not to the hidden state: its step stays affine in ht−1, and its diagonal factor makes it contract (Appendix H.2). Restoration requires the nonlinearity to act on the hidden state (Lemma F.17). 

Recurrent networks as automata. That recurrent networks can implement finite automata, and must organize their hidden states into attractors to do so robustly, is classical (Omlin & Giles, 1996a;b; Casey, 1996); Appendix C.1 discusses this lineage and what changes here. The extraction of finite-state machines from trained networks (Casey, 1996) infers the automaton after training, from the organization of the hidden state space. In the NFSM the automaton is the parameterization itself: each step commits to a table entry, so the extracted tables are exactly the ones the model executes (Appendix H.6). Finally, the algebraic view of targets as transition monoids, with groups and flip-flops as the building blocks of the Krohn–Rhodes decomposition (Krohn & Rhodes, 1965), motivates both our benchmarks and the stacking of layers (Remark G.5). C.1 PROVENANCE OF THE RESTORATION REQUIREMENT AND THE TARGET 

This subsection places (R1) to (R3) and the target of Definition 2.2 in the lineage of classical work, and states what changes here. 

Roots. The restoration requirements have a strong connection to classical work. Separation is linked to Shannon (1948; 1949): signals must be distinguishable to be told apart at all and repeated restoration relates to Oliver et al. (1948) and von Neumann (1956): error must be erased at every stage, not once at the end. (R1) to (R3) inherit both. Likewise, the η-trajectory of Definition 2.1 is the η-pseudo-orbit of dynamical systems, used in this form for recurrent networks by Casey (1996); and that a bounded set closed under one perturbed step forces a strictly stable map is standard in set-invariance control (Rakovi´ c et al., 2005). 

Reliable computation from unreliable parts. von Neumann (1956) shows that reliable compu-tation from unreliable components requires executive and restoring organs in series at every stage, described in 1952 for a setting containing no recurrent networks. Winograd & Cowan (1963) shows that replication without restoration admits no computation capacity: for a redundant automaton the probability of malfunction falls as P ≈ d exp( −c/R ) and so vanishes only as the redundancy grows without bound, where for a coded channel P ≈ 2−n(C−R) vanishes with block length at any fixed rate R < C . Their conclusion is that such automata “do not exhibit noise-free behavior of the type that would permit computation capacities to be defined,” and that what buys capacity is complexity rather than copies. 

Recurrent networks as automata. Hopfield (1982) gives the canonical multistable recurrent sys-tem: an energy descent whose minima are the stored patterns, with basins that absorb perturbation, a restoring organ with the identity as index map. Omlin & Giles (1996a) exhibit the state-tracking version, attractor-based encodings of arbitrary deterministic finite automata, stable at arbitrary word length with finite weights, with the sigmoid-discriminant analysis in Omlin & Giles (1996b). Casey (1996) supplies the converse, that a recurrent network performing a finite-state computation must organize its state space into attractors mirroring the states of the minimal automaton. 1 Studying finite-state machine extraction from trained networks, with no architecture class in view, their The-orem 3.1 shows that a network robustly performing an automaton computation carries mutually disjoint closed sets with nonempty interior, one per state of the minimal DFA, on which its behav-ior agrees with the automaton; their Corollary 3.1 then recovers finiteness from compactness of the phase space. Maass & Orponen (1998) show that arbitrarily small analog noise reduces discrete-time analog computation to finite automata, and to the definite languages in particular together with Maass & Sontag (1999). C.3 MEMORY RECURRENT UNITS AS TRANSITION TABLES 

Memory Recurrent Units (De Geeter et al., 2026) start from a multistable recurrence and assume that it converges between two time steps, so that the state only ever sits on an attractor. Read on those attractors, each input symbol acts as a transition table, and the parallel scan composes tables exactly. 

The BMRU. With state dimension d and all operations coordinatewise, the Bistable Memory Re-current Unit (BMRU) of De Geeter et al. (2026) computes from the input

As a transition table. On the attractors {− αi, +αi} of coordinate i, a symbol acts in one of three ways: it sets the coordinate to +αi or to −αi when zi = 1 , and keeps it when zi = 0 . These are the three elements of the flip-flop monoid FF of Section 7, so the transition monoid of a BMRU layer on its attractors is a submonoid of FF d, one flip-flop per coordinate. Each table costs 2 bits per coordinate, and composition is exact: a later set overrides, a later keep returns the earlier element. The BMRU is therefore scan-representable in the sense of Definition 5.1 with b = 2 d and c = O(d),and the scanned trajectory is the sequential one. The update equation is affine in the state, with Ax = diag(1 − z), and a keep symbol gives Ax = I:read as an update on Rd, it lies on the ρ = 1 branch of Theorem 4.1 and does not restore. Its length independence comes from the state being held on the attractors, that is, stored as an index, which is the restoration the approximation assumed and which the implementation must then enforce. The tables act coordinate by coordinate and never read the state: no symbol exchanges +αi and −αi, so a single layer realizes no nontrivial group, not even Z2.

## D FROM FINITE PRECISION TO THE PERTURBATION MODEL 

Definition 2.1 replaces finite-precision arithmetic by an arbitrary perturbation of size at most η at every step. This appendix explains why this is a correct model: η is set by the number format, every run of a real machine is an η-trajectory, and asking for correctness under every such perturbation is what makes a guarantee robust to the hardware and to the implementation. 

Where η comes from. A machine stores states, parameters, and intermediate results on a fixed number of bits. It can therefore represent only a finite set Q ⊂ H of points; write [ · ]Q for the rounding that replaces an object by a representable one, e.g. the nearest point of Q under round-to-nearest. Since a finite set cannot contain every exact result, rounding moves points in general. It need not move the points that a particular model visits: an affine recurrence whose coefficients and codes are 0 and 1, such as a permutation matrix acting on one-hot vectors, computes every product and sum exactly, in any order, and its rounding error is zero. Such exact structures are the exception for learned models. Gates produced by sigmoids and exponentials of trained weights are generically not exactly representable, the moduli of PD-SSM lie in (0 , 1) and those of Mamba − in (−1, 1) , and the rotation of Appendix E is not representable in any format. We model this generic case by a per-step bound η > 0.

Why every perturbation, and not the actual rounding. The rounding errors of a run are not a property of the model. They depend on the hardware, through the number format, and on the implementation, through the order of the operations: floating-point addition and multiplication are not associative, so two runs of the same model on the same input, one sequential and one with a parallel scan, are not bit-identical. A particular rounding pattern can even help: rounding turns the recurrence into a map on the finite set Q, which can hold a few states at the scale of the rounding error, as the dead bands and limit cycles of recursive digital filters do, in fixed point and in floating point (Parker & Hess, 1971; Kaneko, 1973). Such behavior is not robust, since it can disappear when the number format or the order of the operations changes. We therefore ask for correctness under every perturbation of size at most η, which is what quantifying over all η-trajectories does (Definition 2.2). This makes the guarantee a worst-case one, and the impossibility results should be read accordingly: Theorem 4.1 states that no affine recurrence is guaranteed correct at every length under perturbations of size η, not that every run of every affine model fails. A model whose rounding happens to be harmless, or zero, can still be correct on its actual runs, but that correctness is not robust: it can be lost when the number format, the order of the operations or the parameters change (Appendix E).

## E LEARNING VERSUS FINITE PRECISION 

In practice, the main obstacle to length generalization is learning: a model must find, by gradient descent on short sequences, parameters that implement the target transitions. Our results do not address this obstacle directly. They address a different one, which remains even when learning succeeds. 

Three requirements. Tracking at every length needs a target that is expressible in exact arithmetic (Appendix C), a realization that is robust to a perturbation at every step (Theorems 3.1 to 4.1), and parameters that are found by training. 

A perfectly trained model still drifts. Figure 3 isolates the robustness failure by removing learning altogether. The target is Z7 under the constant word, and the recurrence is the ρ = 1 affine realization that a perfectly trained model would hold: the rotation of R2 by 2π/ 7, whose seven code points are the vertices of a regular heptagon. We round each entry of this rotation once to each number format, and then run the recurrence in fp64, so that no rounding occurs after the weights are set. Rounding the cosine and the sine separately leaves a rotation by a slightly wrong angle, scaled by a radius slightly different from one. The phase error therefore grows linearly with t, and the model decodes the wrong state as soon as it exceeds π/ 7, half the angle between two code points. The radius grows or decays geometrically, so the code points themselves leave the bounded region that restoration would require. First, the drift involves no training error: the weights are the exact solution, up to the resolution of the format. Second, it involves no rounding of the hidden state: the run itself is in fp64, and rounding at every step, or reassociating the products under a scan, only adds to it (Figure 2). Third, a finer format moves the failure but does not remove it. A better choice of representable weights can move the failing length, but no representable rotation by 2π/ 7 exists in any format.

Definition F.10 (Definite automaton) . A semiautomaton (Q, Σ, δ ) (Perles et al., 1963) is k-definite 

if δw is a constant map of Q for every word w with |w| ≥ k, and definite if it is k-definite for some k; we write ca ∈ QQ for the constant map with value a. Equivalently, the state reached after reading a word of length at least k depends on the last k symbols alone: splitting w = uv with 

|v| = k gives δw(q) = δv (δu(q)) , the constant value of δv , for every q and every prefix u. Definite automata are aperiodic, hence recognize star-free behaviors (Schützenberger, 1965; McNaughton & Papert, 1971; Eilenberg, 1974); the converse fails, so “definite” is strictly the stronger of the two conclusions.

A stack of affine recurrences, with arbitrary nonlinear maps between its layers, is not affine as a whole, so Theorem 4.1 does not apply to it directly. Each layer, however, stays affine in its own hidden state. 

Definition F.24 (Stack of affine recurrences) . Split the hidden state into layers, H = Rd1 ×· · ·× Rds ,each carrying the norm induced by ∥·∥ . A map of H is layerwise affine if the new value of each layer is an affine function of the old value of that same layer, whose coefficients may depend, arbitrarily, on the old values of the layers below it and on nothing else; the coefficients of the bottom layer are constant. A stack of affine recurrences is a recurrence whose every Φx is layerwise affine. 

Every stack of affine layers is of this form, whatever the maps between them (MLPs, normalizations, residual connections, input-dependent gates), because what a layer receives at a step is computed from the input symbol and from the layers below it. A causal convolution between layers fits as well, once its buffer is counted as a layer of its own. No continuity is assumed. The composite of two layerwise affine maps is layerwise affine, so every composite Φw of a stack is. 

Theorem F.25 (Depth does not create capacity) . Let Ψ be layerwise affine, of any depth and any widths, and let η > 0. Then Ψ holds (Definition F.20) at most one set in any family of pairwise disjoint sets, so its robust capacity is zero in every region. Consequently, a stack of affine recurrences realizes with length independence at most a definite automaton, at any depth and any width. Proof. The proof is by induction on the number of layers. With one layer, Ψ is affine, and the proof of Proposition F.22(i) uses nothing but the fact that Ψ holds the set: Ψ has a unique fixed point, and every set that Ψ holds contains the open η-ball around it. Two held sets therefore meet. With more layers, suppose that Ψ held two disjoint sets. The bottom layer reads nothing above it, so it evolves on its own as a single affine map. A perturbation confined to the bottom layer is a perturbation of the whole hidden state of the same size, so this map holds the bottom-layer projection of each of the two sets, and by the one-layer case its fixed point lies in both projections. Now cut both sets at this fixed point: keep, from each set, the hidden states whose bottom layer sits exactly at the fixed point, and drop the bottom layer. The two slices are nonempty, since the fixed point lies in both projections; bounded, since the sets are; and disjoint, since a point common to both slices would, with the bottom layer added back, be common to the two sets. A hidden state whose bottom layer sits at the fixed point keeps it there, because the bottom layer does not read the layers above, so on such hidden states Ψ acts on the upper layers only. This action holds each slice: a perturbation of size at most η on the upper layers, with none on the bottom one, keeps the hidden state inside its held set, and leaves the bottom layer at the fixed point. With the bottom layer frozen, finally, the coefficients of the second layer are constant, so the action on the upper layers is layerwise affine with one layer fewer. It holds two disjoint slices, which the induction hypothesis excludes. Nonlinear maps between layers thus change which point each layer settles on, but give no layer a second one. For the consequence, suppose that a stack realizes a nondefinite target with length independence at η, with the cells of Theorem 3.2. G.6 THE GRADIENT ESTIMATOR 

The arg max in equation 12 has zero derivative wherever it is defined, so the logits receive no gradient as written. We train with the straight-through Gumbel-softmax estimator (Jang et al., 2017), and the forward pass stays on indices throughout. We describe one head. 

Forward pass. During training, the logits are scaled by a sampling temperature ν, uxt := θxt /ν ,one independent standard Gumbel variable (Gt)jk is drawn per entry, and the table is σt(k) := arg max j (uxt + Gt)jk . At test time the Gumbel variables are removed and the table is σxt of equation 12. A sampled table is still an element of Td, so parts (i) and (ii) of Proposition G.2 hold at every training step.

The flip-flops. The flip-flop monoid acts on {0, 1} through three maps: the identity, reset (constant

In DFF 5, letters are uniform, except that four identities in a row are always followed by a reset or a set; the target is then a function of the last five letters, and the drawn words are definite. In FF, a letter is a reset or a set with probability 0.1, and the identity otherwise. Runs of identities have unbounded length, with mean 9, so the target depends on arbitrarily old letters and the drawn words are not. The length sweep draws FF with an identity probability of 0.99 instead, so that runs of identities have mean length 99 . The test sequences are then long and their information sparse: the target must be held across long runs of identities, which strengthens the dependence on old letters. 

Tracking Shuffled Objects. TSO N is adapted from the task of Srivastava et al. (2023). It involves 

N ∈ { 3, 4, 5} people (alice, bob, charlie, dana, eve) and as many items (ring, key, coin, stamp, card). Every word and punctuation mark is one token, for a vocabulary of 2N + 7 tokens. A sequence is made of sentences of five tokens, in three parts: 1. a preamble of N sentences, one per person in order, that assigns the items at random: alice has the key . ;2. swap sentences, each between a uniformly drawn pair of distinct people: bob swaps with alice . ;3. a question about a uniformly drawn item, who has the key ? , whose answer, the current holder of that item, is predicted at the last token. When the length is not a multiple of 5, extra “.” tokens are inserted after randomly chosen sentences, at most one per sentence, so that every length is built from the same local patterns. The underlying automaton reads one token at a time. Its state is a pair (p, g ): p is either idle or the first name of the sentence in progress, and g maps every item to its holder, or to “unset” before the preamble names it. A name read in the idle state is stored in p. A second name swaps the items of the two people and makes p idle again. An item closes a sentence p has the item , assigning it to 

p. Every other token leaves the state unchanged, so the question does not modify it. From the start state, with p idle and every item unset, the grammar reaches 44 , 202 and 1132 states for N = 3 , 4, 5.The target at every token is this state, and the answer at the last token is a function of the state and of the asked item. Answering the question is not state tracking in itself: the answer depends on the asked item, which is not part of the state. The last token is “?” and the item is the token before it, so the causal convolution before a higher layer lets its update at “?” depend on the item, and the final MLP reads the answer off the states of all layers. The question thus tests whether the tracked state can be used, not only whether it is kept. 39 Preprint. Under review. 40

Backbone. All models share one backbone of width m = 128 and differ only in the recurrent block. Tokens are embedded, passed through an MLP and a layer norm. Each layer then applies

The baselines have four layers with a recurrent width of 256 : d = 256 channels with dstate = 16 for Mamba − and AUSSM, and N = 256 with nM = 16 dictionary matrices for PD-SSM. The NFSM has one layer on the algebraic tasks and two layers on TSO. Loss. The loss is a cross-entropy on the state at every position, and on the answer at the last position for TSO. Two weights rescale the per-position terms. The first weight is a mask after the first error. For each training sequence, let t⋆ be the first position where the predicted state is wrong. Positions up to t⋆ have weight 1, and later positions have weight e−λ(t−t⋆) with λ = 3 . Once the state is wrong, the later predictions start from a wrong state, and their errors are consequences of the error at t⋆ rather than errors of their own. Supervising them at full weight would ask the model to reach correct states from a wrong one, which pulls the transitions away from the target. The mask concentrates the gradient on the first error, where a transition is actually wrong, and it moves along the sequence as training fixes earlier errors. The second weight is class balancing (especially for the DFF/FF and TSO). Each sequence contributes the weighted mean of its terms, and the loss is the mean over the batch. For the NFSM, a third term keeps the sampling exploratory at the first error. The three affine baselines all lose this. Mamba − contracts: the five clusters collapse onto a single point, which lands on a cell boundary, so after roughly one hundred steps the readout no longer separates the states. This is the ρ < 1 branch, where forgetting the input also forgets the state. AUSSM shows the ρ = 1 branch: the clusters do not collapse onto one point, but they stop being separated, and the states that started apart end up interleaved within the same few cells. H.6 EXTRACTION OF THE TRANSITION TABLES 

Because every NFSM step commits to a table entry, the tables a trained model executes can be read off its logits, and compared with the target entry by entry. This is a strength of the architecture: it turns the evaluation of length independence into a verification. Testing up to a length L only shows that the model is correct up to L, whereas tables that match the target show, by Proposition G.2, that it is correct on every word of every length. An affine recurrence offers nothing comparable, since it has no finite table to read. H.6.1 METHOD 

The procedure applies when the input alphabet Σ is finite, and uses the model at test time, without Gumbel noise. 

One layer, algebraic tasks. For each symbol x ∈ Σ, we pass x alone through the backbone up to the NFSM layer and read the logits θ(i) 

> x

of every head i. The column-wise arg max gives the table 

σ(i) 

> x

∈ Tdi , that is, the state that head i moves to from each of its di states when it reads x. Repeating this for all |Σ| symbols gives every transition of the layer. 

Convolutions and stacks. With a causal convolution of width k before each layer and several layers, as on TSO, the logits of a layer at step t depend on a window rather than on the current symbol: the last k tokens for the first layer, and for a higher layer the last k positions of the residual stream, which are functions of the tokens and of the states of the layers below (Remark G.5). Every window takes values in a finite set, so the method extends in principle: the tables of each layer can be extracted for every window that can occur, from the bottom layer upwards. A causal convolution only remembers its last k − 1 inputs, a finite amount of memory, so the stack remains a finite-state machine and its tables still describe it completely. The windows that can occur grow quickly with the number of layers and of objects, however, and we do not carry out this enumeration on TSO. Appendix H.6.3 instead inspects what the trained stack computes. 

Matching with the target. The tables are indexed by the attractor indices of the heads, not by the states of the task. To relate the two, we explore the joint indices that the tables reach from the initial joint index s0 of the model, and assign a task state to each of them: q0 to s0, and, whenever σx

H.7 WALL -CLOCK MEASUREMENTS 

Sections 5 and 6 claim that the NFSM runs under a parallel scan whose merge is a gather (Proposi-tion G.1). We measure the cost of one layer of the NFSM and of the baselines of Section 7, Mamba −,AUSSM and PD-SSM, against the sequence length L and the state size d (Figure 6). 

Setup. Each layer is timed alone: the embedding, the causal convolution of the backbone and the MLP are the same for all models and are left out. The NFSM has one head of d attractor indices, Mamba − and AUSSM have d channels with dstate = 16 , and PD-SSM has N = d. We vary L from 

26 to 217 at d = 16 , and d from 2 to 256 at L = 2 12 . The inputs are batches of 16 sequences of Gaussian vectors of width 256 , in float32 (with complex64 states for AUSSM and PD-SSM). For each model, we compare the parallel scan with a sequential loop over time steps that computes the same layer. We time a training step, that is, the forward pass and the gradient of the mean squared output with respect to the parameters, including the Gumbel noise and surrogate gradient of the NFSM and the surrogate of PD-SSM. Every configuration runs on one NVIDIA A100 (80 GB). The step is compiled once, called 5 times to warm up, and timed over 10 synchronized calls. We report the median; compilation time is not included. 

Chunked runs. By default, a layer processes the whole sequence at once. When a configuration does not fit in memory this way, we run it chunked : the sequence is split into consecutive blocks of C steps, the layer processes one block at a time and passes its last state on to the next block, and the backward pass recomputes the intermediate quantities of each block instead of storing them for the whole sequence. The result is unchanged up to round-off (which is, once again, another source of perturbation introduced by an implementation choice), and memory is bounded by the size of a block instead of by L, but the blocks are processed one after the other, which costs time. They are circled in red in Figure 6. We emphasize that the chunked approach can be used to fit any configuration in memory, at the cost of time, and that the parallel scan is still used within each block. It was in practice used interchangeably with the unchunked approach during the training of the models depending on the available memory. 

Memory. The peak memory in Figure 6 (bottom) is approximate and should be read with care. The panel shows orders of magnitude and trends, not exact footprints. 

Results. Against the length. The parallel scan is faster than the sequential loop at every measured length, and the gap widens with L. At d = 16 , the NFSM is also the fastest of the four at every length. Against the state size. At L = 2 12 , the NFSM is the fastest model from d = 4 to d = 64 .It grows faster with d than the two diagonal models, since it computes d2 logits at every step. It remains faster than PD-SSM, which also computes a d × d matrix at every step, at every state size we measure. In practice, some architecture have already been optimized for speed and memory by the field, and custom kernels exist for them. We did not used them in our measurements, and we agree on the fact that this is not a fair comparison on deployment. The message of this experiment is not that some models are faster than others, but that the NFSM is compatible with a parallel scan and that it is fast and memory-efficient enough to be trained on long sequences; with scalings that are comparable to those of the baselines, it is a practical architecture.
