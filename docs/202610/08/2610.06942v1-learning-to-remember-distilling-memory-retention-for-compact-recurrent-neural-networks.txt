Abstract 

Deep learning models, particularly recurrent neural networks and their variants, such as long short-term memory, have significantly advanced time series analysis. These models capture complex, sequential patterns in time series, enabling real-time assessments. However, their high computational complexity and large model sizes pose challenges for deployment in resource-constrained environments, such as wearable devices and edge computing platforms. Knowledge Distillation (KD) o ff ers a solution by transferring knowledge from a large, complex model (teacher) to a smaller, more e ffi cient model (student), thereby retaining high performance while reducing computational demands. Current KD methods, originally designed for computer vision tasks, neglect the unique temporal dependencies and memory retention characteristics of time series models. To bridge this gap, we propose a novel KD framework termed Mem ory-Discrepancy Knowledge Distillation ( MemKD ). MemKD leverages a specialized loss function to capture memory retention discrepancies between the teacher and student models across subsequences within time series data, ensuring that the student model e ff ectively mimics the teacher’s behaviour. This approach facilitates the development of compact, high-performing recurrent neural networks suitable for real-time, time series analysis tasks. We provide additional experiments, in-depth theoretical analysis, and insights into the proposed framework across extended time series benchmarks. Our experiments demonstrate that MemKD significantly outperforms state-of-the-art KD methods. Additionally, we demonstrate that it can match the teacher model’s performance across a wide range of compression levels, achieving notable reductions in parameter count and memory usage without a significant loss in accuracy. 

Keywords: knowledge distillation, Time series analysis, Recurrent neural networks 

1. Introduction 

Time series analysis has become fundamental to modern decision-making systems, enabling critical applications from patient health monitoring to industrial fault detection and envi-ronmental sensing. These applications require a careful balance between achieving high performance in handling complex time series data and delivering real-time predictions, while ensuring the solutions remain lightweight and suitable for deployment on edge devices such as smartphones and wearable sensors. Re-current neural networks (RNNs) and their variants, particularly long Short-Term memory networks (LSTMs) and gated recur-rent units (GRUs), are excel at time series analysis due to their adeptness in sequence modeling. RNNs maintain memory-like arrays known as hidden states, enabling them to retain and re-call long-term dynamics within sequential data. However, as input sequences are processed, hundreds of thousands of hidden states need to be updated with nonlinear functions specified by millions of parameters. Consequently, RNNs share common drawbacks inherent to deep neural networks (DNNs), such as high computational complexity, large parameter spaces, and high inference latency. These limitations pose challenges for real-world time series applications, which demand low power and space requirements. Knowledge distillation (KD) transfers knowledge from a large teacher model to a smaller student model, maintaining perfor-mance while improving e ffi ciency. Introduced by Ba and Caru-ana [1] and extended by Hinton et al. [2], Romero et al. [3] for image classification, KD showed that smaller models could match or outperform deeper ones by matching the teacher’s out-puts or intermediate features. Despite its broad applicability, KD remains understudied in time series analysis. Ay et al. [4] conducted the first study on KD for time series classification using fully convolutional networks, following Hinton et al. [2] to align the teacher and student output probability distributions. Subsequently, Ouyang et al. [5] introduced a KD framework for TSC, extracting knowledge in both time and frequency domains by aligning output distributions in separate domains. Ma et al. [6] specifically explored KD using an RNN teacher, yet similarly relied on directly matching the teacher and student outputs. However, these studies primarily adapt KD frameworks orig-inally developed for computer vision tasks, without tailoring their loss functions to exploit the unique characteristics of time series data. Time series values, as opposed to visual data, are unidirectionally correlated, and during classification, short con-tiguous subsequences often contain much of the discriminative information [7, 8]. Therefore, adaptions of image approaches for time series may disrupt knowledge of those discriminative subsequences and their correlations. Considering that the ability to maintain memory is crucial to the success of RNNs, this raises a natural question: why not leverage knowledge of how the teacher learns to retain mem-ory? 2. Background and Related Works 

2.1. Time Series Analysis 

Many real-world phenomena evolve over time, resulting in data that are naturally collected as sequential observations. Con-sequently, time series data typically manifest various systematic patterns, including trends, seasonalities, cyclic variations and irregular movements [9]. However, analyzing time series data poses challenges as it provides only two primary features: the point in time and the observed value itself. Analyzing them as sets of subsequences has been the most intuitive approach for extracting discriminative and interpretable features for further processing. Therefore, most traditional machine learning ap-proaches, including shapelets [8], motif discovery [10], discord discovery [11], and time series chains [12], relied on subse-quence analysis for their success. The enduring success of these methods as stable approaches over decades highlights the im-portance of preserving subsequence-level knowledge, which we account for in designing our knowledge transfer function. 

2.2. Recurrent Neural Networks 

Time series analysis has drastically advanced with the rapid development of deep learning. Among these developments, RNNs are a widely adopted type of neural network that allow for the modelling of the sequential nature of time series data by storing past context [13, 14]. LSTM [15] and GRU [16] were introduced as extensions to vanilla RNNs, addressing common drawbacks such as the vanishing gradient problem and the di ffi -culty in learning long-term dependencies. These variants have been extensively studied for time series analysis, across various application domains [17, 18]. However, these models exhibit higher computational complexities and significantly slower pro-cessing times due to their cascaded connections [19, 20]. This limits their applicability in edge deployment and necessitates the need for model compression techniques. 

2.3. Semantics from Hidden States 

The core mechanism of RNNs and their variants is a self-connected hidden state layer that captures complex relationships between observations within a sequence. Several studies in NLP have attempted to interpret how RNNs work internally with their memory and whether their hidden states encode meaningful semantic information [21, 22]; however, such interpretations of hidden states remain largely unexplored in the context of time series analysis. For example, Karpathy et al. [23] found that when RNNs are applied to a sequence of words, some hidden states can be interpreted as language context, such as quotes or punctuation, while others cannot. Ming et al. [24] explored how information stored in hidden states correlates with certain word clouds. Despite these findings, limited work has exploited such hidden-state semantics for downstream learning, and, to the best of our knowledge, no prior method has explicitly utilized them as a source of knowledge in a KD framework. 22.4. Knowledge Distillation 

The essence of KD lies in two primary objectives: firstly, ex-tracting rich knowledge from the teacher network, and secondly, transferring this knowledge to guide the training of a student network. To ensure the quality of knowledge, various methodolo-gies have been proposed. The most commonly utilized methods are logit-level KD techniques, which extract knowledge from softened output distributions [2]. DKD [25] improves perfor-mance by decoupling logit-level knowledge from target and non-target class distributions. Beyond the logits from the last layer, Fitnets [3] proposed directly matching features from intermedi-ate layers. Transferring feature-level knowledge using attention maps has also been studied [26, 27]. VID [28] aims to maximize mutual information between the student and teacher networks by variational information maximization, while RKD [29] pro-posed extracting relationships among input data samples. These methods are considered state-of-the-art in KD; however, they were originally developed and tested in the context of computer vision tasks. Consequently, the loss functions employed in most of the aforementioned methods were specifically designed to capture visual features and may not have direct analogies for time series data. Recent works have only begun addressing this gap by adapting these image approaches for time series. For example, the work in [4, 5] studied logit-level KD for time se-ries, and DT2W [30] extends Fitnets [3] by replacing Euclidean distance with soft-dynamic time warping distance. Xu et al. [31] utilized adversarial learning to compress RNN models into CNNs in their studies focusing on disparate architecture KD methods [31, 20]. However, possibility of knowledge extraction from RNN memory states has received limited research attention. A closely related study by Murata et al. [32] considers hidden states for time series forecasting by directly aligning the final hidden states of the teacher and student. However, this approach essentially treats hidden states as intermediate features for direct feature matching, without interpreting or exploiting how the informa-tion encoded in these states evolves as memory over the input sequence. In contrast, we propose a new method for compress-ing RNN models that extracts meaningful knowledge from how memory states evolve, rather than simply matching the states directly. This approach enables seamless knowledge transfer from a larger RNN-based model to a significantly smaller one without requiring the teacher and student hidden states to have matching dimensions.

3.2.1. Formulating Knowledge from Hidden States. 

Given an input time series sequence Xi = nx(1) , x(2) , · · · , x(T )o,the vanilla RNN depicted in Figure 2 processes each input time step x(t) ∈ Rn to maintain a time-varying memory state known as the hidden state h(t) ∈ Rm. At time step t, the RNN updates hidden state h(t−1) to h(t) using both x(t) and h(t−1) as: 

h(t) = f (Wh h(t−1) + Wi x(t)) (2) where Wh and Wi are weight matrices and f is a non-linear activation function which constrains the range of h(t). After processing the entire sequence at time step T , a linear layer can be trained upon last hidden state h(T ) to derive the output probability distribution for classification tasks. By unrolling past hidden states in Equation (2), each hidden state h(t) at time step t can be expressed as a function of past input values up to time step t, nx(1) , x(2) , · · · , x(t)o and the initial hidden state value h(0) . In other words, each hidden state vector represents the state of the RNN’s memory at a particular time step, incorporating information about the input sequence up to that point. It represents a compact representation of the input sequence’s temporal dynamics up to that time step. Therefore, the hidden state can be seen as evolving over time as the RNN processes the input sequence, capturing the temporal dependen-cies and patterns present in the data. 3.2.3. Seamless Feature Distillation. 

While hidden states in RNNs capture temporal dependencies and information flow across time steps, they are internal repre-sentations of the data that serve a similar purpose to intermediate feature maps in temporal direction. Feature distillation refers to providing hints from intermediate representations to improve the training of the student, besides directly matching output dis-tributions. Initially introduced in Fitnets [3], feature distillation loss is typically formulated as: 

LFea_KD = ℓf (ϕt(ψt(X)) , ϕ s(ψs(X))) (7) where ψt(X) and ψt(X) are feature maps of teacher and student respectively. Moreover, ϕt, ϕs are some transformation functions to match the shape of teacher and student feature maps and ℓf (.)is a similarity measure to match feature maps. When we consider the hidden state matrices Ht ∈ R(T ×mt ) and Hs ∈ R(T ×ms) across all temporal steps as ψt(X) and ψs(X), respectively, and ft and fs

(in Equation (4) and Equation (5)) as ϕt and ϕs, respectively, our proposed method can be seen as a feature distillation method. Generally it is common for the student’s intermediate feature dimensionality to be smaller than that of the teacher’s. To bridge this gap, many methods [3, 27] introduce an intermediate fully connected layer to connect hint (feature layer of teacher) and guided (feature layer of student) layers. However, this additional layer introduces numerous auxiliary parameters, which signif-icantly increase the space and computational demands while training, sometimes even surpassing the size of both teacher and student models [34]. In contrast, our proposed method does not necessitate such a regressor, even when the shapes of our feature maps di ff er. We calculate the feature distillation loss in an indirect yet semantic manner, thereby avoiding the need for explicit alignment of feature map dimensions. Therefore, our method stands out as an e ffi cient feature distillation method that imposes no additional computational burden to align features.

4.2. Datasets 

We used 56 datasets from UCR-2015 archive [35], which consists of an extensive catalog of time series from various do-mains. We selected datasets representing three categories based on time series length: ’short’, ’medium’, and ’long’ [36, 37, 38]. The length of a ’short’ dataset is no more than 150, a ’medium’ dataset varies from 150 to 500, and a ’long’ dataset has a length of over 500. Additionally, following [38], we prioritized datasets with a large number of output classes, as these are considered the most challenging and have significant real-world applications. Our final set includes 23 short, 17 medium, and 16 long datasets, of which 18 have five or more output classes. The datasets ensure a comprehensive evaluation of TSC methods across various domains, encompassing di ff erent recording durations, sensor configurations, and data collection methods. An overview of these datasets is provided in Table 2. To ensure a fair evaluation, we maintained the original train /test split and allocated 20% of the training set as a validation set. As a preprocessing step, we standardized all time series to a length of 100 using linear interpolation, followed by z-normalization. 

4.3. Experiment Setup 

For each dataset, we first train a teacher model across five random initializations and select the best-performing model for distilling knowledge to student models. The best teacher model is chosen based on the area under the precision-recall curve (AUC-PRC) evaluated on the validation set, ensuring reproducibility in real-world settings [4]. Next, we train the student models by distilling knowledge from the selected teacher model. In addition to MemKD, we implement state-of-the-art distillation methods for comparison, including Base-KD [2], FitNet [3], RKD [29], Att [26], DKD [25], and DT2W [30]. The ablation re-sults, which highlight the importance of each component, are summarized in table 6. We observed that MemKD-Long showed a slight improvement over MemKD-Short, with an increase of 1-2% in average performance metrics. 75.5. Sensitivity Analysis on β.

We also conducted an ablation study on β, which controls the importance of the memory-discrepancy distillation loss term relative to the task loss for six di ff erent datasets. The results are shown in Figure 4. AUC-PRC scores improved for almost all non-zero values of β compared to when β = 0 (i.e. when our loss term is totally absent). The performance of our model remains stable across moderate values of β and is significantly better than when β = 0. For very large values of β, the performance improvement over β = 0 diminishes. This is expected because when β is too large, the distillation loss dominates the total loss, which can lead to over-regularization. In other words, the student model focuses excessively on mimicking the teacher model, potentially at the expense of learning the task-specific features necessary for optimal performance on the target task. 5.6. Evidence for Hidden-State Alignment with the Teacher 

Figure 5 visualizes the last five hidden-state representations of the teacher, Base, Base-KD, and MemKD models on UWaveGes-tureLibraryAll using t-SNE. The Base student exhibits scattered and substantially overlapping hidden-state representations, in-dicating less discriminative placement of samples in the latent space. Conventional KD (Base-KD) improves this organization to some extent, but considerable inter-class overlap remains. In contrast, MemKD produces more compact and distinctly placed class-specific regions, more closely resembling the well-organized structure observed in the teacher. Since the visualiza-tion incorporates the last five hidden states rather than only the final representation, this result suggests that MemKD improves the placement and organization of representations across the stu-dent’s overall hidden states, progressively forming more class-discriminative feature structures. This supports the intended role of MemKD in transferring teacher knowledge throughout the student’s hidden-state learning process rather than constraining supervision only to the final representation. 

6. Conclusion 

Most prior work on feature distillation has explicitly focused on computer vision tasks, with loss functions assumed to cap-ture knowledge solely about visual features. These methods often lack clear analogies for time series. We identified and addressed this limitation by introducing a novel KD framework tailored for deep time series recurrent networks. Specifically, we investigated how memory di ff ers and evolves within RNNs and whether we can use information about memory retention over short contiguous subsequences to improve the performance of a student model. By leveraging a unique loss function that captures memory retention discrepancies across subsequences, MemKD e ff ectively transfers knowledge from a large, complex LSTM model to a significantly smaller LSTM model. The ex-tensive experiments, using fifty-six time series datasets, demon-strate that MemKD significantly outperforms logit-level and feature-level state-of-the-art KD methods, achieving substantial reductions in parameter size and memory usage while maintain-ing comparable performance. Future work will focus on further optimizing the MemKD framework and studying the knowl-edge of memory retention on longer subsequences, providing comprehensive coverage over the whole time series instance.
