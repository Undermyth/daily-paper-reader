Abstract 

Efficient long-context inference is essential for large language models (LLMs), yet it poses a severe computational bottleneck. Hash-based retrieval offers an efficient alternative by encoding queries and keys into binary codes and using Hamming distance for key selection. However, this leads to a critical mismatch between Hamming distance and attention relevance. Query-Key logits depend jointly on directional similarity and feature magnitudes, whereas hash binarization discards magnitude information, causing both false-positive retrieval of low-logit keys and false-negative omission of high-logit keys. To address these failures, we propose Hierarchical Hash Retrieval (HHR) , a coarse-to-fine framework that progressively improves retrieval accuracy through Geometry-Aware Key Routing (GKR) and 

Learned Hash Projection (LHP) . GKR learns a head-wise orthogonal transformation to redistribute feature magnitudes and derive more discriminative page-level logit bounds, enabling effective pruning of low-logit keys while preserving important candidates. LHP then learns a head-wise projection space that aligns Hamming distance with the true Query-Key relevance ranking for fine-grained retrieval. By combining GKR and LHP, HHR suppresses false positives and recovers false negatives, substantially improving the fidelity of hash-based sparse attention. Extensive experiments across diverse LLMs and benchmarks demonstrate that HHR achieves superior performance over existing methods. For example, on LongBench, HHR improves the average score by 1.10 points and, at a context length of 128K, achieves up to a 3.30 × decoding speedup and a 2.83 × end-to-end speedup for Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. The code is publicly available at https://github.com/lianjunl13-sudo/HHR .

## 1 Introduction 

The ability to process long contexts has become a critical requirement for large language models (LLMs) [46, 48] in a wide range of real-world applications, including document understanding, retrieval-augmented generation, and multi-turn reasoning over extended dialogues [37, 41, 51]. However, the complexity of the self-attention mechanism with respect to sequence length imposes a severe computational bottleneck, making efficient long-context inference a pressing challenge [36, 39, 41]. To mitigate this, sparse attention methods have been extensively explored [10, 12, 42, 54]. These methods reduce the number of attended keys by selecting a small subset of important positions, thereby circumventing the need for full attention over the entire context [5]. Among the various sparse attention strategies [6, 31], hash-based retrieval has attracted considerable interest due to its adaptability and efficiency [4, 7]. By projecting queries and keys into a compact space and encoding them into binary codes, hash-based methods first adaptively extract the important keys based on Hamming distance, which can be executed using efficient XOR and POPCNT operations, and then

For instance, MagicPIG [4] employs locality-sensitive hashing to select a small subset of candidate keys from the full KV cache. HATA [12] learns binary hash representations for queries and keys to approximate Query-Key similarity via Hamming distance. Despite these gains, we uncover a critical mismatch in hash-based retrieval, where the magnitude information discarded for efficient hashing is essential for determining attention relevance. Specifically, Query-Key logits depend jointly on directional similarity and magnitudes, whereas hash binarization preserves only the signs of projected features and discards their magnitudes. As a result, Hamming distance deviates from the true Query-Key relevance, leading to inaccurate retrieval. As shown in Fig. 2, this mismatch gives rise to two complementary types of retrieval failure. First, directionally similar keys yield small Hamming distances to the query yet low logits due to their low magnitudes, becoming false positives that waste budget in Region III. Second, keys with large magnitudes yield high logits to the query but diverge directionally, producing large Hamming distances and becoming false negatives that miss important tokens in Region II. To address both failure modes, as shown in Fig. 1, we propose Hierarchical Hash Retrieval (HHR) ,a novel coarse-to-fine framework that tackles false positives and false negatives. HHR comprises two complementary modules, including Geometry-Aware Key Routing (GKR) for coarse-grained pruning, and 

Learned Hash Projection (LHP) for fine-grained hash retrieval. In the first stage, GKR partitions keys into non-overlapping pages and estimates the maximum logit of each page using per-dimension extrema. Pages with bounds below a threshold are discarded to eliminate low-relevance keys early. To obtain more discriminative bounds while preserving exact logits, GKR learns a head-wise orthogonal transformation that redistributes feature magnitudes across dimensions. The resulting extrema tighten the bounds for irrelevant pages, effectively pruning Region III false positives without sacrificing important keys. In the second stage, LHP performs fine-grained hashing over the surviving candidates. Unlike conventional random projections, LHP learns a head-wise projection that aligns Hamming distance with the true relevance ranking. Differentiable sign relaxation enables gradient-based training, while orthogonality, bit-wise decorrelation, and balance regularization improve the quality of the binary space. Consequently, LHP substantially reduces Region II false negatives by assigning a more favourable hash space. Extensive experiments across various LLMs and benchmarks demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed HHR. For example, on LongBench, HHR achieves average scores of 46.96, 44.46, and 45.66 on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Mistral-7B-Instruct, and Qwen3-4B, respectively, outperforming SOTA by 1.10, 2.82, and 2.69. At the same time, HHR achieves up to a 3.30 × decode speedup and a 2.83 × end-to-end speedup at a 128K context length on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Structured sparse attention. Sparse attention mitigates the inference cost of long-context modeling by attending to only a subset of Query–Key pairs. Early approaches rely on structured sparsity patterns. For instance, LongGen combines local windows, attention sinks, and block-sparse attention for efficient long-context generation [11]. DuoAttention assigns full or restricted KV cache depending on each head’s retrieval capability [45]. Star Attention employs distributed block-sparse attention to scale to extremely long sequences [1]. More recent methods move beyond fixed patterns by determining sparse regions in an input-dependent manner. HiP progressively prunes irrelevant blocks through hierarchical search [24]. 

Dynamic sparse attention . Dynamic sparse attention methods operate in an input-dependent manner [6, 8, 21, 28, 40, 54]. FlexPrefill adaptively selects sparse patterns and budgets conditioned on queries and attention distributions [23]. SeerAttention introduces a learnable gating network to predict salient blocks [9]. SpargeAttention discards low-value computations via online block-level filtering [52]. Twilight adopts a hierarchical Top-p mechanism to dynamically adjust the sparsity budget [30], suggesting that recent sparse attention is gradually shifting from predefined patterns toward query-dependent selection. NSA and MoBA 2Figure 1: Overview of Hierarchical Hash Retrieva (HHR). reduce the attention scope through hierarchical token selection and block-level routing, respectively [33, 49]. Another line of work leverages clustering or external memory to compress the search space, including Squeezed Attention, ClusterAttn, and Dynamic Block-Sparse Attention [18, 43, 53]. 

Hash-based sparse attention. Hash-based sparse attention represents another efficient paradigm that leverages hashing to identify important keys relevant to each query. HyperAttention [14] employs locality-sensitive hashing (LSH) to identify dominant entries and approximates the remaining entries through sampling. MagicPIG [4] leverages LSH to enable sampling-based attention approximation, using importance sampling over key-value pairs to estimate the attention output. HAX [50] combines LSH attention with Key Selection attention to capture content-dependent Query-Key interactions and shared attention to a small set of globally important keys, respectively. HashAttention [7] and HATA [12] both learn hash representations for queries and keys, using binary codes to approximate their similarity relationships for efficient pivotal-token selection. However, existing hash-based methods fail to consider the heterogeneous contributions of different feature dimensions to attention matching, leading to a misalignment between Hamming distance and the actual Query-Key relevance, making the retrieval accuracy unsatisfactory.

3.2 Motivation 

Hash-based sparse attention methods typically use random projections to map queries and keys into a lower-dimensional hashing space, convert the projected features into binary codes, and retrieve keys according to Hamming distance [4, 7]. However, Hamming distance can be poorly aligned with the actual Query-Key logits, leading to both false positive and false negative retrievals. 3To characterize this misalignment, Fig. 2 compares the Hamming distance with the Query-Key logits. The orange dashed line denotes the Rop-5% boundary according to Query-Key logits, while the blue dashed line denotes the Top-5% boundary according to Hamming distance. Their intersection divides the key space into four regions: • Region I (upper-left): Keys with small Hamming distances and high Query-Key logits, which are correctly identified as important by both hashing and original attention. • Region II (upper-right): Keys with large Hamming distances but high Query-Key logits, which are important for attention but are missed by hash retrieval. • Region III (lower-left): Keys with small Hamming distances but low Query-Key logits, which are incorrectly retained by hash retrieval. • Region IV (lower-right): Keys with large Hamming distances and low Query-Key logits, which are correctly identified as unimportant by both mechanisms. Regions II and III stem from the misalignment between Hamming similarity and Query-Key relevance. Specifically, the Query-Key logit between a query q and a key k is q⊤k = ∥q∥∥ k∥ cos θ, where cos θ

captures directional similarity and ∥q∥, ∥k∥ determine the magnitude. Hamming distance, in contrast, depends only on binary signs and discards magnitudes, so it mainly reflects directional consistency in hash space but cannot preserve the magnitude information that determines key importance in attention [34]. 3.3 Hierarchical Hash Attention 

Motivated by the above analysis, we propose Hi-erarchical Hash Retrieval (HHR), which addresses the two types of retrieval failure via a coarse-to-fine strategy combining Geometry-Aware Key Routing (GKR) and Learned Hash Projection (LHP), as illustrated in Fig. 1.

## 4 Experiments 

4.1 Experimental Setup 

Baselines and configurations. We compare HHR against seven baselines. Full Attention serves as the dense reference. Under the same token budget, Oracle selects the exact Top-K keys based on real Query-Key attention scores. The sparse retrieval baselines include HATA [12], MagicPIG [4], and Loki [35]. For KV cache compression, StreamingLLM [44] retains attention sinks and recent tokens, whereas SnapKV [29] selects informative cached positions from an observation window. Each baseline follows its recommended configuration under a comparable token budget. All methods use the Top-K ratio of 1.5%, the same prompt templates, and retain full attention in the first two layers. Additional experimental details and the hyperparameter settings are provided in Appendix A. All experiments are conducted on a single NVIDIA A800 GPU with PyTorch [2]. 

Models, datasets, and metrics. HHR is evaluated on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct [13], Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 [20], and Qwen3-4B [47] by using LongBench [3] and RULER [19] datasets. LongBench contains 16 English datasets covering question answering, summarization, few-shot learning, synthetic retrieval, and code completion, on which we report our results. Evaluation follows the official metric for each dataset, including F1, ROUGE-L, accuracy, and code similarity. RULER covers 11 tasks at a context length of 32K. These tasks examine retrieval, aggregation, and question answering. Both task-level and average accuracy are reported. 

4.2 Main Results 

Results on LongBench. As shown in Table 1, on LongBench, HHR demonstrates strong performance across all three model families. Compared with other sparse-attention and KV cache compression methods, HHR achieves average scores of 46.96, 44.46, and 45.66 on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Mistral-7B-Instruct, and Qwen3-4B, respectively, obtaining the best results among these methods for each model. HHR also maintains consistent advantages on single-document QA, summarization, and few-shot tasks. These results indicate that HHR can effectively preserve task-relevant information and achieve strong cross-task generalization. 

Results on RULER. As shown in Table 2, on RULER, HHR also consistently outperforms other methods for all three model families. HHR maintains accuracy close to Full Attention. In contrast, SnapKV, MagicPIG, and Loki suffer substantial performance degradation. For example, HHR achieves a mean performance of 76.23 and 82.71 on Mistral-7B-Instruct and Qwen3-4B, improving the SOTA by 6.43 and 4.02, respectively.

As shown in Fig. 5, the datasets include 2WikiMQA [16], TREC [27], and MultiFieldQA-en [3]. HHR achieves the best performance across all datasets and backbones. After randomizing R, the performance drops by up to 2.52 and 2.23 points on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Qwen3-4B, respectively. After randomizing W, the performance drops by 1.50 and 2.50 points, respectively, indicating that both components play important roles in improving performance. In addition, jointly training R and W

Context Length. We evaluate inference efficiency at context lengths of 16K, 32K, 64K, and 128K. ## 5 Limitations and Future Work 

Despite achieving the best performance in certain cases, HHR still exhibits a performance gap relative to oracle Top-K selection. Therefore, developing more effective strategies remains an important focus for future work. For example, a stronger learning objective could potentially yield a better transformation R and projection W. Moreover, HHR is orthogonal to other compression techniques such as KV quantization. The effect of combining HHR with them remains an interesting question but is beyond the scope of this paper, and we leave it for future work. 

## 6 Conclusion 

In this paper, we identify a fundamental mismatch in hash-based sparse attention, where binarization discards feature magnitudes essential for Query–Key logits, causing false positives from directionally similar but low-logit keys and false negatives from high-logit keys with divergent directions. To address this, we propose Hierarchical Hash Retrieval (HHR), a coarse-to-fine framework combining Geometry-Aware Key 10 Routing (GKR) and Learned Hash Projection (LHP). GKR learns orthogonal transformations to redistribute magnitudes and derive tighter page-level logit bounds for early pruning of low-logit keys, while LHP learns a head-wise projection aligning Hamming distance with true relevance ranking to reduce false negatives. Together, they substantially improve the fidelity of hash-based sparse attention. Experiments across diverse LLMs and benchmarks confirm HHR’s effectiveness and efficiency. 11 Acknowledgments
